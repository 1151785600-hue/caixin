In 2018, precision-hardware maker Guangdong SACA Precision Manufacturing Co. Ltd. (300464.SZ) acquired Shenzhen Sunvalley Innovation Technology Co. Ltd., a leading Chinese e-commerce seller on Amazon, for 1.5 billion yuan ($225.3 million). Roughly three years later, a legal battle arose, and a Chinese court ruled that Sunvalley's former shareholders owed SACA Precision approximately 102 million yuan in provisional compensation and damages for failing to meet the deal's performance commitment (a valuation adjustment mechanism, or VAM).

On the Chinese mainland, VAMs are widely used in mergers and restructurings to protect listed companies and small investors. According to research by Liu Qiliang (a professor at Jiangxi University of Finance and Economics) and Wuyige CPA LLP, from 2014 to 2024, 73.4% of the 1,243 major restructuring deals involving listed companies as buyers included performance commitments. Although VAMs were made non-mandatory in the 2014 revision of restructuring rules, over 50% of deals still include them.

3.

[para.

5

]

Liu argues that performance commitments were intended to reduce valuation risks, promote fair pricing, and protect listed companies and retail investors. However, in practice, excessively high commitments often pair with steep acquisition prices and enormous goodwill, creating a hidden risk of large write-downs later.

4.

[para.

6

]

Many VAMs end in failure. A Caixin analysis of Wind data found that since 2020, nearly 700 listed companies disclosed over 840 major restructuring deals, with more than 410 involving a VAM. Of the roughly 390 deals that have completed their VAM periods, about 20% of targets failed to meet their performance commitments. The share meeting commitments tied to high valuations is even lower.

Disputes over compensation are widespread. Since 2024, more than 110 listed companies have disclosed lawsuits over performance compensation, with over 36 billion yuan at stake.

Winning in court does not guarantee payment. In December 2025, China's Supreme People's Court ruled that Chunghwa Picture Tubes (Bermuda) Ltd. must pay Hua Ying Technology (000536.SZ) 3 billion yuan in compensation, but by July (presumably 2026), Hua Ying had collected only 142 million yuan through court enforcement.

The case of Nanjing Kangni Mechanical & Electrical Co. Ltd. (603111.SH) illustrates severe consequences. In 2017, Kangni paid 3.4 billion yuan for Guangdong Longshine Technology, carrying a three-year VAM. After Longshine missed targets in 2018-2019, Kangni wrote down about 2.3 billion yuan of goodwill, suffered losses of nearly 3 billion yuan in total, and sold Longshine for 400 million yuan. Kangni found executive Liao Liangmao orchestrated fraud inflating Longshine's revenue by 900 million yuan from 2015-2017. Liao was sentenced to life imprisonment. Kangni recovered only 10.4 million yuan in cash and about 131.2 million shares. Additionally, the China Securities Regulatory Commission (CSRC) fined Kangni 300,000 yuan for negligence, and investor lawsuits led to roughly 182 million yuan in compensation payments, with more pending.

Beijing Tongtech Co. Ltd. faced a harsher fate. In 2018, it paid 600 million yuan for Beijing Testor, appraised at over 1,300% above book value, with a four-year VAM promising cumulative net profit of 227.2 million yuan. A 2025 CSRC investigation found Testor fabricated business and inflated revenue by about 432 million yuan and net profit by about 314 million yuan from 2019-2022. These false financials were used in a 2022 private share placement, deemed fraudulent securities issuance. Tongtech was forcibly delisted in January (presumably 2026), affecting tens of thousands of small investors, with final compensation amounts still pending court decisions.

Liu noted that the consequences of failed VAMs ultimately fall on listed companies and minority shareholders, including inability to collect compensation, post-commitment earnings drops, and regulatory punishment for target fraud. Lao Zhiming, former head of M&A at Huatai Securities, argued that VAMs, though well-intentioned, often backfire, creating a perverse effect where "bad money drives out good." Buyers without real M&A skills and sellers adept at packaging or deception are more likely to strike deals, while genuine industrial mergers get squeezed out. He wrote: "Whether performance VAMs are an antidote that regulates M&A or a poison that harms it is a question truly worthy of reflection."
