China Restructures Higher Education to Serve Labor Market Demand

Caixin Global | FULLTEXT | 729 words | 2026-09-23 10:50

1. China has launched a broad overhaul of its universities to end the hierarchy favoring elite research institutions and steer schools toward specialized roles matching labor-market and industrial needs.

]

A policy document issued by the Communist Party’s Central Committee and the State Council — titled *Opinions on Advancing University Reform by Category* — calls for universities to be classified by core mission (research, applied education, or skills training) and by whether they are comprehensive or specialized, replacing a one-size-fits-all model with differentiated development and evaluation.

]

The reform follows an unusually large-scale adjustment of university majors and is driven by demographic change, tighter resources, employment pressures, and industrial upgrading.

[para.

The Education Ministry has made the classification reform a major task for 2026; it is also included in China’s 2026 government work report and earlier plans such as the 2024-35 education-powerhouse blueprint.

[para.

2. The new framework aims to reduce waste caused by unclear positioning and the “blind pursuit” of higher status.

7

Many Chinese universities have tried to emulate broad-based, research-intensive institutions, competing over papers, national research grants, and doctoral-degree programs, leaving some schools ill-suited to their intended missions.

Under the new plan, research universities will train top innovative talent and achieve major scientific and technological breakthroughs, with the “Double First-Class” initiative leading reform.

Applied universities will focus on applied basic research, technological innovation, and commercialization, serving as talent sources for regional development; skills-oriented institutions, including vocational colleges, will prioritize training highly skilled workers and employment outcomes.

The policy also strengthens specialized applied undergraduate programs and high-level vocational schools, while asking industry-focused universities to build on traditional strengths.

4. Applied universities face some of the biggest obstacles. Their achievements are reflected in training practice-oriented graduates, partnering with industry, and serving local businesses, but they are commonly assessed against research-university measures such as leading journal publications, national-level projects, and doctoral programs.

[para.

19

They occupy an uneasy middle ground — innovation seen as less advanced than elite campuses and training viewed as less practical than vocational schools — causing gaps in policy support and resource allocation.

The new document addresses these concerns by calling for governance arrangements suited to different university categories, multi-track evaluation systems with multiple assessors, and incentives for resource allocation.

It also calls for improved mechanisms to match talent supply with demand, including adjusting academic disciplines and training models based on national strategic needs.
