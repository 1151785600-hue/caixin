‘Born to Fly’ documents Hong Kong-born Rahul Sharma’s world record 566km run across Sri Lanka

SCMP | FREE | 1333 words | 2026-09-05

In January 2025, Rahul Sharma ran his first marathon, in Mumbai, India. Months later, he learned of British ultra-endurance athlete William Goodge, who ran across Australia in 35 days, a journey of 3,800km (2,361 miles).

It was all the encouragement the then 24-year-old needed to up his game. Within weeks, the Melbourne-based exercise physiologist had rallied two of his best friends and decided on a goal: he would run 566km (352 miles) from Point Pedro, the northernmost point of Sri Lanka, to Dondra Head, at its southern tip, in six days.

None of the trio – Sharma, Ali Khajeian and Ben Lawson – knew the first thing about ultra-running. But that wasn’t going to stop them.

“I try my best not to be cocky, but it’s just not who I am,” Sharma admits in Born to Fly, a newly released documentary that charts his Sri Lanka run. “I have a big head.”

But why Sri Lanka?

“We didn’t want to follow a blueprint. We wanted to find a country that had never been done before,” says Sharma. Sri Lanka it was then, although, it is not strictly true that no one had attempted it previously; in 2023, Irishman Chris Rickard, assisted by two friends on bicycles, ran a slightly longer route between the same two points in Sri Lanka, in just under 12 days.

Born and raised in Hong Kong, Sharma played cricket from a young age, and represented the city’s under-19 team. He stepped away from the sport after a series of injuries.

Having moved to Australia, he started running to help in his recovery from the injuries, and the activity quickly became something more all-consuming.

Challenging himself to run the length of a country wasn’t just a vanity project, though. Driven by a childhood friend’s battle with leukaemia, Sharma aimed to raise A$20,000 (US$13,100) for the Children’s Cancer Institute (CCI), a Sydney-based research organisation.

Eventually, the dates were set: December 24 to 30, 2025.

Lawson, 25, a journalism major who lived with Sharma while the latter trained, liaised with sponsors, the media and the CCI. He also worked closely with Sharma’s coach to ensure that the runner was getting the optimal amount of nutrients, hydration and rest in between training sessions. Lawson came to know Sharma inside out.

But as the day neared, both Sharma’s friends, who had helped map out his entire journey, had to pull out of the actual trip to Sri Lanka for personal reasons.

“It was a hard conversation and, honestly, I didn’t think that Rahul would have that level of understanding,” says Khajeian, who, like Lawson, nevertheless stayed involved in the project, and would direct and produce Born to Fly. “I know internally he was crashing out. But on the outside, he was very understanding and very mature. It just brought us closer to each other.”

Sharma brought in another friend, Mohammed Abdullah, a Sri Lankan who happened to be in the country at the time.

“Rahul is like an older brother figure to me, so this project was important to me because it was important to him,” says Mohammed.

Lawson passed on elaborate spreadsheets to Mohammed for use in tracking Sharma’s heart rate, meals and water intake during his attempt. Meanwhile, Khajeian promised to stay awake in Melbourne, which is four-and-a-half hours ahead of Sri Lanka, to instruct Mohammed and the videographers on capturing the right shots for social media and the planned documentary.

“He’s very artistically gifted,” Sharma says of Khajeian, 23, who has a degree in finance and is behind several of the social-media reels made for Ebb&Flo, the Australian running club started by Sharma. “I always told him that it was my dream to buy him out of a corporate job because he’s just too gifted to be sitting at a desk.”

Finally in Sri Lanka, Sharma spent almost every waking minute of the six days of the attempt on his feet, stopping only to eat and sleep for three hours per night, initially, in a camper van with a malfunctioning air-conditioner. While he ran, his entourage, which included Mohammed, a physiotherapist, two cameramen and the driver, followed in the camper van.

With temperatures soaring, the conditions were brutal.

“I blacked out on the second day. I have no memory of it,” Sharma says. At one point, he tried to remove his socks only to discover the rubber grips had melted and fused to his skin. They had to be cut off with scissors.

Mohammed and Lawson, who were monitoring Sharma’s heart rate and pace, the latter remotely from Australia, suggested he finish in seven days instead of six.

“There was a lot of colourful language,” Sharma recalls. “I was just lashing out at the team, telling them it was not an option.”

Lawson, Khajeian and Mohammed understood their friend’s frustration.

“One of the things we have become very good at as a group is separating our relationship as colleagues from our relationship as friends,” says Lawson. “So I knew that Rahul’s anger at me wasn’t a personal attack on me.”

From day three, Sharma slept during the day and ran through the night and early morning, to beat the heat. But the challenges kept coming.

An old injury returned in his left knee, becoming “the worst physical pain” he ever felt, he says, akin to two knives stabbing into his Achilles tendon with every step. Painkillers could only do so much, so Sharma and his physiotherapist fell into a different rhythm: every five minutes, he would stop, get restrapped and sprayed down to dull the pain, then continue to run.

“My physio had sleepless nights. My whole team was working so hard for me to be able to run,” Sharma says. Mohammed even blacked out from the stress at one point on the final day. “It would’ve been selfish of me to stop or slow down.”

Before his sixth and final day, Sharma was told by his father that several priests were praying for him. “Son, tomorrow you will fly,” he told Sharma over the phone, words that would inspire the title of Khajeian’s documentary. Spiritually revived, Sharma’s pace picked up on that last day.

Born to Fly’s most affecting moment is when Sharma arrives at Dondra Head. He collapses, bursting into tears as loved ones gather around him.

“It wasn’t just the physical pain,” he explains. “I put my team through hell. I didn’t see my friends, but they still came to support me. I hadn’t seen my dad in nine months. We raised money for cancer. We did all of this, and for it to just finish in six days …”

Six days, 13 hours and 22 minutes, to be exact – a time that earned Sharma recognition by the World Book of Records as the “fastest man to run from northernmost point to southernmost point of Sri Lanka”, all 566km of it. He also succeeded in raising the A$20,000 for the CCI.

Khajeian, who says it took him more than a month to put the two-hour documentary together, has been especially inspired by Sharma’s feat: “It has made me realise that the only thing standing between me and my goals is myself.”

Says Sharma, “I don’t know how fulfilled I would be if I was still playing cricket. I never got that feeling of, ‘This is what I was born to do.’ With running, everything just feels right.”

His next goal is to tackle a 1,000km route in Nepal. “I’ve done one world record at 24. It’ll be two at 25, if I’m eyeing to be the greatest of all time.”

Eventually, though, “I want to build a brand or open clinics through which I can help the community”, says Sharma.

“If I can choose to have a world record within eight months, you can choose to do the same in any walk of life.”
