Signs of Prabowo-Widodo rift grow as Indonesian lawmakers target VP Gibran’s credentials

SCMP | FREE | 1048 words | 2026-11-07

A growing push among Indonesian lawmakers to investigate Vice-President Gibran Rakabuming Raka’s academic credentials is raising questions about the influence of his father, former leader Joko Widodo, as signs of a rift within the country’s ruling elite grow.

The challenge comes from within President Prabowo Subianto’s own coalition, underscoring how the political alliance that helped propel him to power in 2024 may be unravelling, analysts have said.

It also follows the arrest of a prominent Widodo ally in a widening wiretapping scandal and an unusually large military mobilisation in Jakarta, developments that have fuelled speculation about the former president’s waning influence.

Political factions in the House of Representatives (DPR) have begun discussing the use of parliamentary oversight powers following an October 30 Supreme Court ruling that Gibran’s high school equivalency certificate was “invalid”.

Gibran, Widodo’s eldest son, became Prabowo’s running mate in the 2024 presidential election after Widodo backed the former general’s successful bid for power.

Indonesia requires vice-presidential candidates to have completed at least senior high school education.

According to data from the General Elections Commission, Gibran attended Orchid Park Secondary School in Singapore from 2002 to 2004, followed by three years at the University of Technology Sydney (UTS) Insearch from 2004 to 2007.

The Constitutional Court ruled last month that it had not found “convincing evidence” that Gibran “possesses a diploma … from abroad that could serve as the basis for determining that he had completed at least senior high school”.

The court nevertheless dismissed the petition challenging his qualifications, while the Supreme Court ruling on his equivalency certificate did not automatically remove him from office.

The two rulings have prompted lawmakers to consider invoking the right of inquiry, the right of interpellation or the right to express an opinion – parliamentary mechanisms that allow legislators to investigate government actions, question officials or formally state their position on a matter.

“The Prosperous Justice Party [PKS] has received public input from civil society several times at the Central Executive Board office. PKS is committed to pursuing these aspirations democratically and constitutionally, using the rights afforded by the DPR,” Muhammad Kholid, a lawmaker and senior PKS official, told This Week in Asia.

PKS is one of seven parliamentary parties in Prabowo’s broad ruling coalition, which controls 81 per cent of the seats in the legislature.

“We are currently reviewing the options. We are also communicating with other parties. Clearly, DPR must follow up [on these] rulings, especially the Supreme Court’s, within its authority,” Muhammad said.

Habiburokhman, deputy chairman of Prabowo’s Gerindra party, said it would discuss the Supreme Court’s ruling after the parliamentary recess ended on November 10.

Another coalition member, the National Awakening Party, said in a statement on Monday that it was “seriously reviewing” the use of the right of inquiry in Gibran’s case.

While such an inquiry could intensify political pressure on Gibran, removing a vice-president would require a separate constitutional process involving the Constitutional Court and the People’s Consultative Assembly.

Gibran’s lawyer, Muhammad Rullyandi, stressed that the Supreme Court decision did not affect his client’s position as vice-president.

Widodo said on Thursday he would “respect all existing legal processes and also respect the constitutional rights” of parliament.

The former president is himself embroiled in a lengthy legal dispute over his degree from Gadjah Mada University, which opponents claim was fabricated. Widodo has denied the allegation.

Troop deployment

The proposed investigation into Gibran has emerged against a backdrop of political and security tensions following the arrest of three senior police generals – including Widodo loyalist and former presidential chief of staff Listyo Sigit Prabowo – over allegations of wiretapping the president, cabinet officials, journalists and civic leaders.

The presidential palace has said the alleged surveillance could amount to treason.

Soon after the arrest, Indonesia’s armed forces (TNI) deployed troops and personnel to Jakarta, including nearly 21,000 Navy members.

First Admiral Tunggul, a Navy spokesman, said in a statement on Wednesday that the move was to “maintain the security and stability” of Indonesia.

Muhammad Nas, a TNI spokesman, called the deployment “common practice because TNI is required to always be ready to protect the entire nation”. Both spokesmen did not elaborate on what threats Indonesia is facing.

‘Fatal strategic decision’

The latest controversies have raised questions about whether a deeper rift is emerging between Prabowo and Widodo as the latter’s political influence wanes.

Made Supriatma, a visiting fellow at the ISEAS – Yusof Ishak Institute in Singapore, said Widodo made a “fatal strategic decision” when he aligned himself and his family with Prabowo ahead of the 2024 presidential election.

“The commitment at the time was that Prabowo would provide protection for Jokowi and his family,” Made said, referring to Widodo’s nickname. “Whether that commitment is still being upheld is unknown, but we can judge from the [fact] that Prabowo is allowing the [Gibran high school diploma] case to continue.”

According to Cecep Hidayat, a political science lecturer at the University of Indonesia, Widodo retains “non-institutional electoral capital”, including his supporter networks and his association with the non-parliamentary Indonesian Solidarity Party – headed by Kaesang Pangarep, his youngest son.

“Jokowi’s political influence is declining, because when you’re outside the government, that power will gradually fade,” Cecep said.

Still, he warned that Prabowo’s “political legitimacy” could be affected if invoking the right of inquiry led to Gibran’s impeachment.

“Gibran isn’t just a separately elected vice-president, he’s part of an elite compromise. Will Prabowo also be held politically accountable for Gibran’s nomination?” Cecep said.

Gibran’s dismissal could also lead the public to question “the legitimacy of the government elected in the 2024 presidential election”, he said.

Made suggested that the proposal for a parliamentary inquiry into Gibran’s diploma was a tool for political posturing within the ruling coalition, and direct impeachment remained “unlikely” to avoid “infighting” over his replacement.

“Currently, the parties in Prabowo’s coalition are manoeuvring, throwing the ball to each other and doing political bargaining to gauge what advantages they can maximally gain [from the case],” Made said.

“We are the biggest victims of all of this. This political game doesn’t solve anything. It doesn’t help us get out of the crisis, it actually makes it worse.”
