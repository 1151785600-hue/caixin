Nancy Kwan’s enduring legacy, six decades after the release of The World of Suzie Wong – interview

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2264 words | 2026-09-04

To many, Nancy Kwan Ka-shen is the actress who rose to international stardom for her role in the 1960 film The World of Suzie Wong, based on the book of the same name, about a sex worker in Wan Chai who meets a wealthy American (played by William Holden). Kwan’s story, however, is far richer than any single role.

From breaking barriers for Asian actors in Hollywood and standing her ground in an industry that was rigid and often prejudiced, to founding her own ballet studio, producing films and television advertisements across Southeast Asia, and starring in more than 50 films spanning decades, she has lived a life of relentless creativity, resilience and grace.

Now 87, Kwan’s path to celebrity was as serendipitous as it was extraordinary. In August 1959, she stumbled upon a casting call for The World of Suzie Wong.

“I thought it was dreadful because all I did was giggle,” she tells me over a FaceTime call from her home in Los Angeles, in the United States. “I was really quite silly. I was surprised I got a job at all. But they seemed to like it, so I ended up in Hollywood doing more screen tests. Eventually, I got the role.”

Initially, France Nuyen had been cast, but when the French-American actress withdrew, Kwan’s ballet-trained poise and expressive presence made her the perfect replacement. The film catapulted her to global fame, subtly transforming Hollywood’s perception of Asian women at a time when “yellowface” – casting non-Asian actors in make-up to portray Asian characters – was still widespread.

Even decades later, her magnetism remains palpable. Behind her, a poster of Suzie Wong seems almost to lean towards the camera, a reminder that the character and the woman who portrayed her remain inseparable in the public memory. Yet Kwan’s memoir, last year’s The World of Nancy Kwan, reveals a keen awareness of her own agency, her understanding that her story is as much about heritage and personal resilience as it is about fame.

Born in 1939 to Kwan Wing-hong, a prominent, Cambridge-educated Chinese architect, and Marquita Scott, a British model and aspiring actress who would leave the family before the start of World War II but later reconnect with her daughter in the US, Nancy grew up in a family whose legacy was intertwined with Hong Kong’s development and global affairs.

Her great-grandfather, Kwan Yuen-cheung, was the city’s first Western-trained dentist; her great-grandmother, Lai Amui, survived the mainland’s Taiping rebellion (1850-64) to become one of the first Western-trained nurses in Hong Kong. Her great-uncle Kwan King-leung studied alongside Dr Sun Yat-sen and later served as physician to the Empress Dowager of the Qing dynasty (1644-1911). “I don’t know if great-uncle Kwan King-leung’s friendship with Dr Sun Yat-sen endured, but I think it is extraordinary that the man who is considered the father of modern China spent his formative years in the Kwan household,” Kwan writes in her memoir.

Kwan also recalls how, although she has only hazy memories of it, her father (who later remarried) had to hide her and her brother in wicker baskets to travel to Macau by boat and then onwards to mainland China to flee the war after Japan’s attack on Hong Kong in 1941 – an event remembered locally as “Black Christmas”.

Later, Kwan learned that her father had served covertly with the British Army Aid Group, assisting soldiers stranded behind enemy lines in their escape from Japanese forces. Some media have even described him as a “Chinese James Bond”. The family returned to Hong Kong in 1945, resuming their lives amid the city’s post-war recovery.

Hollywood was never Kwan’s first passion. Ballet had captivated her from childhood, and she trained in Britain, attending the prestigious Royal Ballet School, in London.

“I’ve always continued with ballet,” she says. “Then I branched out – jazz and other kinds of dance. Nowadays, I take tai chi.”

That disciplined movement would define her screen presence, most memorably in Flower Drum Song (1961).

“Hermes Pan, the choreographer, told us, ‘Don’t dance like this; this is jazz.’ We did jazz because that’s what the piece called for. It was all about keeping ballet at bay,” she recalls, a glimmer of mischief in her eyes.

At the time of filming, Kwan had no idea how historic the movie would become.

“I just thought, ‘Oh, Flower Drum Song, all great.’ But it was the first time a major Hollywood studio made a film with a mostly Asian cast – that was a real achievement. I didn’t know the business that well back then,” she admits. “It wasn’t until my friend [the late Chinese-American actor] Benson Fong told me, ‘Nancy, this is the first time they’re making a film with an all-Asian cast,’ that I thought, ‘Wow, OK, this is bigger than I realised.’ It was very exciting.”

Decades later, that trail-blazing spirit echoed in the meteoric success of Crazy Rich Asians, the 2018 film adapted from Kevin Kwan’s 2013 novel, starring a predominantly Asian cast and capturing the global imagination. The connection is more than thematic: Kevin is Nancy’s cousin.

“Nancy really paved the way for me to pursue my own creativity,” Kevin, 53, tells me over the phone. “The first time I saw her in Flower Drum Song, I could hardly believe my eyes – just this glamorous creature in my own family. It was always inspiring.”

He fondly remembers the rare glimpses of authentic Hong Kong and the nuanced female characters Nancy brought to the screen. He recalls her performance in the late-1980s miniseries Noble House, adapted from the James Clavell novel, that was shot in Hong Kong, which also starred Pierce Brosnan and Deborah Raffin, as it offered him an immersive look at his heritage.

“It was remarkable to see her playing a sassy, empowered secretary, and years later, seeing her in Dragon: The Bruce Lee Story [1993] as Lee’s boss was another milestone,” he says. “She was there at every significant stage, paving the way for younger talent, for stories like my own.”

Kevin attributes her success to her perseverance.

“She never gave up, no matter the hardships or controversies. She was the first woman to return to Hong Kong and run her own film studio, and she got through everything with determination. Even now, in her 80s, she carries herself with the energy and poise of someone decades younger – not just physically, but in spirit,” he says.

Nancy navigated Hollywood’s entrenched racial attitudes, which often reduced Asian actors to exoticised caricatures. She admired Anna May Wong – considered to be the first Chinese-American film star in Hollywood, in the 20s – and her ability to get through the barriers she faced.

“I didn’t know much about her until I came to Hollywood and found out she was really the first Asian actress from Los Angeles,” Nancy recalls. “I think I met her niece – she comes to a lot of my screenings and book signings – a very lovely girl. I read up on Anna May Wong and even did a documentary about her, doing the voice-over, so I learned a lot about the struggles she faced, especially when they didn’t use Asian actors – they used Caucasians dressed up as Asians, like Luise Rainer in The Good Earth [1937]. She was trying to work during that era, and it was very difficult. When we did Flower Drum Song, the producer told me [Wong] was going to have a role, and I was so excited to work with her. Sadly, she passed away just a few weeks before filming.”

Private life and career are intertwined in Kwan’s reflections. Decades spent in Los Angeles, a brief return to Hong Kong to manage her father’s company and the routines of long-term partnership and care illustrate a life shaped by both focus and balance.

“I came back when my father was ill and, sadly, he passed away,” she recalls. “After that, I was offered the chance to run a company in Hong Kong. I thought, ‘Why not?’ But I quickly realised it wasn’t for me. Being a CEO meant more lunches and meetings than actual work – I wanted to be on the ground, doing things myself. By then, my son was ready to start high school, so that’s why I moved back to Los Angeles.”

Her memoir is filled with vivid, intimate stories of Hollywood encounters. She recounts her then-boyfriend Maximilian Schell, in a fit of jealous rage, hurling his “Best Actor” Oscar at her, and the time she turned down the lead opposite Elvis Presley in Blue Hawaii (1961).

“I was asked to meet someone at Paramount Studios about a film,” she recalls. “I didn’t know it was Elvis. I walk onto this empty stage, and then suddenly, he appears with a few of his friends. He sat me down and sang the ‘Hawaiian Wedding Song’ – I was floored. He was such an entertainer. Of course, I applauded and made a fool of myself.”

Despite the allure, Kwan politely declined. “I told them I’d already signed for Flower Drum Song. Both roles were historic in their own way, but I felt committed to this one,” she says.

She fondly remembers meeting Ursula Andress, who casually mentioned she was about to star in a “B” movie called “007” with Sean Connery, and her own friendship with Bruce Lee, whom she first met in Hong Kong and grew close to while filming The Wrecking Crew (1968), for which he served as martial-arts adviser.

“He was going to teach Sharon [Tate] and me some kung fu for a fight scene. That’s how I got to know Bruce quite well. We would hang out at the house, and he often told me about his plans to return to Hong Kong and make martial-arts films,” she remembers.

That now-famous fight scene between Tate and Kwan is immortalised in Quentin Tarantino’s 2019 film, Once Upon a Time … in Hollywood, in which Margot Robbie, playing Tate, is shown watching that exact scene in a cinema. In her memoir, Kwan also reflects on Tate’s kind nature and tragic death, revealing the personal impact it had on her.

The memoir confronts other heartbreaks. Kwan opens up about the loss of her son and “best friend”, Bernie Pock, whom she shared with her first husband, Peter Pock.

Born in Austria and raised in Hong Kong, Bernie was fluent in Cantonese, adored and mastered martial arts and was described as wise beyond his years. Known for his kindness – he once sheltered a homeless boy – Bernie was a stunt performer in Los Angeles when he was diagnosed with Aids, contracted from a woman he loved and later married, before he died, in 1996, aged 33.

Even in the shadow of loss, Bernie refused to let their story end in tragedy. Together with Kwan and her new husband, Vienna-born actor and producer Norbert Meisel, now aged 96, the family completed a final project: Rebellious (also known as The Biker Poet), a 1995 film about a biker discovering himself, a testament to Bernie’s creativity and enduring spirit.

In the years since, Kwan has devoted herself to Aids awareness advocacy, channelling her grief into purpose. She also wrote Celebration of a Life: Memories of My Son (1997), a memoir that honours Bernie and preserves his memory.

Arthur Dong, the Oscar-nominated filmmaker and American Book Award-winning author of Forbidden City, USA (2014), has interviewed Kwan on many occasions over the past 20 years. He recalls the magnetic presence she brought to every set.

“Nancy candidly confronts the xenophobic and sexist attitudes that once branded her a ‘real-life China doll’, despite the pivotal influence of producer and mentor Ray Stark, who promoted her as a ‘girl of many nations’.”

Kwan became a symbol of possibility for actors navigating Hollywood’s rigid and often Eurocentric standards.

“I am from the East and West. My father is Chinese, my mother is English, and bringing that together … I feel very proud. The world is getting smaller, we recognise each other more and I wish I had even more cultural roots to embrace. It’s a wonderful thing,” she reflects.

“Nancy was among the most beautifully photographed women of the 60s,” notes Dong.

“That she was biracial made her worldwide popularity even more historic, particularly in the image- and race-conscious world of entertainment.”

Her pragmatism has always stood out to him. “In 2004, when I asked her to sum up the roller-coaster trajectory of her career, she replied, ‘What’s fair in life? There’s no fairness. As an actor, you have to keep that in mind; otherwise, you might as well not be in this business. Nothing lasts forever. Times change.’”

Even in her mid-80s, Kwan retains vitality and joy. Asked what she does to relax, she replies, “Probably tai chi, meditation […] that is the way I am.”

Her energy and elegance are palpable. “I’ve been in the business a long time now. I made my mark in the 60s, and now it’s [the mid-2020s]. It’s been a long journey, and I’ve met so many people along the way. Many Asian actors I know are still working, thank goodness, and television has opened up opportunities for them.”

Any advice for budding artists? “Just work hard,” she says. “Do what you can, steadily. Go out there. The film business is changing, too.”
