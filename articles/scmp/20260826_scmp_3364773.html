How to be a good tourist; a guide for the modern traveller

SCMP | FREE | 1172 words | 2026-08-26

From Tenerife to Thessaloniki, demonstrators’ chants and roadside graffiti alike say, “Tourists go home”, although sometimes in rather stronger language.

Around the world, the residents of “must-see” destinations can no longer see each other for the crowds, and increasingly view tourists less as benefactors and more as a bane. Local authorities are responding ever more robustly to their complaints.

To drink alcohol in certain Amsterdam streets now attracts a fine of €95 (US$110), while in the village of Varenna, on Italy’s Lake Como, it’s twice as much for wearing swimwear away from the beach.

Not far away, in seaside Portofino, the introduction of no-loitering zones has effectively put an end to selfies, unless you’re a professional narcissist and the income to be expected from sponsors or followers is greater than the potential €275 (US$317) penalty for blocking traffic.

However, much of the globally growing ill will is not about bad behaviour but merely about numbers.

Destinations that have seen many long-term rental properties turned into more profitable short-term accommodation for tourists are introducing strict regulations (British Columbia) or banning Airbnb and its ilk altogether (Barcelona).

Local satisfaction is crucial to success, but if the visited are unhappy, so are the visitors.

Tourists discover soaring head taxes, 90-minute queues and compulsory advance-purchase tickets with a timed entry, and that the quiet stroll between two pretty mountain villages they have come back to enjoy for a second time now has an entrance fee and one-way-only traffic, and yet is still a conga line.

Individual residents, municipal authorities and national tourism bureaus alike declare that they don’t dislike tourists, and don’t want to discourage them, but they do want to choose the visitors they get.

An official online Tokyo travel guide, How to stay safe and be respectful in Tokyo at night, has little to say about safety in what anyway ranks as one of the world’s least dangerous cities. Instead, it explains how not to offend locals by littering, or by drinking or smoking in public.

But if the Japanese are too polite to say straight out, “Don’t come unless you obey the rules”, José Antonio Donaire, Barcelona’s commissioner for sustainable tourism, feels no such restraint. He wants to restrict visitors to no more than the 15.7 million the city hosted in 2025, and he wants half of those to be cultural tourists – presumably people who swoon at the sight of Joan Miro’s surrealist art or Antoni Gaudi’s soaring Sagrada Família rather than their 15th Estrella Damm lager of the day.

In terms of accommodation capacity, all tourists may be equal, but some are more equal than others. These regulations, fines and fees suggest a definition of good tourists: it’s those who stay longer, spend more with local businesses, respect the needs of residents, do not congregate all in one spot and neither dive into historic fountains (€500/US$576 fine in Rome) nor indulge in drunken caterwauling at 3am (€140/US$161 in Amsterdam).

However, concentrating on attracting good tourists will still not solve the problem of volume, and some destinations are looking to spread the load.

Japan has offered some international visitors free domestic flights to destinations beyond the well-worn Tokyo-Kyoto-Osaka trail that two-thirds of them take.

Spain’s new policy is to encourage visitors to avoid the summer peak, and to look beyond the supply of sun, sea, sand and potential sexual partners along its crowded Mediterranean beaches towards the equally sun-baked sleepy villages of the interior. These are all sugar-cube houses, crumbling castles and excellent walking among hills hatched with olives and vines, where good tourists eat local dishes, rather than fish and chips or pizza.

All these schemes ignore the jumbo jet in the room, though. If we don’t want a future in which the world’s major cities are to be toured by boat rather than by hop-on, hop-off bus, there must be a reduction in the emissions that make such catastrophes ever more likely.

One per cent of fliers produce 50 per cent of airline emissions, so the rest typically urge the elite to cut back first. But the good tourist understands that simply to board a plane is to be a member of an elite. Eighty per cent of the world’s population never takes to the air, so perhaps halving your own flying is not an unreasonable idea. This would reduce emissions, although the only true ecotourism is just staying at home.

In China and Europe, extensive rail systems offer less damaging ground-level travel as an easy substitute. Or there’s the option to halve the number of trips but stay for twice as long. The good tourist leaves the rentals to the locals who need the space and opts for a hotel instead.

Good tourists also avoid getting bogged down in the slough of meme-driven, artificial-intelligence-derived ignorance that is most online travel commentary. That leads to the taking of well-trodden paths between sights ranging from those of genuine cultural and historical significance to those merely of social-media-driven popularity, where the idea of being there, and being seen to be there, is more important than the actual experience.

Today, social media merely ensures that a location may go from overlooked to overwhelmed in the blink of a smartphone’s camera shutter, the image repeated in increasingly dishonest “I was here, too” postings.

The hydroelectric project next to the cliffside temple is cropped out, the sky is edited into an improbable blue and the camera angle narrowed to exclude the hordes of others taking the near-identical shot. The resulting image is of perfect beauty in tranquil solitude while the reality is anything but. And the blame cannot be laid solely at the perfectly pedicured feet of influencers. Every step you take, every post you make, people are watching you.

The good tourist travels flexibly, and keeps quiet about new discoveries. But if you follow the crowd, then it’s unsurprising that crowds are what you find.

As the late Irish travel writer Dervla Murphy told The Guardian newspaper in 2009, “Choose your country, use guidebooks to identify the areas most frequented by foreigners – and then go in the opposite direction.”

Of course, if significant numbers go in the opposite direction, there will no longer be an opposite direction, although when there are no longer “hidden gems” to be found “off the beaten track”, this will at least result in a merciful release from cliché.

There was a time when travel writing was not all how-to guides, or thinly disguised advertising, but written by a tiny number of people who ventured abroad to be a substitute for those who couldn’t.

It’s time to lead the way by sometimes going nowhere, perhaps instead sitting down with a well-informed account from the past of somewhere you might go in the future. Or might not.

Travel less, but travel better.
