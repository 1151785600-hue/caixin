Six legendary libraries, where architecture and academia meet

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1682 words | 2026-10-29

Libraries are hard drives containing the memory of the world in an ancient form. Less pocketable than anything digital, but in many cases far prettier, these buildings are also sometimes works of art in their own right and cathedrals of humanism, whether erected four centuries ago or by a still-living architect of global acclaim.

Their beauty is inessential to their function but celebrates the knowledge they contain, the power and wealth or taste and intellect of those who commissioned them, or plays a role in preserving the essence of cultural identity.

You might visit one to see the masterful carpentry of ornate shelving, elaborate spiral staircases and walkways, stained glass, trompe l’oeil frescoes, carved marble and art, in addition to displays of rare and ancient books, maps, treaties, manuscripts and other historic documents. To the atmospheric silence and the smell of wax polish found in churches is added the warm aroma of ancient leather bindings.

You might enter for some relief from the haste, heat and hustle of a big city, or because the libraries themselves have become popular meeting places, with cafes and places to sit.

Here are six institutions whose collections or buildings should not be overlooked, whatever reason you have for being in town.

The Real Biblioteca del Monasterio de San Lorenzo de El Escorial, Madrid, Spain

Philip II of Spain (1527-98) ruled one of the greatest empires in human history and did so much of the time from El Escorial, creating here the national library of the Kingdom of Spain in a room intended to be one of the most magnificent in the palace, and a rival for the richest libraries elsewhere in Europe, particularly cultural competitor Italy.

The main royal collections were transferred to the new building in 1566, even before the rest of the complex was ready for Philip to move in, although his advisers regretted that such magnificence was created in a remote spot, rather than in one of the university cities of the time.

Today, its improbable out-of-town location is part of the attraction, just a short local-train journey northwest from Madrid, followed by a stroll through pretty countryside that’s a pleasant relief from the capital’s intensity. The palace is ever more imposing as approached, and the heavily frescoed ceiling of its library, by a Milanese master referencing Michelangelo’s Sistine Chapel, is worth the trip in itself, even without taking into account what’s beneath it.

This includes ornate shelving and displays of ancient maps, coins, medals and scientific instruments. Philip’s giant armillary sphere, like a skeletal globe and representing the great circles of the heavens, has stood in the same spot here since 1593, when it was still thought that the sun and the rest of the heavens revolved around the Earth.

And there’s still the great church and the labyrinth of palace chambers, with their tapestries, art and furniture to see, before a reflective journey back to Madrid.

Veneranda Biblioteca Ambrosiana, Milan, Italy

Italy’s fashion capital is home to at least five historic libraries, of which the 18th century Biblioteca Nazionale Braidense is perhaps the most beautiful. But in terms of its collection, the Ambrosiana is the greatest of all, with more than a million volumes, including thousands of incunabula: printed books from before 1501, which are now very rare.

It was also one of the first in the world to open its doors to anyone who could read or write, and its users included mathematician, inventor and astronomer Galileo Galilei (1564-1642) – not your everyday influencer – who called it “heroic and immortal”. He may turn out to be right; opened in 1609, it has lasted for more than four centuries so far.

The hush of its long red-carpeted main room, with an ornate arched ceiling and dark bookcases in two tiers, is home to the world’s greatest collection of drawings and writings by Leonardo da Vinci (1452-1519), much of it on open display. Here, the polymath painter of The Last Supper (1495-98) and the Mona Lisa (1503), who was also an architect and engineer, can rather thrillingly be seen thinking out loud about geometry, anatomy, physics and assorted inventions, with many detailed illustrations in his own hand.

The Ambrosiana complex also incorporates an art gallery with masterpieces by Caravaggio, Botticelli, Raphael and many others, spanning four centuries.

You’ll need at least half a day here.

The Real Gabinete Português de Leitura, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil

While beautiful ancient libraries are most numerous in Europe, the 16th century voyagers to the New World took their ideas with them, and Rio’s Real Gabinete was founded in 1880 as a memorial to modern Brazil’s Portuguese roots, and to that country’s era of maritime dominance, by then already a fading memory.

While the library’s calm and cool interior is a welcome break from the swimwear parades of Rio’s beaches, there’s vibrant colour and decorative detail enough almost to match the city’s famous carnival. The eye scarcely knows where to rest, and many visiting photographers seem unsure where to point their lenses first.

Spiralling wooden pillars with gilded bosses support three galleried tiers of shelves. The heavily ornamented Manueline style of Portuguese cathedrals from 350 years earlier has been adapted to new purpose, but the interior is colourfully illuminated by a stained-glass dome in a cast-iron frame that was hi-tech for its day.

This is certainly memory in built form, harking back to past cultural ascendancy. The facade is a copy of a Lisbon monastery – carved limestone panels were created in Portugal and shipped out – and the library’s most prized possession is a first edition of poet Luís de Camões’ epic Os Lusíadas of 1572, widely considered Portuguese literature’s greatest work.

The library’s luxurious pseudo-antiquity is an almost shocking discovery in a humdrum Rio backstreet, although it’s far from merely a monument. The Real Gabinete still receives copies of every book published in Portugal, and so holds the largest overseas collection of Portuguese works.

The Morgan Library and Museum, New York, United States

Modern Madison Avenue may hardly seem the place for quiet contemplation, but here in 1906, the fabulously wealthy banker and industrialist J. Pierpont (JP) Morgan built himself a library that’s now part of a modern complex by “starchitect” Renzo Piano (he was behind the modern glass atrium and expansion completed in 2006), who also designed The New York Times Building (opened in 2007) and the Whitney Museum (current location opened in 2015).

But entering the Morgan Library makes you feel as though you’ve stepped through a time portal, as the building is in a style that was popular in the early 20th century, called American Renaissance, which harks back to the Europe of three centuries earlier. There are triple tiers of walnut shelving beneath a coffered ceiling copied from a Roman villa, murals of literary figures and walls hung with tapestries, which, with an ornate fireplace, manage to give it an air of domesticity.

The main draw, though, is Morgan’s acquisitions, which include the original manuscript of Charles Dickens’ A Christmas Carol (1843), the original score of a Mozart symphony, the only surviving complete draft of a Jane Austen novel (the early Lady Susan of 1794) and original prints by English poet, painter and printmaker William Blake (1757-1827).

It’s all highly restorative before a return to New York’s bedlam.

The Qatar National Library, Doha, Qatar

Although we’re now in the era of the e-reader and the on-demand download of just about anything, the age of great library-building is not over.

Doha’s Rem Koolhaas-designed building of 2018, with its 45,000 square metres of floor space, is supposed to resemble two folded pieces of paper, emphasising the importance of the written word in Qatari heritage. But on first impressions, it looks like a UFO about to launch itself into the sky.

The library’s vast and airy interior, well supplied with spaces in which to sit and read, is a haven against Doha’s often scorching heat. It’s more like a town square, and is a popular place to meet, with access to 3D printers and other tech as well as more than a million volumes in 16 languages available for loan. The interior is so vast that it looks ready to hold hundreds of millions more.

Only the basement recalls ancient European libraries, its warren of smaller spaces containing a magnificent display of Arabic calligraphy and illuminated manuscripts, very early maps of Arabia and accounts of both European exploration and the French invasion of Egypt, in 1798.

Yururi Yusuhara, Yusuhara, Japan

Libraries can be great without being grand or intimidating, and even the biggest architectural stars sometimes involve themselves in small projects.

Yusuhara, a sleepy community of 3,300, high up in the central mountains of the Japanese island of Shikoku, well away from the hordes tramping the famous 88-temple pilgrimage around the coastline, has almost become a second home to Kengo Kuma.

The architect is known for his large-scale projects, such as the Japan National Stadium, in Tokyo, but at Yusuhara, he’s indulged himself with local materials and a desire to develop traditional Japanese styles and techniques to produce something fresh. Amid typical Japanese domesticity, he’s built six wooden buildings, including a corridor bridge, a hotel that faintly resembles a haystack and an extraordinary library.

To enter from the wooded hills of the surrounding countryside is to seem to arrive at a forest rather than leave one. From a smooth cedar-wood floor (shoes must be removed, of course), pillars fork as they rise and end in a tangle of criss-crossing branch-like structures that descend again from the ceiling.

At floor level, the building is constructed of shelving that doubles as seating and as stairs forming multiple levels that also seat audiences for public events.

This isn’t a library in which to escape the pressures of the outside world, but a fully functional modern community service – complete with more than 70,000 volumes – unlike any other you may have visited.
