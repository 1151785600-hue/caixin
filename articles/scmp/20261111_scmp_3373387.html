Is Lula’s upset win also a victory for Chinese firms in Brazil?

SCMP | FREE | 1482 words | 2026-11-11

With domestic profits narrowing and production capacity expanding, China’s firms are continuing to widen their overseas footprints in search of new, more lucrative markets. In this series, we examine China Inc.’s next phase of “going global” and the complex, challenging international environment its companies have chosen to enter.

A presidential election in Brazil, a country thousands of miles away, might seem like just another headline to many in China. But for Chinese companies doing business in South America, the stakes are considerably higher.

When right-wing candidate Flavio Bolsonaro took the lead in the first round of voting in early October, Ken Liu, a Chinese exporter of digital textile printing equipment operating in Brazil, grew concerned about the implications for his business.

Brazil introduced a visa exemption for Chinese passport holders in May, allowing stays of up to 30 days for purposes including tourism and business.

With Bolsonaro seen as less aligned with China than incumbent president Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva, Liu took a cautious approach, arranging for some employees to apply for work visas in case the policy changed. He was planning to send more staff to expand the company’s sales in the Brazilian market.

Lula’s upset victory in the run-off in late October was therefore welcome news for Liu.

“Lula will generally be more friendly towards Chinese companies than Bolsonaro,” he said. Bolsonaro has advocated closer ties with Washington.

As economic ties between Beijing and Brasilia deepen, Brazil has become an increasingly important destination for Chinese investors. Lula’s re-election offers the prospect of policy continuity and closer bilateral ties, but it is not an unqualified win for Chinese companies.

Lula is, above all, a pragmatic politician who understands well Brazil’s limitations when it comes to expanding its footprint in key industries ... in which China is a leader

Victor Gonzalez, PC Woo & Zhonglun Wende

His push for foreign investors to manufacture locally and help develop Brazilian industries could raise costs. Bolsonaro’s narrow loss in the run-off also showed that Brazil remains politically divided, adding another layer of uncertainty for Chinese companies operating in Latin America’s largest economy, businesses and analysts said.

“Lula is, above all, a pragmatic politician who understands well Brazil’s limitations when it comes to expanding its footprint in key industries such as electric batteries, chips and mobile phones, in which China is a leader,” said Victor Gonzalez, a senior adviser for China-Latin America legal affairs at Shenzhen-based law firm PC Woo & Zhonglun Wende.

Given the commercial sensitivities surrounding the sharing of Chinese companies’ valuable know-how – even among close partners – Lula has pursued a strategy of reciprocity, Gonzalez said.

“Lula’s most valuable attribute has been that of having offered quid pro quos as a way to potentially receive concessions, too.”

Since returning to office in 2023, Lula has made two state visits to China, seeking to attract investment and gain access to Chinese expertise in advanced technology, infrastructure and manufacturing.

He is also focused on rebuilding ties strained under his predecessor, Jair Bolsonaro, 71, the father of his presidential rival in this year’s election who was sentenced to prison in 2025 for plotting a coup to remain in power.

During his April 2023 visit, Lula secured 32 business agreements covering areas such as the digital economy and renewable energy, according to the Brazilian government. His May 2025 visit yielded a further 27 billion reais (US$4.96 billion) in investment pledges from Chinese companies.

As bilateral ties have strengthened, China has maintained its position as Brazil’s largest trading partner while increasing its investment in Latin America’s largest economy.

Chinese investment in Brazil rose 45 per cent year on year to US$6.1 billion in 2025, making Brazil the world’s largest destination for Chinese investment, according to the Brazil-China Business Council. The increase far outpaced the 1.3 per cent growth in China’s global outbound investment.

But the surge in investment also comes with expectations from Brasilia, as Lula wants Chinese capital to help Brazil develop its own industrial capabilities, rather than simply deepen its reliance on imported technology and manufactured goods.

In an October podcast interview, Lula said Brazil should develop its own battery industry rather than “depend on the Chinese”. He also called for the country to produce its own chips and mobile phones. At the same time, he described Chinese investors as “bold” and said Brazil was “taking advantage” of the opportunity.

The dual message reflects Lula’s approach to Chinese investment: welcoming the capital and expertise that Chinese companies can bring, while seeking to use them to strengthen Brazil’s domestic industries.

Launched in 2024, the Brazilian government’s New Industry Brazil plan aims to revive domestic manufacturing and upgrade the country’s industrial base by 2033. Its targets include digitalising 90 per cent of domestic industries and tripling the share of national production in selected new-technology segments.

“Brasilia will keep an open door for Chinese trading and investment, as long as Chinese leaders and executives also understand that Lula’s main demand will be that of supporting the development of local industries or at least preventing them from declining,” Gonzalez said.

For Chinese companies, such a shift would entail higher costs and could require companies to accelerate their investment plans.

But the opportunities in South America’s biggest economy make the trade-off worthwhile, particularly as Chinese electric vehicle (EV) makers face hurdles in other overseas markets and slowing sales growth at home, Gonzalez said.

Chinese carmakers are among the companies most actively expanding local production in Brazil.

BYD is seeking permits to double annual production capacity at its Brazilian plant to 600,000 vehicles, as it nears completion of its stamping, welding and paint facilities, Reuters reported in late October.

Great Wall Motor also launched local production in Brazil last year, while Geely completed production of its first locally manufactured model in cooperation with Renault in September.

The expansion of leading Chinese carmakers in Brazil could also bring more of their suppliers to the country, according to an overseas sales executive at a Chinese EV maker, who asked not to be named because he was not authorised to speak on behalf of the company.

“BYD has invested in battery factories and related supporting industries in Brazil,” he said. “Such deep commitment of major companies like BYD would help pave the way for a broader range of Chinese suppliers and businesses across the supply chain to invest into the Brazilian market.”

Beyond the costs of localising production, Brazil’s divided political landscape presents another challenge for Chinese companies.

Although Lula won re-election, right-wing parties strengthened their position in Congress. They are set to hold a majority of seats in the Chamber of Deputies, while the right will also command nearly two-thirds of the seats in the Senate, potentially complicating Lula’s efforts to advance his legislative agenda.

Lula’s narrow victory also underscores the country’s political divisions. He won 51.79 per cent of valid votes in the October 25 run-off, defeating Flavio Bolsonaro, who received 48.21 per cent, according to Brazil’s electoral authorities.

The result reversed Bolsonaro’s lead in the first round, when he won 47.03 per cent of valid votes against Lula’s 45.16 per cent.

For Chinese companies, the challenge will be navigating not only policy uncertainty but also a political system in which Lula may find it tricky to secure congressional support for his agenda, Gonzalez said.

“Something that could be a challenge for Chinese companies during Lula’s fourth mandate … will be to understand and navigate a still very divided country and fractured political system where the majority in both chambers of Congress will be partially dominated by conservatives,” Gonzalez said.

Despite these political uncertainties, some Chinese businesses remain optimistic about Brazil’s commercial prospects.

Chen Le, an overseas sales manager at Chinese kitchenware exporter Wujo Group, said many of his Brazilian customers had hoped Flavio Bolsonaro would win the election. Chen, however, welcomed Lula’s re-election.

“Our overall outlook for the Brazilian market over the next year remains optimistic, because our exports of kitchenware to Brazil grew throughout Lula’s previous term,” Chen said.

Meanwhile, Liu, the digital textile executive, expressed long-term confidence in the market, citing Brazil’s consumer spending and the potential to modernise its textile industry.

“While Brazil has a stark wealth gap and a large lower-income population, I only realised after arriving … that much of the money consumers earn goes straight back into the economy,” he said.

Liu said Brazil’s traditional textile sector was undergoing modernisation, creating opportunities for suppliers of Chinese equipment. He also saw scope for further growth as more Chinese companies entered the market.

“I feel that certain parts of Brazil still resemble the China of a decade ago. However, with the arrival of Chinese enterprises, better logistics and the widespread adoption of automated equipment, the local market will gradually transform,” he added.
