Will China’s new local surtax provide relief for its regions’ fiscal headaches?

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1581 words | 2026-09-18

As even China’s wealthiest provinces continue to run deficits and rely on the central government to make ends meet, Beijing is loosening the reins on the country’s highly centralised fiscal system, with legislation for a new tax category – the local surtax – under way.

Late last month, the Ministry of Finance unveiled a draft local surtax law for public consultation. The proposed tax would merge three existing levies – the urban maintenance and construction tax, education surcharge and local education surcharge – and would be charged on value-added tax (VAT) and consumption tax payments. Provincial governments would be allowed to set rates within an 11 to 13 per cent range.

Analysts described the move as a modest but constructive step in China’s broader fiscal reform agenda. While far from a cure-all for local financial distress, the change would give local authorities much-needed flexibility to manage their spending, they said.

“It appears that the top leadership has heard the calls [for fiscal reform] and is aware that many localities, including coastal regions, are becoming cash-strapped,” said a frontline official with a local finance department in Zhejiang. Although the eastern province is among China’s most financially self-sufficient, it still posted a fiscal deficit of 360.6 billion yuan (US$53.8 billion) in 2025.

During China’s property boom, local authorities were able to rake in huge sums from selling land, enabling them to balance their books even as they handed over most tax revenue to the central government.

But a prolonged slump in the housing market has effectively closed off that source of income, leaving local governments under mounting pressure as they fund an expanding range of public services for a rapidly ageing population – from public transport to healthcare – and work to meet heavy bond repayment obligations.

The proposed local surtax marks the first move towards rebalancing the skewed fiscal relationship between China’s central and local governments – a long-term reform mandate laid out by the ruling Communist Party’s Central Committee at a plenum in July 2024.

“There is only one core reason the central government is pushing ahead with this reform this year – it’s time for fiscal rebalancing,” said Li Xin, a partner at Shenzhen-based DeHeng Law Offices. “Local authorities are under such acute fiscal strain that adjustments have become unavoidable.”

How does the new surtax work?

Representing a “major component of China’s fiscal and taxation reform”, the proposed local surtax is poised to become “the largest single tax category” in China’s local tax system and a pillar source of income for provincial authorities, according to Tian Zhiwei, director of the Institute of Public Policy and Governance at Shanghai University of Finance and Economics.

But it was not designed to boost fiscal revenue, analysts from Moody’s Ratings said in a report this month, adding that the proposed local surtax rate was broadly aligned with the combined effective burden of the three existing charges it would replace, and that overall fiscal revenue was likely to remain broadly unchanged.

Under the current system, the urban maintenance and construction tax is levied at 7 per cent, 5 per cent or 1 per cent of VAT and consumption tax payments, depending on the taxpayer’s location, while the education surcharge and local education surcharge are fixed at 3 per cent and 2 per cent, respectively.

In 2025, the effective combined rate nationwide stood at 11.6 per cent, according to Xinhua, compared with the 11 to 13 per cent range proposed under the new legislation.

The reform would change the institutional status of these revenue streams from three sources distributed across tax, non-tax and government fund accounts to a single tax that would be fully retained by local governments, the Moody’s analysts said, adding that this would improve revenue transparency and simplify fiscal administration.

A key proposed change concerns the two education-related surcharges: by raising their status from fees to taxes, the reform would give provincial governments greater flexibility in spending the proceeds.

Currently, the two surcharges are earmarked for education-related spending, including the purchase of teaching equipment and school building renovations. If enacted, the new law would allow local authorities to use the revenue at their discretion.

In 2025, combined proceeds from the two education-related surcharges reached 396.4 billion yuan, according to Xinhua. If enacted, the reform would allow local governments to redirect the revenue towards repaying maturing debt and meeting civil service payrolls, with civil servants facing pay cuts and delayed salary payments even in some of China’s wealthiest regions.

“Turning fees into tax and making it an income stream for local authorities marks a step towards a bigger solution,” the Zhejiang official said, on condition of anonymity.

Meanwhile, experts said the reform would also streamline tax collection and cut compliance costs.

“On collection, three separate sets of rules will be consolidated into one … Taxpayers will only need to submit one tax return, lowering manpower-related costs for both businesses and tax authorities,” said Li, the Shenzhen lawyer.

With the two fees’ conversion into taxes, taxpayers who failed to pay or who underpaid their liabilities would face penalties, including overdue payment surcharges and fines, she added.

“This gives local tax authorities much stronger incentives to bolster tax collection,” Li said.

Relief for cash-strapped local governments?

In total, the three revenue sources generated 913.4 billion yuan last year, according to Xinhua. Although the proposed local surtax would be the largest tax source fully retained by local governments, its revenue would remain relatively modest.

Last year, domestic VAT revenue reached 6.9 trillion yuan (US$1.0 trillion), half of which is allocated to local governments under the current revenue-sharing arrangement.

Corporate income tax revenue totalled 4.1 trillion yuan, and personal income tax revenue amounted to 1.6 trillion yuan. The central government kept 60 per cent, leaving 40 per cent for local governments.

Consumption tax for the year brought in 1.7 trillion yuan. Imposed on certain categories of goods – such as alcohol, tobacco and fuel – the income generated goes entirely to the central government.

“The issue is that even with our income dropping, a big portion still needs to be turned over to the central government as state tax, leaving us with limited fiscal resources … The main complaint is that the state is taking too much,” said the Zhejiang finance official.

Daisy Xiao, director of Asia-Pacific international public finance ratings at Fitch Ratings, said the reform should modestly improve local revenue flexibility and spending discretion, but would be unlikely to materially ease structural pressure on local finances because debt growth had far outpaced the expansion of fiscal resources in recent years.

“The extra tax income would be small against persistent structural deficits, which are largely financed through special-purpose bonds linked to capital spending,” Xiao said.

“Even so, the measure is a constructive step in broadening more stable local revenue sources and giving local governments greater fiscal discretion,” she added.

More broadly, the move was consistent with China’s fiscal reform agenda and longer-term objective of reshaping local fiscal incentives away from property-related revenue and towards economic growth, consumption and sustainable tax bases, according to the Moody’s analysts.

“While incremental in nature, it represents a step in expanding provincial fiscal autonomy, supporting a more sustainable local revenue structure,” they added.

Rare autonomy for rate-setting

Under the proposal, provincial governments would be allowed to set the new rate within a range of 11 to 13 per cent, signalling greater flexibility.

“This is notable because most major tax rates in China are set by the central government, leaving local authorities with few levers to adjust revenue,” Xiao said.

Rate-setting discretion within an approved band would give provinces a limited additional countercyclical tool, allowing them to respond to changing revenue needs and local economic conditions, she added.

Tian, from Shanghai University of Finance and Economics, said final tax rates would be the product of provincial governments’ “trade-offs”.

Authorities would need to strike a balance between retaining businesses and easing fiscal pressure: set the rate too high and they risk driving firms away; set it too low and they forgo revenue when public finances are already under strain, he said.

“That is one reason why the central government gives provinces the discretion over setting tax rates – local authorities know their own circumstances best,” Tian added.

Wealthier provinces may choose lower rates to strengthen their competitiveness, while fiscally weaker regions could opt for higher rates to bolster revenue, according to Moody’s.

“As a result, fiscal management could become incrementally more province-specific, reflecting local economic conditions and policy priorities,” the analysts added.

But the reform could also bring unintended consequences for some localities. Under the current urban maintenance and construction tax rules, a 7 per cent rate applies in urban areas, 5 per cent in counties and designated towns, and 1 per cent elsewhere, including rural areas and oil-producing fields.

As the proposed legislation would replace that system with a single rate across an entire province, businesses in counties and rural areas could face a higher tax burden, according to Li, the lawyer.

“From a provincial perspective, the overall tax burden may see little change, yet for individual companies, it translates into real-world cost increases.”
