China's policy push to steer bank credit toward technology, green industries, and small businesses is creating intense competition among lenders, while healthy firms outside these favored sectors struggle to obtain loans. A bank branch manager described visiting a tech company that was too busy hiring and expanding to even need a loan, contrasting sharply with non-tech companies that find banks reluctant to lend to them.

Since the 2023 Central Financial Work Conference defined "five major categories" (technology, green, inclusive, pension, and digital finance), banks have received strong incentives to expand lending in these areas. The results are dramatic: People's Bank of China Governor Pan Gongsheng said new loans to real estate and infrastructure fell from over 60% of the total a decade ago to around 10%, while the five categories now account for more than 70% of new lending.

Technology companies are among the biggest beneficiaries. Outstanding loans to small and midsize tech enterprises reached 4.15 trillion yuan ($622.6 billion) at the end of June, up 20% year-on-year. Competition is intense, especially for smaller loans under 10 million yuan that can qualify as both technology and inclusive finance. According to one branch manager, banks have "greatly relaxed restrictions on interest rates and terms."

The rapid expansion raises questions about credit quality. A city commercial bank manager said technology-labeled loans have grown rapidly over the past two years, creating hidden risks. Banks often rely on government lists of high-tech firms and "little giants," but "the threshold is generally not high," according to a joint-stock bank employee.

Technology borrowers tend to be younger companies with less predictable cash flows and higher failure risks, unlike green loans concentrated in established businesses. Regulators have responded by allowing greater tolerance for nonperforming loans (NPLs) to tech companies, with inclusive loan NPL ratios permitted to be up to 3 percentage points higher than the overall ratio.

Pressure is particularly acute in inclusive finance. Outstanding inclusive loans to micro and small businesses reached 38.52 trillion yuan at the end of June, nearly five times the level at the end of 2018. While some joint-stock banks are shrinking this business due to rising bad loans, state-owned banks face pressure to maintain growth. This creates unintended risks—one executive noted companies are splitting borrowing across multiple lenders to take 8-10 million yuan from each, making it harder for any bank to see the borrower's full leverage.

Loan intermediaries add another risk layer. While legitimate intermediaries help small businesses, illegal operators forge documents and use shell companies that maintain tax payments and bank transaction records to appear genuine. A compliance officer told Caixin that investigations found bank employees colluding with illegal intermediaries. Regulators have launched repeated crackdowns over the past two years targeting forged documents and shell companies.

The problems unfold amid a broader shift in China's financing system. In July, corporate bond and equity financing reached 464 billion yuan versus a 130 billion yuan decline in corporate loans; in August, such financing reached 335.1 billion yuan, exceeding the 260 billion yuan increase in corporate loans. Nonfinancial corporate bond issuance reached 2.71 trillion yuan this year as of early September, up about 210 billion yuan year-on-year, with five-year AAA-rated bonds priced around 1.8% on average.

Banks face additional constraints. The Self-Regulatory Pricing Mechanism for Market Interest Rates, guided by the central bank, prevents destructive competition by encouraging minimum pricing standards. One bank employee said high-quality borrowers can face loan rates above 2%, while comparable bond financing costs only 1.5% to 1.7%, making bonds far more attractive to the very companies banks most want to lend to.
