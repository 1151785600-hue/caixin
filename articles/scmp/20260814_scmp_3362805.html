Wok with Yan’s return: Hong Kong-Canadian TV chef Stephen Yan resurfaces decades after disappearing

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2701 words | 2026-08-14

“When Marco Polo went to China, you know what he did? We showed him how to make chow mein noodles, we showed him how to make wontons.”

An energetic Chinese man with a high forehead and gregarious smile is standing in a studio kitchen, dishing out as many puns as he is plates of wok-fried rice for a studio audience at the Canadian Broadcasting Corporation (CBC), in Vancouver.

From 1978 to 1995, Stephen Yan garnered legions of fans in Canada, the United States and beyond, largely thanks to half-hour episodes of Wok with Yan, each seeing a dish prepared from start to finish in real time. The chef would cook up Cantonese classics such as beef and broccoli, garlic spare ribs and stir-fried prawns while dressed in aprons emblazoned with witticisms such as “Wok’s New, Pussycat?”, “Wok up a Storm” or, in this early episode, “Wok-a-Doodle-Doo”.

“When [Polo] went back to Italy, he learned how to make it all right,” says Yan to the camera, in his Cantonese-inflected English, “but he changed the name chow mein to spaghetti and changed wonton to ravioli. Wok the heck, I’m going to make one thing perfectly clear: I want to make this thing right and correct. I don’t want to make it the wrong way. I want to make it the Yan way.”

Once Yan had plated a dish, he’d invite someone from the audience to dine with him, and, this being Canada in the 1980s, they would not all be proficient with chopsticks. Yan would open a fortune cookie, translate the Chinese message inside and then, as the credits rolled, the rest of the studio audience would make sure no food went to waste.

With the coast-to-coast-to-coast reach of the CBC, Yan became a national figure and arguably the first Asian celebrity television chef in the West.

“I watched [Yan] with my grandparents because, back then, your house had one TV,” says Karon Liu, who was only a few months old when his family moved to Toronto in 1987, one of more than 355,600 Hongkongers who moved to Canada in the late 80s, according to the Canadian International Council. “So, what they watched, you had to watch,” says Liu, who is now a food reporter with the Toronto Star newspaper. He remembers his grandmother hollowing out a cucumber and filling it with stir-fried beef, making it look like a boat, because that’s what Stephen Yan did in one episode.

“He was the Chinese Julia Child. He wasn’t some snooty Michelin French chef; he cooked the way my grandmother would cook at home,” says Liu. “He explained a lot of ingredients that weren’t as well known outside of a Chinese audience at that time.”

While Chinese-Canadians delighted in Yan’s familiarity, other Canadians were learning that Chinese food was not just egg foo yung or sweet and sour chicken balls, a step towards the multiculturalism the country would more fully embrace. But then one day in 1995, after more than 500 episodes, Wok with Yan ended, and the chef was not seen on television or in other media again – well, until he briefly surfaced the year before last.

Born and raised in the Toronto, Ontario suburb of Scarborough in the 80s, one of Yan’s biggest fans was Steve Gaskin. Before he was old enough to go to school, Gaskin’s Guyanese father and Chinese mother would drop him off at his grandparents’ home at 7am before going to work, and pick him up 12 hours later.

“I found it very hard to connect with my gung-gung [maternal grandfather] because my parents never spoke Cantonese, and he didn’t speak much English. I took Cantonese lessons, but I didn’t retain any of it,” says Gaskin. His grandfather, who ran a laundromat business in his adopted home, brought his family from Hong Kong to Canada via Guyana more than 50 years ago.

“My gung-gung only communicated in Chinese,” says Gaskin, “but just from grunts and facial cues, I knew what he was saying.”

The pair managed to bond over Wok with Yan. “I would sit next to him on the La-Z-Boy and just without even talking, we would laugh at the jokes and he would just kind of nudge me when something funny came on, or he wanted me to watch the technique.

“In the 80s, Stephen Yan was probably the only Chinese-Canadian celebrity. I was probably six years old, and the only Asian person on the big screen was Bruce Lee or Jackie Chan, so to see somebody like Stephen Yan, who’s in Canada, who was so approachable, was really important.”

In 2018, Gaskin and a friend launched Retrokid, a company that, through T-shirts and accessories, recalls childhood memories for Gen Xers who grew up watching Canadian television shows such as Mr Dressup, Polka Dot Door and Degrassi. Gaskin was determined to track down his hero.

After years of dead ends, in 2022, he finally got a response. But it wasn’t until a year later that Yan agreed to meet him.

Gaskin is coy about how he finally got in contact with Yan, divulging only that the former chef wanted “to work with a company that understands everything he’s done”. And with the success of Retrokid and the wider appetites for online nostalgia spun into products, Yan the savvy businessman must have seen that the time was ripe for a revival.

“Stephen Yan always said that he appreciated that there was this personal connection I had with my gung-gung as to why he wanted to do this with us.”

Together, they turned out “Wok with Yan” T-shirts, hoodies, tote bags, badges and fridge magnets, bearing the chef’s likeness from his television days. And, of course, a range of aprons with witty Yan-isms has proved to be the bestseller.

To help launch the collection, Yan agreed to fly from Vancouver to Toronto in August 2024 for a meet-and-greet with fans.

“It was pretty shocking to see him just come out of the arrivals gate,” says Gaskin, who picked Yan up at the airport. “Like, oh my God, it’s Stephen Yan. I didn’t know if he was going to come off a plane in a wheelchair or on crutches, right? But no, he’s super strong and mentally healthy for 85.”

While Yan was in Toronto, Retrokid gave Liu a chance to interview the chef for the Star, and, like Gaskin, the reporter was transported back to his childhood.

“It was really surreal,” says Liu. “He was still making jokes, he was just like a grandpa, where you ask one question and then his answer is, like, 10 minutes long because he’s lived such a full life.”

According to what he said in the interview, it was Yan who decided to pull the plug on his show in 1995.

“One day, I asked my accountant how much money I needed to survive,” Yan told Liu. “He said I already had a house and I didn’t need a dollar more. I wanted to contribute my time to those less fortunate, so I helped out with orphanages and schools in places like Thailand and China. I had enough earthly belongings.

“Back then, I had three restaurants [in Vancouver] and I had three disciples, including Martin Yan [no relation; a Hong Kong-American who worked with Yan in the 70s],” he explained. His protégé’s own television show, Yan Can Cook, also first aired on Canadian television in 1978 (initially as Yan Can), before having a highly successful run in the US, on PBS.

Stephen Yan also told Liu that, after quitting the show, he spent more time with his family and became much more active. “I ran 11 marathons, four times at the Boston Marathon and won the masters division. I also climbed Mount Everest.”

For someone so beloved, there isn’t a lot of biographical information available online about Yan, and he guards his private life closely. His birth date, for example, is not widely known, only that he was born in Hong Kong in 1939.

Not long after, in December 1941, the Japanese occupation of the city began and basic ingredients such as rice, flour and oil were rationed.

“[As a child,] I was poor and went to people’s stores where they would give me leftover food,” Yan told Liu.

“I was spending my time on the streets next to a theatre that was starring a Chinese actor, who was Bruce Lee’s father. Bruce and I became friends, and we used to play in a nearby warehouse, but I needed a job.”

When Yan was nine years old, a restaurant cook gave him work in exchange for food. One of his most laborious tasks was to take thousands of lotus seeds from a pot of boiling water, without gloves, and use a toothpick to push out their bitter cores. After three months, Yan was allowed into the kitchen, to learn from the cook, who taught him how to kill and pluck chickens, and use every part of the bird, as well as shop for the best ingredients.

Yan claimed to have been the fastest dishwasher at the restaurant. “Humans are adaptable and can do anything if they have the opportunity,” he told Liu.

He became a fully-fledged cook, and when he was around 15 years old, the chief cook of the restaurant arranged for him to go to school. Later, Yan said, he was one of two students chosen by an American senator to study business administration in the US, to which he sailed on an 18-day voyage to San Francisco.

Fearing being called up to the military as the US fought in Vietnam, Yan crossed the border to Canada in the late 50s or early 60s, he told Liu, one of 20,000 to 30,000 draft dodgers to have done so, according to Canadian immigration statistics.

By the late 70s, Yan was already managing his own restaurants when he was noticed by a television talent scout while leading cooking demonstrations in a shopping mall in the run-up to Chinese New Year.

The weekly Yan’s Woking debuted on Vancouver station BCTV in 1978. Two years later, national broadcaster CBC bought the show, rebranded it as Wok with Yan and shifted formats to present a daily afternoon cooking series.

Gaskin says some 230 people showed up at Toronto’s storied Royal Theatre for 2024’s meet-and-greet.

“It was great to give [Yan] a platform where he could just say thank you to all of his fans and the fans showed up,” says Gaskin. “Obviously, we could have packed that theatre to 500 people, but we also wanted to give the fans a chance to meet him, to actually go up and take a photo, and they loved it.”

Since then, Yan has remained elusive. All attempts to contact him by this author have been fruitless. The Retrokid team has been sworn to secrecy; Liu says he never got direct contact. And even now, almost two years after Yan’s rarest of public appearances, he shows no signs of emerging into the spotlight again, even to contribute to this article, to connect with his hometown, Hong Kong.

Vancouverite Billy Snaychuk wasn’t at the meet-up but considers himself a fan. “My most favourite part about Stephen Yan would have to be his aprons, and the way he always made the audience laugh,” he says. “He just captured the audience right away from the get-go. He definitely caught me as a kid, because I just loved watching him.”

Snaychuk’s father, a fisherman and entrepreneur, got to know Yan through Chinese friends who caught fish for the chef, delivering them to his restaurant, Yan’s Garden. From the age of six, Snaychuk watched Wok with Yan with his mother every day after school.

“My parents had the largest collection of Stephen Yan books, and I remember sneaking out of my room in the middle of the night and grabbing his books on the bookshelf and bringing them back. And I would just look at all the pictures and read the recipes,” he recalls, with a chuckle.

Once a month, his parents invited friends over and cooked dishes from Yan’s cookbooks, including Snaychuk’s favourite, gold coin beef.

“The flavour profile in it is just next level. And the texture of the beef, you can almost chew it with the roof of your mouth and your tongue, it’s just that tender,” says Snaychuk, who would follow in his hero’s footsteps and become a chef himself. He was promoted to executive chef at the age of 28 and went into restaurant consulting and even competed in the Food Network Canada cooking reality TV show Wall of Chefs.

As he got older, Snaychuk paid attention to Yan’s technique, particularly how he created flavour profiles by adding ingredients to the wok at different times.

“A lot of people, when they cook, they just kind of throw everything in there. If you do that, the whole dish is basically going to taste the same. But if you add things at different times and take them out and put them back in, you layer the flavours. And that’s something I really noticed – he layered his flavours with his cooking.”

While watching Yan was a joy for so many, working for him was not always so pleasurable, says former assistant Brenda Gonneville, who also has no idea where Yan lives now. “He’s not the face you see on TV.”

In 1981, Yan hired a 26-year-old Gonneville to help him in his office, which was above Yan’s Chinese restaurant, in Port Coquitlam, British Columbia. She had worked as an assistant to high-powered executives in the steel industry, says Gonneville, so was “well versed in dealing with high-powered, difficult people”.

Her job, as a native English speaker, was to look after Yan’s public relations, devise witty lines for the aprons and procure props and have them shipped to Ottawa, where his show was taped for two seasons before moving back to Vancouver. She recalls being the only Caucasian in an office full of Cantonese-speaking people but says she got on well with all the other staff. Gonneville was aiming to forge a career in entertainment, so worked for a low wage. After paying rent, she says, she was left with enough money only for a diet of white rice and boiled eggs.

“After three months, I’d had enough,” she says, “because he is a perfectionist, and nothing pleases him. He’s a hard worker. He expects more than 100 per cent from his employees.”

One day, she says, he was dictating content for a show and Gonneville was struggling to write it all down in shorthand, because he was speaking quickly.

“He had me read it back and I said, ‘I’m sorry, I didn’t get most of that.’ I decided then and there, and so did he, that we’d come to the end of the road.”

Fortunately, her brief stint with Yan led to a 20-year career in film and television.

When she returned to pack up her things, the office manager “took my hand and said, ‘Don’t you worry, you lasted longer than anybody’”, she recalls with a laugh.

“[Yan’s] an A-type personality, he’s driven. He expected everybody to put in the same effort as him, and if you failed, he wouldn’t put up with it.”

Difficult he may have been for those he employed, but, given the delight he’s brought to television viewers, one cannot begrudge his telling the hosts of Toronto daytime talk show The Social, in August 2024, that, “I was hiding; I want a simple life.

“I […] cannot believe that people remember me after 30 years,” he said on The Social. “And they want to know, am I alive? I am! I’m alive!”

And maybe that’s enough, to know that he’s alive. With Yan now well into his 80s, a quiet, simple life is perhaps just wok he needs.
