Philippines eyes drones and ‘system of systems’ to boost asymmetric defence

SCMP | FREE | 1074 words | 2026-08-27

Facing a far larger Chinese military presence at sea, the Philippines is looking to drones not as a substitute for warships and aircraft but as a relatively low-cost way to make its forces harder to find, suppress and outmanoeuvre, analysts have said.

The administration of President Ferdinand Marcos Jnr has focused on expanding the military’s drone fleet as part of its external defence strategy. Defence Secretary Gilberto Teodoro Jnr told lawmakers on Tuesday that Manila also needed to secure communications, artificial intelligence and operational networks to integrate unmanned systems into military operations.

During a House appropriations committee hearing on the Department of National Defence’s proposed budget, Teodoro said the initiative would be funded through a proposed 50 billion-peso (US$810 million) allocation for military modernisation in 2027, a 25 per cent increase from this year’s budget.

“We have to integrate the capabilities and have proper working procedures in using them,” Teodoro said. “We currently have a fleet of unmanned aerial vehicles, which provide intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance services for us. As we mature on the cycle, then we can acquire more.”

Acquiring drones was not the main challenge, he said, the more sensitive issue involved the systems they carried and the capabilities those systems could provide.

“Initially, we have to have the connectivity and the network capabilities to operate them as real time as possible … Connectivity and the security of connectivity is paramount for us,” he added, noting they were in “close contact” with several countries with drone expertise.

Analysts said Manila’s shift towards asymmetric external defence and integrated operations increased the strategic importance of acquiring low-cost, high-utility drones and strengthening its cyber capabilities.

“Drone development entails sustainable effort on subsidising local research and development, optimising technology transfers and building indigenous capability,” Chester Cabalza, president and chief executive of the Manila-based International Development and Security Cooperation think tank, told This Week in Asia.

“The Armed Forces of the Philippines is looking to Ukraine’s development of an indigenous drone capability as a model. Rather than relying solely on expensive conventional hardware, locally produced drones could support asymmetric maritime deterrence, with aerial and maritime drones serving as force multipliers.”

South China Sea concerns

Justin Kyle Malarayap, a visiting assistant professor at Arellano University in Manila, said the Philippines was building networks to support drone operations in preparation for a potential South China Sea contingency.

“As we’ve seen recently, the Philippines has been interacting with Ukraine, the US and Japan to build these networks and capabilities to improve Philippine joint military operations,” he said.

Vina Nadjibulla, co-founder and chief executive of the Centre for Strategic Statecraft, a Canadian foreign policy think tank, agreed that drones could serve as force multipliers.

She said the significance of Teodoro’s disclosure was that Manila was treating drones as part of a “system of systems” connecting unmanned platforms with sensors, secure communications, command-and-control networks, ships, aircraft and land-based missile systems.

“That is consistent with the Philippines’ broader Comprehensive Archipelagic Defence Concept. The lesson from Ukraine and other recent conflicts is that a drone is only as valuable as the network behind it,” Nadjibulla said.

The archipelagic concept is Manila’s framework for shifting its military focus towards external defence across the country’s territory, maritime zones and approaches.

Manila and Kyiv are reportedly in discussions to advance an October 2025 initiative to pursue drone co-production, a proposed partnership that could combine Ukraine’s battle-tested technology with the Philippines’ transition towards a more agile, technology-driven defence posture.

The Philippine military currently operates unmanned systems across all three service branches for intelligence, surveillance, reconnaissance and emerging combat missions.

The army uses Elbit THOR and commercial DJI drones, while the air force operates Hermes 450 and 900 platforms. The navy and marine corps deploy ScanEagle drones for maritime surveillance and are developing unmanned surface and underwater systems.

In April, the air force and its government and industry partners also unveiled locally developed autonomous, loitering and armed drones for reconnaissance and precision-strike missions.

Discussing Manila’s drone expansion, Teodoro said the priority was integrating the systems into military operations, including the secure communications and network capabilities needed for effective use.

Multi-year investment

Building that integrated architecture, however, requires sustained funding.

Nadjibulla said the proposed allocation was insufficient to develop the full defence network Manila ultimately needed, adding that credible deterrence would require multi-year investment rather than the occasional acquisition of individual platforms.

“It can build a significantly better and operationally useful network in the near term. The priority has to be resilience and redundancy. Partners can substantially accelerate that process,” Nadjibulla said.

Philippine allies such as the US, Japan and Australia were coordinating investments in sensors, information-sharing systems and cybersecurity, while Canada was providing maritime domain awareness capabilities such as “dark vessel detection”, Nadjibulla said.

“The Philippines does not need technological parity with China. It needs sufficient awareness, resilience and distributed capability to complicate Chinese military planning and strengthen deterrence by denial,” she said.

However, achieving such capabilities will require Manila to make difficult spending decisions.

Malarayap said persistent financial constraints were forcing the government to prioritise military projects based on their demonstrated combat effectiveness and evolving threat assessments.

Yet he was optimistic that Manila could build the communications and operational networks needed to make unmanned systems effective in a near-term conflict.

The Philippine military was prioritising communications systems to improve its response to natural disasters and security contingencies, and thereby boost archipelagic logistics, Malarayap said.

Cabalza was more cautious, saying Manila’s AI-enabled surveillance and command-and-control capabilities were limited, but increased defence funding could help the armed forces address those gaps.

“This is a David vs Goliath scenario of UAVs. While military armaments are key to war success, strategy places the best part in its execution. The world has not yet seen a sea battle of swarming UAVs,” Cabalza said.

For Nadjibulla, unmanned systems could help offset the advantage of China’s much larger fleet but could not eliminate it, and that disparity was precisely why drones and other unmanned platforms made strategic sense for the Philippines.

“China will retain an overwhelming numerical advantage in ships and aircraft, so Manila cannot realistically compete platform for platform,” Nadjibulla said.

“Drones offer a relatively inexpensive way to generate persistent surveillance, distribute sensors across a very large maritime area and make Philippine forces harder to find and suppress.”
