Wear now, pay later: the new rules of Southeast Asia’s indulgence economy

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1682 words | 2026-09-05

With its padded leather and gold “CC” lock, Coco Chanel’s instantly recognisable bag is practically part of the uniform for any self-respecting Bangkok property agent – an apt accessory for a city that recently edged into the top 10 anywhere for luxury spending.

But in chastened economic times liquidity and luxury are no longer in lockstep, challenging many of those with expensive tastes to find new ways to keep up appearances while balancing their books.

Jirapun Ngam, 42, works in the fast-paced business of buying and selling the Thai capital’s skyline.

She pays for her designer goods on instalments, keeping cash to buy, renovate and flip homes as they hit the market.

“I need cash to invest in my business, but I also want luxury items,” she said. “With instalment plans, I get the product immediately and pay it off monthly with only a small amount of interest.”

Her calculation reflects a growing mindset among Southeast Asia’s aspirant middle class, a cohort unwilling to let their tastes decouple from the super-rich, despite the economic turbulence of the last few years.

Instead, an alternative luxury economy is evolving.

Live-streamed bargains, online resale markets and instalment purchases are bridging the gap between aspiration and affordability.

It is a market tier that is “resilient rather than immune” to economic headwinds, according to Mariella Merlino, a luxury commercial and merchandising executive.

“The higher end of the customer base remains strong, while more aspirational consumption is more exposed to macroeconomic pressure.”

But even in belt-tightening times, setting luxury aside can be hard to do.

Jirapun said the Chanel Classic bag and Rolex watches lent “a polished and distinguished image” to her and her husband.

“Could I afford to buy them without financing? Absolutely. But this makes more sense.”

Similar forces that are squeezing household spending have reshaped how governments plot their economic plans for the coming months as the jarring blows of tariffs, fuel costs and inflationary spikes rip up growth forecasts.

Throughout Southeast Asia, luxury has become a bellwether of confidence – holding firm from Jakarta to Ho Chi Minh City and growing in the region’s bling capital, Singapore.

In Bangkok, a city whose haves flit through a world of glamour and valet-parked supercars, top brands have clustered around the money.

Louis Vuitton and Dior opened concept stores over the last two years – the latter a garish “Gold House” in downtown Chidlom, where ultra-high net worth visitors arrive in Lamborghinis at malls stacked with high-end shops and restaurants.

Thailand’s luxury retail sector is estimated at around US$4.4 billion and expected to grow by 5 per cent each year for the next two to three years, despite the flattening out of demand from China where an economy in the teeth of Washington’s tariffs has lost some of its sheen over the last few years.

The big beasts of Thailand’s luxury property sector certainly have faith in the future.

They are backing the continued spending of the global uber-rich whose record stock gains, tech assets or energy holdings have soared in value while the real economy has been ground down by the US-induced Iran crisis.

On Tuesday, Asset World Corporation – majority-owned by Thai billionaire Charoen Sirivadhanabhakdi, one of the country’s richest people – announced a US$90 million investment in a new ultra-luxury hotel on the city’s Chao Phraya river.

Thailand’s leading mall operators Siam Piwat, Central Group and The Mall Group are also developing hundreds of thousands of square metres of prime Bangkok property for shopping experiences shaped by luxury brands.

“What makes Southeast Asia particularly interesting is that luxury brands are not looking at the region purely as a defensive market. They are investing for future growth,” said Merlino, former regional head of luxury for Singapore-based online fashion giant Zalora.

“The amount of investment going into malls, flagships, hospitality-led concepts and client experiences suggests that both brands and developers see Southeast Asia’s affluent consumer base becoming strategically more important within Asia.”

While those aim at the global super-rich who spend freely as they flit across Asia’s capitals, wealth experts say the thriving second-hand and resale market for luxury points to multiple layers of demand in Southeast Asia.

For Paphon Manaspakorn, CEO of Brandname Money, credit and collateral are proving a lucrative entry point to the luxury market.

He says his company’s balance sheet proves demand for luxury is here to stay, with around 200 million baht (US$6 million) in loans out so far for bags, watches and jewellery – on down payments of 30-50 per cent of the item’s value.

“People are completely open and accustomed to paying in instalments,” he told This Week in Asia, as the embarrassment of buying luxury on finance – or pawning it for quick cash – dissolved.

In fact, the taboo of today’s society may be not having the item, rather than how you got it.

“Your car is on instalments, your house is on instalments. You can see on TikTok that people even do TikTok instalments, or Shopee PayLater,” Paphon said.

Customers pay back at reasonable, legal rates of under 12 per cent a year, he says, insisting there is none of the strong-arming associated with the loan industry in Thailand if clients miss a payment.

“Should luxury only belong to the very wealthy? We wanted to make it accessible – so that you can achieve this kind of success sooner, as long as you are financially disciplined and pay in instalments.”

Instead of a contraction as the global economy bucks under energy price pressures, Paphon forecasts a doubling of the value of loans his firm seeks to provide in coming years.

And the “other side” of the business – pawning luxury items – is also doing a roaring trade.

“As the economy slows, many people come to pawn items to get cash,” he said. “I have a married couple who are customers who pawned two watches worth 11 million baht combined – one a Richard Mille and the other a Patek Philippe.”

Rich getting richer

The world’s super-rich are accumulating so much, so fast that they have in many ways cut ties with the “normal” economy experienced by the majority.

Last year, there were 556,850 ultra high net worth (UHNW) individuals across the world, according to Altrata which maps wealth – a nearly 15 per cent increase on the year, mainly powered by the technology boom.

Altrata’s most recent report, published in June, defines someone with US$30 million or more in assets as UHNW.

The US dominates with about 40 per cent of the world’s UHNW individuals.

But Asia is growing fast, with just over 141,000 people, a quarter of the global number, most of them clustered in China, India, Japan and South Korea.

The Asian super-rich also differ from their American peers, according to Julius Baer, a Swiss wealth management firm.

They are younger – and count many more women among them – and their businesses across manufacturing and tech supply chains have proved adaptive despite the US tariff onslaught on China.

“Luxury demand globally is increasingly concentrated among high net worth and top tier clients, whose spending tends to be less sensitive to short-term economic cycles,” Merlino said. “In Southeast Asia, this is reinforced by continued wealth creation and a growing concentration of affluent consumers across the region.”

In Julius Baer’s annual wealth and lifestyle report, published last month, five of the top 10 cities for spending by the global rich were in the Asia-Pacific.

Singapore retains top spot, with Hong Kong (fourth), Shanghai (sixth), Sydney (eighth) and Bangkok creeping in at 10th spot – elbowing out New York and Dubai.

The study, based on an index of prices for 20 goods and services from supercars to private education, showed being super-rich was around 10 per cent more expensive this year than last due to inflation.

Wealth experts say luxury goods have increased by an average of 12.3 per cent, with gold doubling in price, taking with it other precious metals to drive up the prices of jewellery and watches.

Still, Julius Baer said the Asia-Pacific “remains a powerhouse of global affluence”, with Singapore “the world’s most expensive city for HNWIs [high net worth individuals] for the fourth consecutive year”.

The report was compiled before the impact of the Iran crisis.

Taste and spending patterns are changing, with some experts saying Chinese consumers are warming up to domestic luxury brands, suggesting that Western fashion houses may not have it all their way going ahead as currency fluctuations and geopolitical ructions lead many to spend their money closer to home.

Rental vehicles

Yet in an economy whose gears are shifting, even high net worth Thais and holidaymakers are seeking to avoid onerous financial burdens without jettisoning their luxe experiences.

Twenty-four hours behind the wheel of a McLaren 720S will set you back around US$5,400 at Primes Automotive, a Bangkok-headquartered luxury car rental company.

While the lights have flashed red on growth, government debt and the household spending of many Thais, the cost of renting a supercar seems to be no barrier to those with money to spend.

Company marketing manager Anakin Rittimontree says hard times tend to sieve out those who want a luxury just for show.

“Some people used to want them [supercars] for events, others for multilevel marketing businesses or to project an image of success,” he told This Week in Asia.

“These days, far fewer people rent luxury cars simply to create an image. Most of our customers are genuine car enthusiasts.”

The company has seen 22 per cent growth over the last year in the rentals of the 50 or so Bentleys, McLarens, Ferraris and Lamborghinis at its Bangkok showroom.

It was a sign of a more discerning, mobile global elite who “view everything as an investment”, Anakin said.

“If they know they will drive a luxury car fewer than four days a week, they choose to rent rather than buy. Consumers are becoming smarter. They pay only for what they actually use, and financially it makes much more sense.”
