Hong Kong Student’s Death at U.K. Camp Was Preventable, Review Finds

Caixin Global | FULLTEXT | 783 words | 2026-09-29 03:58

1. A 16-year-old Hong Kong student, identified as Luna, died by suicide at a London summer camp after persistent failures by her boarding school, host families, and camp management left severe mental health risks unaddressed

[para.

1

]

. She was found dead in her residence more than 40 hours after her last key card entry, having overdosed on medication after telling staff she wanted to leave the day before

[para.

2

]

. The child-safeguarding review concluded her death, ruled a suicide, could have been prevented due to failures to share information, assess risks, and establish a safety plan

[para.

3

]

.

2. Luna arrived in Britain in September 2022 to study at Wrekin College in Shropshire, after her parents hoped the new environment and the school’s psychology courses would help her recover from anxiety and depression worsened by Hong Kong’s social unrest and the Covid-19 pandemic

[para.

5

]

. She had been out of school for over two years and had received psychiatric treatment prior to arriving; while her parents discussed her depression and anxiety with the school before enrollment, the school only learned after she arrived about her ADHD diagnosis and history of self-harm

[para.

6

]

.

3. Within three weeks of starting school, her condition deteriorated, causing insomnia, daytime sleepiness, self-harm, and suicidal thoughts

[para.

7

]

. She was taking several psychiatric drugs brought from Hong Kong, including antidepressant, antipsychotic, and ADHD medications, but no UK doctor conducted a comprehensive assessment of her condition and medication regimen

[para.

8

]

. The school sought help from the NHS’s Child and Adolescent Mental Health Services (CAMHS), but the referral wasn’t pursued after she declined to engage; the review stated that, given evidence of self-harm and suicide risk, agencies should have continued assessing her risk and devised a safety plan despite her reluctance

[para.

9

]

.

4. In spring 2023, her mental health worsened again, and she resumed harming herself

[para.

10

]

. That summer, on her educational guardian’s recommendation, she enrolled in a London psychology summer camp run by a private company, housed in a University College London residence but with no teaching or academic affiliation with the university

[para.

10

]

. She quickly disliked the program and asked a counselor to leave the camp the day before her death; the guardian advised she should make the request herself and explain it to her parents

[para.

11

]

. She missed class the following day, staff knocked on her door but didn’t verify she was okay, and she was found dead on the afternoon of the third day

[para.

12

]

.

5. The review identified serious shortcomings by her guardians. The basic guardianship service appointed a guardian who never met Luna after her arrival in Britain and declined to come to the school during an early mental health crisis, saying the visit wasn’t part of the guardian’s responsibilities

[para.

13

]

. A second, Cantonese-speaking guardian later took over, but lacked formal training in child mental health, self-harm, and suicide risk, despite exercising broad influence over decisions affecting her care

[para.

14

]

. Luna stayed with four host families, at least two of whom had no knowledge of her mental health history; during one placement, she said she wanted to jump off a bridge, leading the host family to decline further accommodation

[para.

15

]

. Instead of conducting a further risk assessment, the guardian told her parents the host family was ill or away and moved her to another placement

[para.

16

]

.

6. Financially, Luna’s parents paid 24,036 yuan ($3,400) a year for guardianship services, plus an additional 13,353 yuan for her mental health needs

[para.

17

]

. The second guardian profited from arrangements for her care, including adding a markup to counseling offered by one host-family provider

[para.

17

]

. The guardian recommended the psychology camp, which cost 60,000 yuan, and received a referral commission from its operator; Luna’s parents said they learned of this commission only after her death

[para.

18

]

.

7. About 26,000 non-British children board at private schools in the UK each year, but British visa rules don’t require international students to have educational guardians, although schools sponsoring student visas must ensure suitable care arrangements

[para.

19

]

. The Association for the Education and Guardianship of International Students (AEGIS) operates a voluntary accreditation program, but membership isn’t mandatory, and there is no national law establishing minimum qualifications, training requirements, caseload limits, or supervisory rules for educational guardians

[para.

20

]

. The safeguarding review urged the UK Department for Education to establish statutory national minimum standards and clarify that guardians and host families hold positions of special trust; it also recommended that international boarding students with mental health conditions be assessed by a UK-based clinician upon arrival

[para.

21

]

. It further found that schools communicating with overseas parents through guardians blurred responsibilities and delayed the delivery of critical mental health information to Luna’s family
