China narrows the path to elite official training scheme for citizens graduating overseas

SCMP | FREE | 1212 words | 2026-11-06

China is further narrowing access to a key government recruitment programme for overseas-educated graduates, limiting their pathways into the country’s civil service and party-state system.

More than a dozen provincial-level regions, including Beijing and Shanghai, opened applications for the 2027 xuandiaosheng recruitment rounds in September and October.

Xuandiaosheng, or the “selected graduates” system, is designed to select and train university graduates to become elite cadres and officials within the party and government. Recruits must spend several years working at the grass-roots level in townships and sub-districts before moving into leading government and party roles.

None of the local government notices released so far has offered an application channel for overseas-educated graduates, continuing a trend that was evident last year.

An Australian-educated arts graduate who called herself James said she had considered the xuandiaosheng programme among her main career options as she looked for work this year.

“Job hunting is already so difficult. I really valued the opportunity to apply for the xuandiaosheng programme but now I have no chance at all,” she said.

Part of her decision to pursue a master’s degree overseas was driven by what she perceived as welcoming signs from the programme around 2023. At least 10 provincial-level regions, including Beijing and Guangdong, explicitly listed selected top-ranked overseas universities as eligible in their xuandiaosheng recruitment notices for the 2023 and 2024 graduating cohorts.

Similar frustrations have surfaced on RedNote, a Chinese social media platform also known as Xiaohongshu. “I can’t believe it. I went to graduate school just to qualify for the programme,” one comment read.

The programme dates back to 1965, when China’s leaders saw rural assignments for university graduates as a way to train “successors to the revolution”, according to state media outlet Xinhua’s Liaowang magazine.

President Xi Jinping has backed the programme since at least 2008, when he was still vice-president. He has called for its improvement and urged officials to select outstanding university graduates to train for grass-roots positions.

In 2018, the Communist Party’s Organisation Department issued new guidelines to strengthen and improve the xuandiaosheng programme, calling for more targeted recruitment, as well as stricter training and management of recruits.

The party describes the programme as a sourcing project for “building a high-calibre, professional cadre”, according to its official website.

Entry requirements are stricter than for regular civil service recruitment and vary by region, as the central government and local governments run their own xuandiaosheng recruitment programmes.

But they generally require applicants to be party members and outstanding student leaders, and favour local universities or well-known institutions with high global rankings.

For students whose undergraduate degrees did not meet eligibility requirements, earning a further degree from an overseas university on the approved list used to offer a route into the programme. That door seems to have closed.

“From 2024 and 2025 onwards, the programme shifted to recruiting exclusively from mainland Chinese universities, effectively shutting out overseas graduates,” said Jasper Jiang, an adviser who helps overseas students prepare for civil service and public institution recruitment and posts preparation advice on RedNote.

The shift became apparent in late 2024, when provincial-level governments began releasing their 2025 xuandiaosheng recruitment notices.

Of those that had listed overseas universities in their 2023 and 2024 recruitment notices, only Shanghai and Henan continued to accept overseas graduates.

By this year, none did, with some provinces, including Heilongjiang in the northeast, explicitly stating in their recruitment notices that graduates from overseas universities were excluded.

Cleo, who gave only her first name, said she had once been eager to join the government, only to find the key route into government service had already closed to her when she graduated in 2025.

“Overseas-educated graduates are not eligible for the xuandiaosheng programme, leaving me with little choice but to sit the broader national and provincial civil service exams,” said the Hong Kong-educated postgraduate, who ultimately failed to enter the civil service.

Jiang said it had become more difficult for overseas-educated graduates to enter the civil service, partly due to “growing competition from returning graduates for fewer positions” now that the xuandiaosheng programme was off the table.

China has cut its national civil service recruitment for two consecutive years, with positions falling more than 10 per cent – from about 39,700 for the 2025 intake to 35,700 for 2027 – according to Xinhua.

Victor Shih, a professor at the University of California San Diego’s School of Global Policy and Strategy, said national security concerns could be among the reasons behind the restrictions.

“Senior leaders may suspect those who have lived overseas of being sympathetic to foreign perspectives, or worse, of engaging in espionage on behalf of foreign countries,” Shih said.

He said such concerns could affect “both recruitment into the government and promotions”, although there had been exceptions.

Chinese state security authorities identified overseas students among citizens considered vulnerable to recruitment by foreign espionage agencies, according to a post published by Xinhua on its social media account last year.

“The overseas student community has long been a target of foreign espionage and intelligence agencies,” the Ministry of State Security said in a post on its social media account in July 2025. The ministry also identified overseas-based personnel and people with access to classified information as recruitment targets for foreign agencies.

Ja Ian Chong, an associate professor of political science at the National University of Singapore, said competition among major powers could be one reason for the heightened emphasis on national security.

“Competition with the United States and its allies, as well as friction with places such as Europe and Australia, means it may be easier to suspect the sensibilities or even loyalties of people with significant external contact,” Chong said.

He Qiwei, an assistant professor at the University of Edinburgh’s economics school, said “eligibility for the xuandiaosheng programme is itself a valuable opportunity”, as it determined who could compete for a place in the government’s elite talent pipeline.

“The recent restrictions in certain provinces may have implications beyond the immediate recruitment process,” He said.

“They change which groups of graduates have access to this career pathway and could potentially increase the relative attractiveness of domestic education for students interested in government careers.”

While studying abroad was once seen as a route to better jobs and higher pay, the value of an overseas degree appears to have declined in China’s increasingly competitive job market in recent years.

A 2006-07 survey found that returnees from Japan, Canada and Hong Kong earned about twice as much as their locally educated counterparts, according to David Zweig in a 2018 article for the University of Hong Kong’s Centre for Higher Education Research.

By 2015, the premium had narrowed. Returnees with postgraduate degrees earned about 20 per cent more than their domestic counterparts, while overseas bachelor’s degrees offered no significant earnings advantage, according to a 2021 study by Du Zaichao, Sun Yuting, Zhao Guochang and Zweig and published in The China Quarterly.

A 2025 résumé experiment published in China Economic Quarterly, conducted by He Haoran, An Lulu and Lin Jiayue, also found that applicants educated overseas were 16.2 per cent less likely to receive interview feedback than comparable applicants educated in China.
