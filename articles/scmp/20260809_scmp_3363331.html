Myanmar touts economic potential but is it pie in the sky with it still at war?

SCMP | FREE | 1032 words | 2026-08-09

Rina left Myanmar shortly after the 2021 coup as military violence escalated against pro-democracy protesters like herself and the economy of her country, once a Southeast Asian frontier that had flickered with promise, ground to a virtual halt.

Now in Bangkok, the 21-year-old has found work in a cafe, a regular, albeit low-salaried income, the minimum required by the millions of her age group forced to survive outside a domestic economy in ruins, where civil war has killed an estimated 100,000 people in five years.

But her thoughts on who is to blame for the destruction of her country remain unchanged: Min Aung Hlaing, the junta chief who became civilian president this year after elections in the parts of the country the military controls.

He is currently in the Thai capital basking in the welcome of an official visit to Thailand, following trips to China and India that have offered a diplomatic ladder out from isolation since the coup.

In Bangkok, the welcome included a red carpet, honour guard, gala dinner and a chance to offer business leaders a vision of his civil-war-torn nation as a future regional infrastructure and investment hub – rather than the regional basket case his coup created.

“Min Aung Hlaing is the reason we are living here outside our country. The day he is gone – and the military is defeated – we will go back and we will build our country again,” she told This Week in Asia, giving her nickname to avoid any recriminations.

“But we will have to wait. Who knows how long? Maybe it will be three, five or 10 years?”

The realisation that the former army chief – wanted for war crimes at The Hague and sanctioned from the United Kingdom to the United States – is firmly at the helm of an albeit fractured country, has also shaped Thailand’s diplomatic outreach.

Thailand has embarked on what it calls a “calibrated re-engagement’’ with Myanmar, a position it says is inevitable given the long shared border and interests that involve close cooperation, no matter who is in charge in Naypyidaw.

“We have issues every day and need to cooperate … so bilaterally we need to talk, we need to cooperate particularly on issues of border security: the border itself, how to work together to suppress the transnational crimes, scams, drugs,’’ Thai Foreign Minister Sihasak Phuangketkeow told reporters on Thursday evening, adding that air and cross-boundary water pollution were the most pressing issues, referencing toxic run-off that had hit river communities along the Mekong and its tributaries in northwestern Thailand.

While emphasising that Thailand wanted to keep Myanmar pegged to outcomes of a five-point consensus – an Asean bloc peace deal to end violence against civilians and ensure aid gets to the millions of displaced since the coup – Sihasak said there were also economic reasons to extend a hand to Myanmar’s government.

“It’s not just the immediate opportunities,” he told reporters. “Many of the private sector are looking long-term. If Myanmar returns to peace and stability there will be a lot of opportunities, whether in trade and investment.”

While Myanmar’s economy has shown signs of life this year after years of sky-high inflation and negative growth partly caused by a devastating earthquake, experts say it is far from recovering its previous footing which began with political and economic reforms in 2011 – a period of growth abruptly ended by the coup.

“The projects Min Aung Hlaing is pushing with neighbours centre on infrastructure construction, gas development, and energy,” said Htwe Htwe Thein, an associate professor of international business at Curtin University in Australia.

Min Aung Hlaing’s economic plans pivot on highways, railways, dams and power projects, she added, to remodel the war-shattered but strategically important country – pinned between India, China and Thailand – as a manufacturing hub in the heart of Asia’s supply chains.

But “serious obstacles’’ stand in the way of that bright vision, Htwe Htwe Thein added, in a country where the United Nations say 16 million people – nearly a third of the population – need humanitarian relief and foreign investment has collapsed as the war has deepened.

“For one, how do you build infrastructure corridors through active war zones? No serious investor is going to commit capital to projects running through contested territory the government doesn’t fully control,” she said.

“Layer sanctions and the banking restrictions that follow on top of that, and any financing tied to Myanmar comes under heavy scrutiny at every step.”

That leaves economic drivers of jade and gems, tin, copper and rare earths operated by military cronies and shadowy tycoons rather than creating a wider economic base as the likely outcome, she added.

“Real investment has narrowed to resource extraction carried out with little proper due diligence, and also murky financial flows. None of that is capital that builds a manufacturing base or delivers broad-based development.

“It’s extraction and survival financing, not growth.”

While the economic picture uneven at best, Thailand’s renewal of relations has come under scrutiny.

The militaries of Myanmar and Thailand share good relations that have stretched back decades, according to Paul Chambers, an analyst of Thai politics at the University of Oklahoma in the US, adding that Myanmar’s “abominable human rights record’’ has rarely been the deciding factor in their diplomacy.

Myanmar’s diplomatic reintegration in its Asian neighbourhood owed more to military gains of the last 18 months, he said, that were secured in part with Chinese and Russian military aid that had pulled back the patchwork of pro-democracy rebels and ethnic armed groups that at one point threatened to overwhelm the military.

Still, the Association of Southeast Asian Nations is yet to fully readmit Myanmar to its main summits until the five-point consensus is firmly back on track.

Instead, Myanmar’s tip-toe back into the diplomatic fold, represented by this week’s Bangkok trip – after Beijing and New Delhi – reflects the national interests of its neighbouring countries, according to Richard Horsey, Crisis Group’s senior Asia adviser.

“China, India, and Thailand are taking on real reputational risk by doing so, and the rest of the world is watching,” he added.
