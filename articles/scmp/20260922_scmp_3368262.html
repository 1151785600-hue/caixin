The US-China tech war has evolved since the last Xi-Trump summit. Who has the edge now?

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1761 words | 2026-09-22

When the presidents of the United States and China meet in Washington, the world will be watching to see whether their two countries can make good on their agreement to work towards “constructive strategic stability”. In the fourth part of this series, Sylvia Ma looks at the economic and technological levers available to both in their protracted rivalry.

For years, Huawei Technologies has served as a central battleground in the contest between the world’s two largest economies. As Washington tightens restrictions, the Chinese tech giant continues to find new ways forward – to the point that “becoming Nvidia” is now an internal goal.

Days before President Xi Jinping’s trip to the United States for a high-stakes meeting with his American counterpart Donald Trump, Huawei announced a major technological breakthrough: a system that it said was capable of enabling up to 1 million processors to operate as a single, unified computer.

The announcement followed earlier semiconductor advances, namely the Tau Scaling Law and LogicFolding architecture, which aim to deliver high-performance chips without relying on the most advanced lithography tools, whose sale to China remains restricted.

Ahead of the Xi-Trump summit, Huawei rotating chair Xu Zhijun last week voiced a widely shared hope among entrepreneurs that the world’s two superpowers could “coexist and get along in a peaceful way” and that government actions would avoid interfering with normal, innovation-driven market competition.

“With increasing geopolitical tension, those days [of globalisation and full competition] are leaving us,” Xu said, asserting that enterprises were now trapped in a reality where “you cannot buy what you want to, you cannot sell what you want”.

As geoeconomic competition continues to reshape US-China relations, the sources of leverage available to Washington and Beijing are also coming under scrutiny.

Four months after Trump visited the Chinese capital in May, when both sides agreed to work towards “constructive strategic stability”, traditional economic levers, such as pledges to order Boeing aircraft, have almost flatlined. At the same time, the technological contest has accelerated, spanning China’s push to catch up in artificial intelligence and semiconductors and Washington’s drive for self-reliance in rare earths – one of Beijing’s main trump cards.

“The more interesting change is that both sides appear increasingly conscious that their leverage is reciprocal,” said Matteo Giovannini, a non-resident associate fellow at the Centre for China and Globalisation. “Neither side has a decisive advantage without imposing significant costs on itself.”

Chasing the AI frontier

Since May, Chinese AI developers have increasingly sought to combine powerful open-weight models, whose parameters are made available for developers to download, modify and run, with a broader domestic hardware base to reduce reliance on Nvidia chips.

When Moonshot AI launched its 2.8-trillion-parameter Kimi K3 in July, it made the weights available to developers. The model was soon adapted to run on Alibaba Group Holding’s Zhenwu M890 and Huawei Technologies’ Ascend 910C chips, while new models from Z.ai, also known as Zhipu AI, and Alibaba – owner of the South China Morning Post – have followed.

Paul Triolo, a partner and technology policy lead at DGA-Albright Stonebridge Group, said the release of such “capable models” had “arguably” narrowed the gap at the frontier.

Although the US still leads in proprietary frontier models, Washington has increasingly shifted its focus to how Chinese developers gain access to those capabilities. A report released by three US government agencies earlier this month accused six Chinese AI firms of using “distillation” – a technique in which a smaller system is trained on responses generated by a more powerful one – to extract capabilities from American-developed models.

China’s Ministry of Commerce rejected the allegations of “aggressive, malicious” AI distillation as groundless, warning that Beijing would retaliate if Washington weaponised the claim to stifle the country’s tech sector.

Elly Rostoum, a senior fellow with the tech policy programme at the Centre for European Policy Analysis (CEPA), disagreed that China’s progress could be explained solely by distillation, calling such claims “short-sighted”.

“Even if they distil it, they are smart enough to take the technology, distil it and build on it within six weeks – in such a short time – as we’ve seen with Moonshot AI,” she told a media briefing last week.

Meanwhile, Joe Khawam, managing director of legal and AI policy at the Law Reform Institute in Washington, said that while US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and other officials had weighed sanctions over distillation, some policymakers could favour a diplomatic solution given the risk of Chinese retaliation.

“One of the big questions is how truly important distillation is for specific Chinese AI companies to maintain their position as fast followers in frontier AI models,” he said.

Yao Xu, secretary general of the Centre for Global AI Innovative Governance at Fudan University, said the two countries held leverage in different parts of the AI technology ecosystem. “The United States has the chips, but lacks effective planning and public persuasion,” he said.

“China can do better at planning and securing public trust in policy, but we lack the chips.”

The battle over advanced semiconductors

Although the US retains a major advantage in cutting-edge chips, Chinese players have spent the past four months bolstering the domestic hardware ecosystem underpinning the country’s AI models.

“The US has a lot of leverage around ... [the] ecosystem for advanced chips,” Kevin Allison, a senior fellow with the tech policy programme at CEPA, told the same media briefing. “China has yet to catch up, although it’s been making progress kind of under pressure from US export controls.”

Unable to access the machines capable of extreme ultraviolet lithography – the cutting-edge chipmaking technology dominated by Dutch giant ASML – Chinese companies such as Huawei and Alibaba’s T-Head division are pursuing alternative ways to improve performance.

Huawei, for instance, anticipates a major domestic shift towards its AI infrastructure for model training next year, as its Ascend processors gain ground on Nvidia in China despite supply bottlenecks.

Washington continues to bar Chinese firms from accessing the most advanced Nvidia chips, even after approving sales of the H200 model, and has yet to close loopholes that allow remote access to processing power hosted in overseas data centres.

In early August, Bloomberg reported that US regulators were reviewing how Chinese AI companies accessed Nvidia chips through computing power rented overseas.

“While the US still holds the leading-edge chip advantage, it is now treating it as a bargainable asset rather than a hard wall,” said Alicia Garcia-Herrero, chief economist for the Asia-Pacific region and the Middle East at French investment bank Natixis.

Alex Capri, a senior lecturer at the National University of Singapore’s business school, said he expected the coming Xi-Trump talks to renew interest in Beijing’s push to secure relief from export controls on high-end chips and lithography equipment. That could include Washington extending its suspension of the “50 per cent affiliates rule”, which would otherwise expand tech export restrictions to overseas subsidiaries that are at least 50 per cent Chinese-owned. The suspension is set to expire in November.

As foreign memory-chip giants increasingly devote production capacity to high-bandwidth memory, which supports AI computing, Chinese manufacturers such as Yangtze Memory Technologies (YMTC) and ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT) have continued expanding their presence in conventional memory, strengthening China’s domestic memory supply chain.

Data released by Counterpoint Research last month showed that YMTC had entered the world’s top three NAND suppliers by shipment volume for the first time, accounting for 14 per cent of global bit shipments.

Leverage beyond rare earths

Beyond AI models and chips, both the US and China have increased their use of restrictions and export controls across technology and other supply chains, creating additional sources of potential leverage.

On the American side, the regulatory dragnet is expanding rapidly into downstream hardware.

The Federal Communications Commission expanded its Covered List in July to include foreign-made advanced robotic devices and connected power inverters. It is also reportedly drafting a ban on new Chinese-made optical transceivers used in data centres, with officials aiming to publish and implement the measure later this year.

Analysts at Bernstein described the recent US ban on new foreign-made mobile robots as “the formal beginning of US-China decoupling in robotics,” in a note published last month.

Although China’s robotics industry remained reliant on US AI chips and simulation platforms, it had moved faster in scaling training datasets and acquiring high-value data, the analysts said. Data would consequently be Beijing’s “base case” response, with rare earth magnets potentially serving as an escalation lever, they added.

China accounts for about 60 per cent of global rare earth mining and nearly 90 per cent of processing, according to the Washington-based Centre for Strategic and International Studies. As the world’s leading producer of rare earth elements, it has long used critical minerals as a strategic lever in its protracted trade conflict with the US.

Washington has accelerated efforts to reduce its rare earth dependence, with Elon Musk earlier this month touting the Cybercab’s rare-earth-free motor, although analysts regarded the development as only a slight step towards meaningfully weakening China’s leverage across industries.

At the federal level, USA Rare Earth secured access to up to US$1.6 billion in US government funding in June to build out a domestic mine-to-magnet supply chain – though Rostoum of CEPA said China had “a 20-year head start in going around the world” on a “shopping spree” to secure critical minerals.

“The Chinese definitely have the upper hand when it comes to rare earths,” she told the media briefing last week.

Beyond rare earths, analysts also pointed to China’s ability to leverage its position in global manufacturing and supply chains.

“As the US seeks to de-risk from China, Beijing retains considerable leverage through the depth and scale of its industrial ecosystem, something that is much harder to replicate quickly than individual supply chains,” said Giovannini, also a fellow at the University of Hong Kong’s Asia Global Institute.

“The key question ahead of the meeting is therefore less about which side has more leverage and more about which side is willing to bear greater economic costs in exercising it.”
