Xi-Trump Washington summit aimed at putting US-China ties on equal footing: Wang Yi

SCMP | FREE | 1416 words | 2026-09-26

Chinese President Xi Jinping’s visit to the United States was crucial to maintaining the overall stability of China-US relations and aimed at putting the relationship on an equal footing.

Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi, who accompanied Xi on the three-day trip to Washington, gave the assessment on Saturday, expanding on relations between the two countries.

According to state news agency Xinhua, Wang said Xi’s visit “maintained the overall stability of Sino-US relations” and “will have a deep impact on the world’s peace and development”.

The Chinese foreign minister also expanded on one of the eight outcomes of the trip – an agreement between the two leaders to build a “constructive China-US relationship of strategic stability based on respect, fairness and reciprocity”.

“It means the two countries have equal sovereignty and no one is more superior than the other. None is domineering or abusive and they should resolve their own concerns on an equal footing,” Wang said.

“Respect” referred to respect for each other’s sovereignty, territory and core interests, while “fairness” meant rejection of the “zero-sum mindset”, he added.

Wang said the exceptional reception given to Xi and his wife Peng Liyuan, including the personal welcome US President Donald Trump and his wife Melania gave on the tarmac on arrival, reflected respect for Xi and the importance of China-US relations to the United States.

After the trip, China and the United States issued separate statements about the outcomes, saying they had agreed to improve cooperation on artificial intelligence, tariffs and chemical precursor control.

The two countries agreed that their leaders would also support each other in hosting the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum and Group of 20 summits later this year, signalling their intent to attend the meetings in person.

The agreement means that Trump plans to travel to the southern Chinese city of Shenzhen in November, while Xi is scheduled to go to Miami in December, making this year unusual in terms of the number of face-to-face meetings between the two men.

Also unusual was the mention in both statements about the leaders’ references to the US-China alliance during World War II, a partnership that Xi and Trump highlighted several times during the trip.

Xi, also accompanied by chief of staff Cai Qi and Vice-Premier He Lifeng, left Beijing for Washington on Wednesday and returned to the Chinese capital on Saturday afternoon.

The Chinese statement about the trip said both sides agreed to open a communication channel on incidents involving “artificial intelligence”. The US statement said they agreed to rename AI as “super intelligence”, a campaign Trump has been pushing in recent weeks.

The two leaders also formally endorsed a US$30 billion reciprocal tariff reduction arrangement reached earlier by the Chinese and US trade teams, as well as an extension of their trade truce.

The two countries affirmed the establishment of the Board of Investment. The US said the board would discuss “potential investment opportunities and investment-related impediments”.

On geopolitics, both countries said they agreed that Iran should not have nuclear weapons and that no country or institution should impose tolls on international waterways.

They also lauded the arrest in August of 21 Chinese citizens for making and distributing precursor chemicals for illicit drug producers in North America, saying the action was the fruit of China-US cooperation on fentanyl.

While neither statement mentioned Taiwan, a sticking point of bilateral relations, Wang said the issue was brought up during the meetings.

“President Xi Jinping set out China’s principled position on the Taiwan issue, stressing that China is firmly committed to safeguarding national reunification and territorial integrity,” Wang said.

“[Xi] urged the US side to maintain the correct position of opposing ‘Taiwan independence’, handle the Taiwan issue prudently, and remove obstacles to China-US strategic cooperation and the development of bilateral relations.”

In a veiled reference to Japan, Wang said Xi also urged the US to share the “responsibility to uphold the post-war international order and prevent the revival of militarism”, with this year marking the 80th anniversary of the start of the Tokyo war crimes trials.

There were also differences between the statements from the two countries, which has become the norm in recent years since they stopped issuing joint statements.

The US said China had agreed to import at least 10 million tonnes of coal from the United States next year and again in 2028 – a commitment that Beijing did not refer to.

The US also said the two sides would “continue to work on” US concerns about China’s exports of rare earths and critical minerals to the US, “with the goal of ensuring shipment levels return to appropriate levels”. Again, the Chinese statement was silent on the issue.

In addition, the White House said the next meeting of the newly formed US-China Super Intelligence Dialogue would take place by November.

China’s Ministry of Commerce confirmed on Thursday that its senior trade negotiators had already held their first talks with the US on artificial intelligence.

One of the biggest differences was over the American presence in Asia.

Although the US highlighted its alliance with China during WWII, it nevertheless implied that it would remain a force in the region, which Beijing has repeatedly condemned as US hegemony.

“Since World War II, the United States’ forward military presence has preserved a free and open Indo-Pacific, contributing to generations of American prosperity,” the US statement said.

The Chinese statement instead highlighted potential improvements in military-to-military communication.

“The Chinese and US militaries agreed to sign as soon as possible a memorandum of understanding on strengthening crisis communication and prevention,” it said.

In the meantime, Iran continued to wait for an official US response to its proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz in exchange for the US ending its sanctions.

The proposal was made at the UN General Assembly and conveyed to the Americans via Qatari mediators, Iran said.

Trump and Xi also discussed concerns about Russia and North Korea, saying the US “advocated” for trilateral arms-control negotiations between the US, Russia, and China.

Wu Xinbo, dean of the Institute of International Studies at Fudan University in Shanghai, said the affirmation of the investment board exceeded expectations as differences within the US had stalled progress.

“The US statement said specifically that the investment board will discuss potential investment opportunities and investment-related impediments, and that is going towards the direction that China has hoped for. And the work of this board is one thing we need to look out for,” Wu said.

He said the historical alliances during WWII mentioned by both sides was particularly interesting and it was not expected, especially as Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi spoke to Trump in New York before Xi’s visit.

“Trump is different from [his predecessor Joe] Biden. Biden has a strong ideological orientation and when he talked about these things, he would certainly talk about the shared values of the United States and its allies – things like democracy,” Wu said.

“Trump is a nationalist, so he has a strong memory of Japan’s attack on Pearl Harbour. You can see that he likes to bring up Pearl Harbour from time to time. So for him, as a nationalist, there is nothing unusual about talking about World War II.”

Chen Qi, professor of international relations at Tsinghua University, said there was room for further discussion on trade and hopes for trade relations to stabilise with the Apec and G20 meetings later this year.

“The trade truce extension until January will [also] give more room for imagination for the rest of the world,” Chen said.

“When they are meeting so frequently, in November and December, do you think it is possible to upend the trade relationship and reopen the door to additional tariffs? I don’t think there is any room for that,” he said.

Daniel Kritenbrink, a former US assistant secretary of state for East Asian and Pacific affairs, said the frequent meetings between the two leaders this year meant there was less pressure for deliverables during this trip.

“I think we have to keep in mind that we are in this extraordinary period right now where two presidents are meeting so frequently that I think the amount of pressure on each interaction is slightly less than would traditionally be the case,” said Kritenbrink, now a partner at Washington-based strategic advisory firm The Asia Group.
