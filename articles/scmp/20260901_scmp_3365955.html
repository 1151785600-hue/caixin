‘Aloof’ but engaged: what India’s SCO comeback reveals

SCMP | FREE | 1143 words | 2026-09-01

Two years ago, Narendra Modi skipped the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation’s (SCO) summit in Astana entirely, sending his foreign minister instead.

That absence was redressed first at last year’s summit in Tianjin and again this week in Kyrgyzstan, where India’s prime minister stood shoulder to shoulder with China’s Xi Jinping, Russian President Vladimir Putin and the leaders of other SCO member states.

Analysts say New Delhi’s revived engagement with the 10-member bloc reflects both its hedging against an evermore unpredictable United States and its desire to counterbalance China’s influence at the SCO – even if they expect it to remain a “somewhat aloof member”.

Founded in 2001 by China, Russia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan and Uzbekistan, the SCO was originally envisaged as a vehicle for Eurasian security cooperation, expanding over the years to include Pakistan, Iran and Belarus as full members, with a wider circle of Asian and Middle Eastern countries as observers and partners.

India joined in 2017, but it has never been an entirely comfortable fit. China is widely seen as the bloc’s dominant force, bankrolling regional trade and infrastructure projects at a scale no other member can match, while the presence of Pakistan, Delhi’s arch-rival, serves as a perpetual irritant.

Still, Modi arrived in the Kyrgyz capital of Bishkek on Sunday to attend the 26th SCO summit, a two-day gathering concluding on Tuesday, where he was expected to address fellow heads of state on regional stability, counterterrorism and economic cooperation.

‘Anti-Western’ aversion

Before last year’s summit in Tianjin, there were signs that Delhi was downgrading its engagement with the bloc, said Chietigj Bajpaee, a senior research fellow for South Asia at British think tank Chatham House – citing not only Modi’s 2024 absence but India’s last-minute decision to make the meeting it hosted in 2023 virtual.

“This reflects India’s aversion to being part of any organisation that is regarded as overtly anti-Western,” Bajpaee said.

That aversion had not simply disappeared, he said, but improved ties with China and strained relations with a US that had imposed steep trade tariffs and pressured India not to buy Russian oil helped motivate Modi’s move.

It also came with a price tag, of sorts. “Delhi’s renewed support for the SCO likely came in exchange for Beijing’s support for India’s Brics chairmanship this year,” Bajpaee said, pointing to the next Brics summit, scheduled to be held in Delhi from September 12-13.

The two Asian giants, both members of Brics and the SCO, have been gradually normalising ties – opening new military hotlines, resuming direct flights and easing visa restrictions – after the deadly border clashes in 2020 that froze relations for years.

Still, “Delhi prefers to project an image as a non-Western rather than an anti-Western power”, Bajpaee said, noting India’s parallel push for deeper ties with the West.

The result would likely be India remaining “a somewhat aloof member of the SCO”, he said – engaged, but not fully at home.

Yet Delhi’s presence is also what keeps the SCO from becoming dominated by Beijing, according to Ivan Lidarev, a political scientist and research fellow at the National University of Singapore’s Institute of South Asian Studies.

An SCO without India might solidify into a Chinese sphere of influence

Ivan Lidarev, political scientist

“An SCO without India might solidify into a Chinese sphere of influence in Eurasia to India’s detriment and turn the organisation into an anti-US bloc,” he said, adding that this concern was part of why Russia pushed for India’s admission in the first place.

For both Beijing and Moscow, the SCO served as a way of keeping Delhi close and limiting how far it drifted towards Washington, Lidarev said, while enabling the slow accumulation of trust.

“The SCO has acted as a giant confidence-building measure between Delhi and Beijing,” he said. “It has committed the two to the same vision of multipolarity, with an emphasis on the Global South.”

Modi’s appearance in Bishkek serves another, more immediate purpose: paving the way for Xi’s possible attendance at this month’s Brics summit in Delhi.

“A no-show by Xi will be a blow to the summit, particularly in light of Modi’s participation in last year’s Tianjin SCO summit and to the thaw in China-India relations,” Lidarev said, adding it could also complicate next year’s Brics summit that China is set to host.

There is a message for Washington embedded in all of this, too.

India’s push to strengthen the “non-Western side” of its multi-alignment foreign policy strategy amounted to “a careful and increasingly difficult international balancing act”, Lidarev said – one intended to subtly remind the US that India had other options, at a time when trade talks between the two countries appeared to have hit a wall.

Diminishing returns?

Other observers cast doubt on the continued utility of SCO membership for India, with Shanthie Mariet D’Souza, founder and president of the Mantraya Institute for Strategic Studies in Goa, seeing an organisation that offers the country less and less.

“China’s growing dominance and the organisation’s consensus-based structure limit New Delhi’s ability to shape its political and security agenda, particularly on issues such as terrorism and regional conflicts,” she said.

But she stopped short of calling for India’s withdrawal, noting that the SCO was one of the few forums where India can sit across the negotiating table from China, Russia and the Central Asian states all at once.

“Its value is therefore increasingly tactical,” D’Souza said – giving Delhi a venue to raise concerns about terrorism as its presence prevented the bloc becoming an “exclusively China-Russia sphere”.

“India may no longer expect major geopolitical outcomes from the SCO, but it cannot afford to abandon it,” she added.

Modi’s regional diplomacy did not begin in Bishkek. He spent two days in Uzbekistan first, on a state visit from Saturday to Sunday, meeting President Shavkat Mirziyoyev and formally elevating ties to a comprehensive strategic partnership.

The visit yielded increased Indian access to Uzbek uranium and critical minerals, and a bilateral pledge to push two-way trade past US$5 billion by 2030.

That stop was no accident of scheduling, according to Balaji Chandramohan, a foreign-policy analyst and research scholar at the University of Madras.

He said the SCO allowed India to reaffirm its commitment to Central Asia and the Middle East at a time of considerable global disruption, shoring up both regional connectivity and its own energy security.

“India will expand its strategic outreach in the Central Asian region as a part of its extending Look West and Look North Policy,” he said, referring to Delhi’s parallel diplomatic strategies for deepening ties with West and Central Asia as extensions of its own neighbourhood.
