Angkor Wat marathon: 6,500 runners race through Cambodia’s ancient Khmer empire

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1599 words | 2026-09-09

Under the cloak of darkness, runners gather in their hundreds at the entrance to the Angkor Wat temple complex, in Cambodia’s Siem Reap. They wait in groups, stretching muscles to pumping house music while a little way off, apsara dancers and traditional Khmer musicians help lift the early-morning energy.

Volunteers, many of whom have been at the site since 2am, hand out bottles of water to a crowd of runners and spectators that started arriving at 3.30am, ahead of the staggered starts of the 11th Angkor Empire Marathon.

From a small stage framed by the stone carvings of mythical Nagas at the main-road end of the causeway that crosses the moat to Angkor Wat, two emcees shout out motivational phrases in Khmer and English.

As the clock ticks down to 4.30am and the start of the first of four races – the full, 42km (26 miles) marathon – pacers make their way to the starting line, competitive racers cramming in behind, waiting for the klaxon to sound. With minutes to go, dignitaries deliver speeches from a stage, wishing competitors luck, thanking sponsors and praising the event’s success over the years.

“Angkor Wat always has such a special vibe, so it will be exciting to run among all of the ancient temples,” says Christine Hellington, 32, an Australian who recently moved from Cambodia to Vietnam and is competing in her first marathon. “I’m hoping the atmosphere will help push me through, so I can actually finish it.”

National Olympic Committee of Cambodia (NOCC) president Thong Khon, Chinese ambassador Wang Wenbin and NOCC secretary general Vath Chamroeun together light a torch with a blazing flame to officially open the event, then the klaxon sounds and the runners set off towards Siem Reap city, roared on by spectators young and old, who are lining the road.

For many competitors, such as Hellington (who did manage to complete the 42km, in five hours and 34 minutes), this marathon’s appeal isn’t just about finishing. Unlike conventional city races, in which competitors run in the shadow of skyscrapers and urban landmarks, here, runners spend much of the course among the temples, settlements and reservoirs that once formed the heart of the Khmer Empire (802-1431).

According to Thong Khon, the 11th Angkor Empire Marathon (which took place on August 2) saw a 25 per cent increase in the number of competitors compared with last year. Of the 6,500 total, 1,050 were international runners hailing from 51 countries, with the bulk having come from China, Japan and Britain.

Over the two days before the races, entrants were required to visit the Royal Angkor Resort, on the outskirts of Siem Reap, to collect their race gear: their race number – mine was 7008; a T-shirt; a drawstring bag; and a lanyard. Here, the competitive spirit was already being stirred, with pre-race activities ranging from football target practice to mini runs, as well as games and freebies dished out by sponsors. Volunteers and officials were in attendance to offer advice and encouragement.

At 4.30am on the day itself, 1,000 people set off on the full marathon. At 5.30am, another 1,000 took on the half-marathon, and the 10km (six miles) attracted 3,000 runners, who set off at 5.40am. The final 1,500 took part in a 3km (1.9 miles) fun run, and set off at 5.50am. Each route started and ended on the road at the entrance to the Angkor Wat complex.

The 42km route led deep into the Unesco-crowned Angkor Archaeological Park, passing Ta Prohm – the temple featured in the 2001 film Lara Croft: Tomb Raider – and sacred Srah Srang, a vast reservoir known as the “Royal Bath”. The runners pushed through the ancient capital of Angkor Thom, home to the multifaced Bayon temple, and past some of the park’s lesser-known places of worship, including East Mebon, Pre Rup, Banteay Kdei, Prasat Top and Ta Keo, all of which took on an unearthly aspect in the mist. With a section through Siem Reap city and another through a traditional village, Preah Dak, where the smell of num banh chok – the Khmer breakfast dish of rice noodles – wafted through the air, it’s easy to see how the race made it onto Forbes Travel Guide’s “12 Marathons Worth Travelling For in 2026” list, rubbing shoulders with China’s Great Wall Marathon and the Boston Marathon, in the United States.

Clutching his finisher’s medal, Vann Chan Thada seems energised after having completed his second Angkor Empire Marathon, in four hours and 55 minutes.

“The marathon was really challenging with the [searing heat], but it was really exciting with the views on the route that we ran,” says the 25-year-old Cambodian. “Running through the ancient temples gave me a different vibe compared with running in the city. We ran in the early morning under the tree-lined shade, and the sunrise with the temples made us feel like we were running through the golden era of ancient Angkor.”

The 10km race is timed to begin as the sun starts to peek from behind Angkor Wat. In addition to serious athletes, families and groups of friends are taking part, chatting, laughing, stopping to snap selfies and cheer each other on along the way. The 10km is the perfect option, in other words, for beginners such as me.

The seasoned racers storm ahead at the sound of the klaxon, leaving us more casual runners space to take in our surroundings and shoot photos before setting off.

Rays of sunlight pierce a high canopy dripping with cooling condensation along the road from Angkor Wat to Angkor Thom’s South Gate. Mist hovers above waterways and the silhouettes of temples suddenly emerge from the treetops, creating a whimsical atmosphere that feels as though it’s been lifted from a fairy tale.

Dawn is also when nature springs to life, the treetops alive with birdsong and the faint morning call of gibbons. Unperturbed by the pounding feet of thousands of runners, mischievous macaques join spectators on the sidelines, snatching discarded water bottles from the road with which to rehydrate.

Crossing the finish line to energising cheers in one hour and 34 minutes leaves me just enough time to compose myself before English expat Jim Thompson, 46 and dripping in sweat, does the same.

“I’ve run before in very uninspiring places, but the key difference is that this race has the temples of Angkor Wat as a sublime backdrop to your achievement of running,” says Thompson, who is no stranger to the experience, having competed in the Angkor Wat International Half Marathon in 2013 and 2014. The longer-established half marathon is a separate NOCC event that takes place around the temples in December each year.

“The setting in ancient Angkor Wat is the No 1 highlight; the way that the temples slowly emerge from the darkness into the dawn, and all the while this beautiful mist appears in the sunlight, raining back down onto the runners. All of the other participants were a definite highlight, too, spurring you on to the finish.”

The Angkor Empire Marathon was launched in 2014 as an international event designed to promote Siem Reap and the Angkor Archaeological Park as a destination for sports tourism while contributing to charitable and community initiatives.

This year’s marathon came at a challenging time for Cambodia’s tourism sector, which is struggling to rebuild international visitor numbers after the Covid-19 pandemic. Figures from the Ministry of Tourism reveal the nation welcomed 1.75 million international arrivals in the first half of 2026, marking a 47.9 per cent year-on-year decline, the dramatic drop attributed to the closure of land borders with Thailand due to the ongoing conflict between the two nations; the Middle East war and its impact on airfares; and the negative publicity regarding online scam centres operating in Cambodia.

Angkor Enterprise recorded a 31.69 per cent drop in ticket sales for the Angkor Archaeological Park during the same period, highlighting the importance of finding new ways to draw visitors to Siem Reap.

The return of thousands of runners to Angkor Wat offered a welcome boost, a renewed energy having been injected into the city during the race weekend. Restaurants and bars were bustling and shopkeepers engaged in lively bartering to sell their wares.

Tuk-tuk driver Mr Chai secured a steady flow of customers throughout the marathon weekend, he says: “Business has been very quiet lately, so it has been good to be busy and see so many people at the temples again.”

Furthermore, proceeds from the Angkor Empire Marathon and Angkor Wat International Half Marathon directly benefit children’s hospitals, landmine victims and disability support organisations, and the Cambodian Red Cross, among other humanitarian initiatives.

Athletic pursuits in the days of the Khmer Empire – as depicted in bas-relief on the walls of Angkor Wat – served a dual purpose, being a form of military preparation for soldiers as well as a major element of social entertainment. Likewise, today’s Angkor Wat runs are bifunctional: helping to revitalise the local tourism industry while raising funds for charitable causes.

The entry fee for the full marathon for international competitors this year was US$75.
