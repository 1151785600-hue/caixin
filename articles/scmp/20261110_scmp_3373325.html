How Dong Mingzhu, China’s manufacturing ‘iron lady’, is maintaining her cool in the AI era

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 3149 words | 2026-11-10

Dong Mingzhu chairs Gree Electric Appliances, a home-appliance giant based in southern China’s Guangdong province. Having worked her way up from a frontline sales job, she is one of China’s best-known figures in manufacturing. As a deputy to the National People’s Congress, she has long advocated for manufacturing innovation, higher incomes and greener consumption.

In this interview, conducted when she was named an inaugural “Cool Leader” at an assembly of the UN Environment Programme’s Cool Coalition in Singapore, Dong discusses Chinese companies’ overseas expansion, how automation transforms manufacturing and her management philosophy.

This summer, Chinese-made air conditioners helped Europeans cope with unprecedented heat. As the world faces growing climate risks, how is Gree expanding access to cooling while reducing its environmental impact?

In a quick response to the European market, we shipped air conditioners using R290, a propane refrigerant with low global warming potential, as well as portable units that require no installation. Both were developed with European Union regulations and local installation constraints in mind. When demand surged, retail sales of our air conditioners in certain markets, including France, rose 50 per cent year on year.

Of course, some of that growth may have been temporary, driven by the immediate need.

But fundamentally, climate change has become a more urgent problem. It threatens human survival and we have to address it. Even if someone can afford to run an air conditioner around the clock, there is a wider cost: we cool the room indoors while releasing heat outdoors. As populations grow, that pressure increases.

Ideally, cooling would use no energy at all. That is why we need technological breakthroughs.

We are working to cut energy use and reduce reliance on conventional power. For example, our solar-powered air conditioning can draw on clean electricity when the sun shines, while storage lets us save surplus power generated during the day for use at night.

We’ve established an internal framework for carbon neutrality, with targets for each year. We’re also working on energy-efficiency standards and making every stage of our products’ life cycle greener, from design and manufacturing to recycling. We have a national-level key laboratory focused on energy-efficient air-conditioning equipment and systems, as well as a dedicated research division for new-energy technologies.

There is more to explore. Can we harness heat in the air as a useful energy source? Can we cool a room to the temperature people want without adding heat to the air outside?

Gree has pursued greener cooling for more than a decade. We first proposed a design for powering air conditioners directly with solar electricity in 2012 and developed a working system in 2013.

Our solar-plus-storage range now spans more than 10 product categories, from centrifugal chillers integrating solar generation, storage and flexible use of direct-current power to screw chillers and refrigeration units.

These products can be used in different venues such as factories, shopping centres, offices and cold-storage facilities, and have reached more than 35 countries and regions.

If making money is not your only purpose, you keep asking harder questions and working to solve them. I want Gree to use its capabilities to make the world a better place.

Gree was among the first Chinese companies to expand overseas. In 2015, you came up with the slogan “Let the World Fall in Love with Made in China”. Now, more than a decade later, what has changed in the relationship between Chinese manufacturers and global markets?

The biggest change is that consumers increasingly recognise the quality of Chinese-made products. Chinese travellers may often spot Gree’s name on a building overseas, and local consumers will say they like our air conditioners.

At the core is that Chinese companies are moving from exporting products to building brands overseas.

For years, Chinese home-appliance makers competed globally mainly as original design manufacturers [known as white labelling]. Their share of global manufacturing grew, but their own brands had a limited presence in overseas retail markets.

That is changing. The global market share held by Chinese home-appliance brands rose from 2.9 per cent in 2015 to 10.9 per cent in 2025. Gree now sells to more than 190 countries and regions, with its own brands accounting for over 70 per cent of export sales.

Geopolitics and trade relations are growing more complex, with armed conflicts in some regions. What new challenges does this pose for Chinese companies expanding overseas?

Everyday consumer products should not be dragged into political conflicts. Whatever their skin tone or language, people pursue a better life and should have access to good products.

For Gree, the answer is to keep making the world’s best air conditioners, whatever happens around us. I do not have a separate playbook for overseas markets. I have never drawn a sharp line between domestic and international markets; China is part of the global market too.

What matters more is what consumers need. Europeans need heating in winter, for example. How can we make it more environmentally friendly? Oil-filled radiators and gas heating can come with high equipment and maintenance costs. If we can offer efficient, stand-alone heating, we can reduce the environmental impact and lower consumers’ bills. Whether it sells well, the numbers will tell.

That does not mean the challenges are not real. Tariff barriers are becoming more entrenched and complex. Building a brand overseas requires a deeper understanding of local needs, while swings in distributors’ inventories and regional conflicts can directly affect demand. We are still finding our way through these issues.

Gree carefully selects local business partners. If the market demand is massive, it is possible to establish local manufacturing plants.

Many companies are making acquisitions overseas, but a business cannot grow by just buying up assets. It is like a building: you need a solid foundation first. For us, that means making reliable products consumers want. Once the foundation is sound, you can build on it. I am not interested in playing a numbers game.

Gree’s 2026 interim report showed a decline in overseas revenue and cited the impact of setbacks in the Middle East. How do you view the drop?

The war has indeed affected the market. It would be unrealistic to say that “we go in regardless and avoid any decline.” We have to face the situation as it is, but it will not change our commitment to developing better technology and products for consumers.

Can anyone go through life without catching a cold? Sometimes you may even end up in hospital. What matters is whether you recover. Companies, communities, countries and societies all face difficulties as they develop. The important thing is to confront them.

Gree has been diversifying its revenue sources beyond air conditioners. Which sector do you see as the next growth engine?

We would all love to become a trillion-yuan company overnight, but you have to build a business step by step.

Gree has grown from an air-conditioning maker into a global technology company serving both consumer and industrial markets. Industrial products, intelligent equipment and green energy are emerging as new growth drivers.

In the first half of this year, revenue from our intelligent equipment business rose 19 per cent [to about 374 million yuan (US$56.05 million)], sales from computer-controlled machine tools and industrial robots rose 20 per cent. Our self-developed high-speed dual five-axis gantry machining centres are now being supplied in volume to several new-energy vehicle makers.

In the green energy sector, our silicon carbide business was up 200 per cent year on year. Our silicon carbide power devices are already in mass production for home appliances, and expanding into solar-plus-storage systems and charging stations.

Whether we’re improving our core products or entering new markets, we develop the key technologies ourselves. Over time, that builds capabilities that are hard to replicate. We’re focused on technical depth and a more independent supply chain, not scale for its own sake.

China’s consumer demand is under pressure, and Gree cannot be immune to that. How do you find the consumer market now? How have shopping patterns changed in recent years?

I think the market is just taking an afternoon nap. That’s all.

China’s real estate downturn has indeed had an impact, but our sector does not rely solely on property development.

Consumers are moving beyond asking whether they have an appliance to asking how good it is. They care more about quality, higher-end products and how those products make them feel. They also want appliances that work together across the home, and artificial intelligence that responds to their needs rather than simply adding features to a spec sheet. Old-for-new trade-in policies have become an important source of support for domestic demand.

That means future growth will come from improving our products’ market fit, not chasing volume for its own sake. Technology must solve real problems – whether that means avoiding cold air blowing directly on people, removing formaldehyde or keeping temperature and humidity steady. We are also expanding beyond air conditioning: sales in the home appliances segment grew more than 20 per cent in the first half of this year.

There is also more we can do in the existing market through equipment upgrades and trade-ins, while pursuing new opportunities in data-centre cooling, high-temperature heat pumps and zero-carbon parks.

In a market increasingly driven by replacement purchases, people will pay more for a better experience, higher energy efficiency and greener products. That is the market foundation for our long-term investment in research and development.

How far has Gree’s push into automation and smart manufacturing gone, what obstacles have you encountered, and what lessons does the journey offer to other Chinese manufacturers?

Intelligent manufacturing has two sides: smarter products and smarter production.

Opening the curtains or turning on the television with a voice command is a sort of progress. But real intelligence means devices can sense what is happening and interact with people. I’m in Singapore now, and I can check my air conditioner, refrigerator and washing machine at home from my phone. If food has been in the fridge too long, it tells me what I need to use soon. Technology like this should make life healthier, easier and more comfortable.

We are now making much more than air conditioners. We also make refrigerators, washing machines, dishwashers and other kitchen appliances. Because we design these products as part of one connected home system from the start, they can work together rather than being pieced together later.

Meanwhile, on the production side, automation can relieve workers of physically demanding, repetitive tasks and help them move from routine operations into skilled technical roles.

We have our own intelligent equipment business – manufacturing equipment that includes industrial robots and computer-controlled machine tools. We handle everything in-house, from design and components to system integration.

Our Jinwan plant in Zhuhai, selected last year as a “pioneer-level” smart factory [the highest tier of China’s national smart-factory programme], shows what this takes. We had to develop core equipment, connect data across R&D, procurement, manufacturing and sales, and move beyond automating individual tasks to coordinating the whole operation. That last step required changes to workflows and management, not just technology.

We made it through a coordination model, which connects design, suppliers, production, quality control, logistics and warehousing on a shared platform.

Twenty years ago, some labelled Chinese manufacturing as cheap and poor quality. That has changed. For China’s move towards smart manufacturing, the lesson is to combine the ability to build core equipment, connect operations digitally and use data across the entire process – and then turn that into a system others can adopt.

There’s no unique challenge. The question is whether you are willing to do the work and risk failure.

Automation frequently sparks public concerns regarding job losses. From a corporate leadership perspective, how do you address these concerns, and what specific programmes or initiatives have been implemented by Gree to upskill, reskill and help its workforce adapt to a technology-driven manufacturing environment?

We have never laid people off because of automation.

People do leave each year, for many reasons. Some return to their hometowns or move to be with a partner. Others want more freedom at work and find manufacturing’s rules and standards are not for them. Some learn a little and decide they are ready to strike out on their own.

Other home-appliance companies regularly poach our people. One company hired 600 to 700 Gree employees in a year, including frontline workers. I came to see that as proof that we train good people. Rather than recruit from companies I consider less capable, I would rather invest in developing young talent.

This year, we opened a Gree technical school. Its teaching team brings together experienced Gree engineers, senior technicians and specialist teachers. The school offers programmes geared towards fields including smart manufacturing, electrical technology and information technology.

In the past, we replaced people as they left; now we do not necessarily fill every opening.

Automation is about training the skilled workers that smart manufacturing needs. The more automated a factory becomes, the more it needs people who can operate and manage its equipment. By combining classroom learning with hands-on training, we want the skills that students acquire to match the technology our factories actually use.

Automation does create a tension, but working through it can make a company stronger. A smart factory that once needed 14,000 people may now need only 2,000. That does not mean I would dismiss 12,000 workers.

Automation has changed the mix of jobs, so workers need to keep learning and upgrading their skills. Employees can move into more skilled roles. Someone who once drove a forklift might manage an automated logistics system instead.

A production line at our Jinwan plant needs just 20 skilled employees, but they must be able to monitor equipment, analyse data and diagnose faults.

Technology replaces repetitive tasks while creating demand for higher-level skills. Workers need to keep learning as jobs change.

We also recruited 5,000 university graduates this year. Many of Gree’s senior and middle managers joined after graduation and grew with the company. That is why they have such a strong connection to the group.

We have always aimed to become a diversified, technology-driven global industrial group. That means more than expanding into new businesses. We want to move beyond selling products to providing technology and helping build an industrial ecosystem. Our technical school is one way we’re developing the people needed to make that happen.

Historically, every major wave of technological innovation has forced companies to transform. How is Gree navigating the current rise of AI?

Before phones could store numbers, I was able to remember 200 or 300 of them. Now I sometimes struggle to recall my own. In that sense, technology can weaken an ability when we stop using it.

Of course technology should advance. But we need to think about where it takes us and how we use it. That is a question for society, not just Gree. Journalists worry that AI will write their articles. If we eventually lose the habit of thinking for ourselves, what is left of us beyond eating and drinking?

Problems are born with solutions. AI may create opportunities in services – for example, if people have more time and money, they may travel more, right?

Every era brings opportunities and setbacks. What matters is how we respond. Gree is changing every day, regardless of what happens outside. Our goal remains simple: make the world’s best products. There is no need to make a grand speech about it.

Listed on the Shenzhen Stock Exchange, Gree is one of the biggest home appliance makers in China, with a market capitalisation of more than 200 billion yuan. But there is a significant disparity in how Gree and some rivals are valued in the capital markets, with price-to-earnings ratios differing. How would you respond?

Someone once told me, “Our company is doing very well – our price-to-earnings ratio is 100.” I was startled. If a valuation is out of line with the business’s underlying assets, shareholders may be the ones who lose out.

Gree should be an honest, steady company. Over the years, we have raised only 5 billion yuan from the stock market and paid shareholders more than 160 billion yuan in dividends. Tell me, is that good or bad?

To build a business that lasts, systems matter, but choosing the right people matters more. A new leader can change the rules. You need someone who will preserve what works and keep improving it. People are crucial.

You started from Haili, Gree’s predecessor, and helped build a small Zhuhai factory into a company that made it to the Fortune Global 500. How do you view your relationship with Gree? Has your own mindset changed over the years? And how do you feel about being called the “Iron Lady” for your decisive, no-nonsense leadership style?

Gree is the soil. But even fertile soil cannot make a weak seed sprout and bear fruit; even the best seed will struggle in poor soil. The individual and the company need each other to grow.

I’m the same as I’ve always been. It’s that simple. Gree is my life.

As for “Iron Lady”, I do not care what people call me. What matters is whether I have done my job well.

You often speak about hard work, responsibility and helping others. Succession is a question facing Gree and many other Chinese companies that grew during the reform era. How do you think about it, and what values do you hope to pass on?

Every company has its own culture, so there is no single answer. Gree’s culture is straightforward: we must keep innovating, and everyone must be loyal to the company. Integrity comes first. The ideal is to have both integrity and ability; ability without integrity can be dangerous. Those values must endure. You cannot set integrity aside when a new generation takes over.

Hard work, responsibility and helping others are not uniquely Chinese values. They matter everywhere. People will see things differently, but we can work through those differences.

Your experience has inspired many young women along the way. What would you say to them?

People come from all walks of life and will have all kinds of different things to say. Just stay true to yourself.
