Black Lake CEO’s strategy to sell AI solutions to factories: turn to food couriers

SCMP | FREE | 1240 words | 2026-07-27

When Zhou Yuxiang, founder and CEO of Shanghai-headquartered Black Lake Technologies, wanted to sell his artificial intelligence (AI) automation solutions to millions of family-run factories across China, he came up with an unconventional strategy: hiring food delivery workers to make sales.

His entire sales team, hired from various industrial cities, survived a gruelling five-day boot camp on articulating how AI can solve specific manufacturing pain points.

“They are very street-smart,” the 36-year-old said in an exclusive interview with the South China Morning Post. “Good college graduates are often thin-skinned. They linger in front of a factory and can’t get in.

“But for food delivery guys, they just give a cigarette to the security guard and get in … They can also bypass guard dogs, or find a back door to get into a factory to meet the owner because they deliver food.”

With a sales force of more than 420 delivery workers, Black Lake, established in 2016, has grown to serve 35,000 factory clients, 95 per cent of which are small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs).

The International Data Corporation also ranks it as mainland China’s top provider of cloud-based production management software.

Hong Kong ‘very attractive’ for next step

This success earned Zhou an invitation to meet Premier Li Qiang in October last year to brief him on how to use agentic AI to digitise the backbone of the mainland’s economy against the backdrop of technological self-reliance as a key goal for the next five-year plan.

To Zhou, the key factor that will determine the country’s competitiveness in manufacturing is how to make nearly 5 million manufacturing SMEs and factories agile, flexible and able to swiftly respond to market demands.

By launching tailored products for them in 2021, what began as a socially responsible initiative has evolved into a highly profitable business with more than 100 per cent annual growth providing cloud-based software-as-a-service.

At the Nusa Dua Forum in Bali organised by the SCMP, Zhou said Black Lake would soon expand into Indonesia, Thailand, Vietnam and the Philippines later this year.

The emerging tech company currently has a reported valuation exceeding 7 billion yuan (US$1 billion). While Zhou remained tight-lipped on whether the business had plans to list in Hong Kong, he acknowledged the allure of the financial hub.

“We’re born in China, but we’re going global. I think capital market-wise, Hong Kong has attracted the smartest and most diverse group of international savvy investors who can appreciate our value, who can see our potential, and their long-term positioning rather than short-term arbitrage,” he said in his first interview with a Hong Kong media outlet.

“If we move to the next stage, I think Hong Kong is a very attractive place.”

He also revealed that Black Lake was collaborating with two universities in Hong Kong on AI and supply chain research, describing scholars in the city as “world-class” and possessing a “global vision and understanding of China”.

From ‘cheap and good’ to on-demand produce

Zhou said that Black Lake was expanding across China, going from its traditional coastal industrial strongholds, including the Yangtze and Pearl River Deltas, into inland provinces such as Sichuan, Hebei and Hubei.

“The booming e-commerce market gave Chinese consumers a much stronger voice on what they want,” the Dartmouth College alumnus said.

A common theme he spotted among small businesses was that they all needed supply chains that could react instantly to address niche, fast-changing demands.

But he noted that such businesses lacked the know-how, while sizeable enterprises had heavily relied on Western players for industrial automation.

“In China, there is a paradigm shift from just making things ‘cheap and good’ to producing based on real-time demand,” he said. “It’s beyond the human brain … That’s why AI technologies and digital tools come into play.”

Among its popular products for SMEs were mini-programs that allow workers to simply scan bar codes in a factory via WeChat to trigger tasks, and wearables to digitise shop floors.

He observed that two more trends accelerated the rapid adoption of AI in mainland manufacturing: the trade war-related restructuring of global supply chains and a rapidly ageing workforce of veteran factory managers.

“We are building AI brains to replace these retiring workers to continue the miracle of the Chinese manufacturing sector,” he said.

Agility in action: two success stories

Those partnered with Black Lake to rewire manufacturing with AI ranged from giant chains to family-run businesses.

One of its largest clients was beverage chain Mixue Bingcheng. In 2018, founder Zhang Hongchao set his sights on overseas expansion but wanted to deviate from the standardised menu model of competitors such as Starbucks.

He required the company to roll out five to six new flavours every month without facing stock-outs on popular items.

By digitising their supply chain – from farm to processing to logistics – with the help of Black Lake, Mixue achieved the agility needed to scale globally. The beverage brand now has nearly 60,000 stores worldwide.

Zhou said another smaller yet telling example was a plastic packaging factory in Suzhou.

Founded in the 1990s, the parents’ generation relied on massive, predictable bulk orders such as lipstick tubes from traditional giants, including L’Oreal and Unilever. But margins were squeezed as cheaper labour emerged in Southeast Asia.

Zhou said that when the factory founder’s daughter took over, she realised that they could not predict the market a year ahead or produce the same thing all year round. She instead pivoted to serve smaller e-commerce cosmetics brands.

Black Lake’s CEO said these brands demanded tiny initial batches, for example, 10,000 units instead of 300,000, but required the factory to ramp up production within five days if a product went viral on TikTok.

By equipping workers with wearable devices guided by real-time data, the factory eliminated reliance on human guesswork for material transfers. They squeezed line-changeover idle times from six hours down to just one hour.

“Previously, the dad only gave the daughter 5 per cent capacity for those agile orders … When we visited them two years ago, 60 per cent of the orders were agile orders,” he said. “If you only produce standardised products, you very quickly lose the market.”

The future: AI’s ‘Google moment’

As mainland companies expand overseas, Zhou argued that agility was even more valuable than improving efficiency, while agentic AI – capable of real-time, data-driven decisions – could also bridge cultural divides.

Despite these advancements, the entrepreneur said he believed the AI industry was only just getting started.

“If we compare AI with the internet, we are still at the ‘Yahoo moment.’ We have chatbots. But how do we put the AI capability into workforce enhancements or replacements? I think there’s still a lot to do,” he said.

He said Black Lake currently had three agentic AI products deployed and seven more in incubation – ranging from converting design files into automated workflows to AI-driven quality assurance and procurement dispatching.

“In certain scenarios, our AI has already achieved 97 per cent reasoning accuracy, successfully replacing roles traditionally done by humans. But there are still 80 per cent of roles left to address,” he said.

“In the next two to three years, the spotlight will move from foundation models to the application layer. That is when everything changes … That will be the ‘Google moment’ for AI.”
