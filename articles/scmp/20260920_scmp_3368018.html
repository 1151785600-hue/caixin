Why China is playing it cool amid Trump’s disruption and churn

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1747 words | 2026-09-20

When the presidents of the United States and China meet in Washington, the world will be watching to see whether their two countries can make good on their agreement to work towards “constructive strategic stability”. In the first part of a series, Mark Magnier looks at expectations in the US for the summit.

Chinese planners have been frustrated for months at the slow speed of decision-making and limited engagement by a distracted US administration as the days tick down to next week’s high-stakes summit between US President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping.

This may advantage Beijing by precluding trade and security concessions that require extensive groundwork, according to former US officials, analysts and people involved in coordinating the visit.

“How are we going to get anything done if we can’t even agree on the basic logistics,” said a person involved in planning. “The Chinese are … not thrilled with it. But as a result, we have ever-shrinking expectations for the visit, and the Chinese are OK with that too.”

Any expectation of a massive soy deal or a large number of Boeing aircraft was a long shot, he added.

“The Chinese are all signalling that their expectations are very low, that there should have been more preparation,” said Bonnie Glaser, managing director with the German Marshall Fund of the United States.

“It’s hard to identify anything that is a sure thing,” she added. “I haven’t heard the term ‘deliverable’ even used.”

But reduced expectations also speak to a US president who disparages planning in favour of in-person deals with his “gut” – and to increasingly frequent presidential summits.

“This is not a relationship where you can expect big numbers or major gift packages being announced any more,” said Yun Sun, China director at the Stimson Centre. “We seem to have unrealistic expectations that these two leaders should come together, sit down, announce the ending of all wars and world peace. That’s just unrealistic.”

While planning has picked up ahead of the first full US-China state visit in Washington since 2015, arrangements have been creaky as the White House juggles the Iran war, rising inflation and Trump’s declining popularity.

“There probably won’t be breakthroughs because Trump’s team is not capable of that,” said Jeremy Chan, senior analyst with the Eurasia Group. “But there also won’t be a breakdown.”

Drawn-out issues include a US$30 billion for US$30 billion, or “30-for-30”, reciprocal tariff reduction deal likely to be a summit cornerstone; extending a trade truce that expires next month; when, where, and on what issues meetings will be held; and who will meet an expected Chinese business delegation.

One impediment has been the small circle of White House decision-makers.

“It’s a bandwidth issue,” the person involved in planning said, citing a reluctance to involve the US president too early.

“You’ve got folks who don’t want to bring Trump into the discussion yet because they think he might land someplace different than they land,” he said. “The longer they put it off, the better people are going to be able to shape a contained visit.”

What each side hopes to achieve

For Trump, the summit is an opportunity to celebrate his power, host a strong leader he admires and bask in the pomp and ceremony he relishes.

“Trump wants a diversion, something showing he is a ‘big guy’ peer and managing China to good effect,” said Kevin Nealer, a partner with The Scowcroft Group consultancy. “My worry is they lack any affirmative agenda. There’s nothing we’re doing together and plenty that divides.”

Xi, meanwhile, can showcase China’s growing clout, solidify gains made on Taiwan and show that Beijing faced down the US on trade and prevailed.

“They want the world to see they can manage Trump and they want the world to say, ‘look, we’re being treated as the equal,” said Ryan Hass, China director at the Brookings Institution and former National Security Council (NSC) official.

“Part of their broader narrative is, we’re the new adults in the room; it’s the US that is the disrupter. They’ve got a lot of reasons that they want this to go right.”

Summit format to mirror Trump’s May trip to Beijing

The summit format will be a meticulous mirroring of Trump’s May trip to Beijing, lasting no more than 48 hours, with no additional stops, speeches or side trips out of the capital. This suits China’s equal footing messaging, analysts say. It also blunts criticism that Chinese business leaders accompanying Xi are outsourcing American jobs, since they are only replicating Trump’s entourage.

A key focus is likely to be the 30-for-30 tariff reduction framework. Washington wants to include US farm exports to appease Trump’s rural farm base hit by higher costs tied to the Iran war and his aggressive tariff policies.

It also wants China to export so-called 4A products to the US, such as apparel, footwear and televisions, to ease voter affordability concerns and address China’s failure to fully satisfy the 2020 phase one agreement. China, however, would rather export higher-value goods and has expressed interest in a package larger than US$30 billion that Washington reportedly balked at.

On the political front, China at one point offered over a dozen non-economic issues for discussion, according to one source, only to see these largely rebuffed, with the administration laser-focused on three: a possible November Xi-Trump meeting at the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (Apec) summit in Shenzhen; a possible December meet-up at the G20 in Florida; and China’s help in pressuring Iran, something Beijing rejects given its ties with Tehran.

“It’s basically been the Chinese in a holding pattern, waiting for the US to stand up some sort of interlocutor,” the source, who declined to be named, said. “Two of those are logistics, and one of those is a non-starter … So, we have nothing to discuss on the political-diplomatic track.”

What was likely, analysts said, were some largely symbolic announcements on law enforcement, space, military communication and something on artificial intelligence, arranged this weekend by Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Chinese Vice-Premier He Lifeng.

“We should have exceedingly low expectations for the AI dialogue. In all likelihood, it will be a one or two-hour conversation in consecutive translation between two men in their 60s and 70s,” said Jon Czin, a Brookings fellow and former CIA analyst. “Moreover, the administration does not have a coherent view on what – if anything – should be done about regulating AI at home, never mind in tandem with our principal competitor.”

Expect US-China trade truce to be extended

Another likely announcement is an extension of the trade truce Xi and Trump reached in South Korea last year. But even this had seen wrangling, analysts said. China pushed for a 24 or 27-month extension, while Washington sought six to 12 months, reportedly believing this boosted its leverage amid the lingering distrust over phase one outcomes.

Looming over the talks – and a source of Beijing’s growing confidence – is China’s tight control over global rare earth production essential for hi-tech products. While Beijing wants to cement its constructive strategic stability framework, those close to negotiations said Washington saw it more as a holding action until it eased China’s grip and potentially resumed its trade war.

“If it’s two years, it’s not strategic stability,” said the person involved in planning. “China has got one definition and Washington has got a very different definition.”

Taiwan not on the agenda

Taiwan was not expected to be contentious, sources said, given Beijing’s satisfaction with Trump’s suggestion in May that the US would not fight a war over Taiwan.

“If I were the Chinese, I would say “you got basically everything out of Trump that you are likely to get’,” said Zack Cooper, senior fellow with the conservative American Enterprise Institute.

“Anything else that he does carries at least a 50 per cent chance that he says something that you’re not going to like. So why poke this issue?”

Beijing will, however, likely convey its displeasure with any Taiwan arms sale soon after the summit – as seen with December’s record US$11 billion package – any interaction with Taiwanese officials or any record weapon sales.

Beijing sees Taiwan as part of China to be reunited by force if necessary. Most countries, including the US, do not recognise Taiwan as an independent state, but Washington is traditionally opposed to any attempt to take the self-governed island by force and is committed by law to supplying it with weapons.

Within the administration, meanwhile, mud wrestling and competing objectives abound. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick has been sidelined from most China discussions, partly given his prominent role in threatening 50 per cent tariffs against China leading up to “Liberation Day”. That backfired when China imposed rare earth restrictions.

“That revealed the American underbelly to the Chinese,” a Washington insider said.

Bessent remains the lead on the China file but has numerous other responsibilities, while US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer oversees the 30-for-30 framework, keen on helping achieve balanced trade and blunting Chinese exports that could further deindustrialise the US.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio, a long-time China hawk, meanwhile, had been noticeably quiet, potentially burnishing his anti-Communist credentials for any 2028 presidential run, analysts said.

This weekend’s expected Bessent-He meeting and Thursday’s call between Rubio and Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi just days before the summit, also point to ineffective planning or outcomes.

“Maybe the Chinese are happy to have that,” said Cooper, a former NSC and Defence Department official. “But if anyone wanted a deliverable out of the summit, the last thing you’d do is wait until three days before arrival to try and hammer out the details.”

At least the two were talking, however, analysts said.

“Just to think how deeply hostile we were,” Sun said. “I’m not sure if it’s going to end well, but it’s already a miracle.

“The evaluation of whether this visit is a success should not just be contingent upon the big-ticket items.

“It’s also the overall stabilisation of the bilateral relationship, which people should remember could generate the largest nuclear war in human history. So on low expectations I would argue that no bad news is good news.”
