In Depth: Huafa’s Debt Crisis Tests the Limits of Local Government Support

Caixin Global | FULLTEXT | 863 words | 2026-11-06 04:53

1. Zhuhai has mobilized more than 40 billion yuan ($6 billion) to support Zhuhai Huafa Group, its largest city-owned company, after a debt scare rattled China's bond market. The intervention has left unresolved the question of how a fiscally strained local government can stabilize a sprawling conglomerate carrying 404.7 billion yuan in interest-bearing debt. Huafa's assets exceed 750 billion yuan, and its businesses range from housing and infrastructure to technology and financial services, making its troubles more complicated than those of a conventional property developer.

2. Huafa's liquidity problems surfaced publicly on Oct. 26 with reports of overdue payments on nonstandard financing. Its domestic and offshore bonds fell sharply, and by Oct. 30 selling spread beyond Huafa. Credit bonds and government bonds declined together, exchange-traded funds faced concentrated selling, and yields on 10-year and 30-year Chinese government bonds rose significantly. Several longer-dated Huafa corporate bonds fell more than 20% during trading on Nov. 2 and 3, triggering temporary trading halts.

3. On Nov. 4, the 21st Century Business Herald reported that Zhuhai had delivered more than 40 billion yuan in support through resource consolidation, restructuring and market-based transactions, pledging to mobilize nearly 70 billion yuan in additional resources. Zou Hui, director of Zhuhai's state-assets regulator, described Huafa's difficulties as temporary challenges caused by an industry downturn compounded by legacy burdens. Prices subsequently rebounded on four offshore yuan-denominated Huafa bonds and some heavily sold domestic issues.

4. Zhuhai established a task force to address Huafa's debt problems, with a guiding principle of protecting publicly traded debt while restructuring maturities and interest rates of nonstandard obligations. Some major lenders have indicated support verbally; others have sought additional collateral. The city and Huafa have reported the situation to Guangdong's provincial leadership, and for now Zhuhai is expected to handle the problem itself.

5. Huafa traces its origins to a property-management unit established in 1980, and for three decades remained largely a local developer with assets of about 30 billion yuan in 2011. After Li Guangning became chairman in May 2012, he expanded the group beyond Zhuhai into major cities, particularly Shanghai. By 2020, listed unit Huafa Industrial's annual sales had reached about 120.5 billion yuan, nearly 10 times their 2012 level. Li also championed diversification, expanding into six major business segments before being taken away for investigation in August 2025. Under his leadership, authority was highly concentrated, with most fund transfers requiring his signature.

6. By the end of June 2026, bank loans and nonstandard financing totaled about 251 billion yuan, more than 60% of Huafa's interest-bearing debt, with outstanding credit bonds amounting to about 106.9 billion yuan. The group had about 50.1 billion yuan in cash available after restricted funds were excluded, giving it cash coverage of just 0.32 times against more than 154.1 billion yuan in debt maturing within a year. Between 2019 and 2023, group assets nearly doubled to 729.6 billion yuan, revenue more than doubled to 179.5 billion yuan, but profit attributable to the parent rose only modestly from about 1.3 billion yuan to 1.77 billion yuan.

7. Property development and urban development provided the group's underlying profits but also became the main sources of accumulating risk. In 2025, Huafa Industrial's revenue rose 38.85% to 83.43 billion yuan, but it swung to a net loss attributable to shareholders of 9.5 billion yuan due to low-margin projects, inventory writedowns and falling valuations of investment properties. Huafa Group reported an attributable net loss of 11.03 billion yuan for the same year. Alongside property losses, local government payment arrears have tied up tens of billions of yuan in Huafa's urban development businesses, which grew out of Zhuhai's government-led, enterprise-operated development model. Major urban development projects still require about 82.79 billion yuan in investment.

8. Rating agencies have underscored Huafa's growing reliance on government help. On Nov. 4, China Lianhe retained its AAA ratings on the group but placed them on negative watch, citing continuing losses, weaker operating cash generation and concentrated repayment pressure. Zhuhai's capacity to provide support is itself under pressure—its 2025 general public-budget revenue was 49.41 billion yuan, and government-managed fund revenue fell 64.2% to 3.27 billion yuan, down more than 90% from 2021. Huafa's interest-bearing debt was equivalent to 8.2 times the city's annual general public-budget revenue, and a fixed-income team at China Zheshang Bank said fiscal cash alone was unlikely to provide a full backstop.

9. Some asset sales are already moving forward. On Oct. 29, a Huafa subsidiary announced that government-owned Gree Group and Zhuhai Transportation Holding Group would buy stakes in a holding company for a combined 4.57 billion yuan. Huafa chairman Guo Jin said on Nov. 4 that the group was accelerating home sales and putting idle land to work, and recorded property sales of 29.47 billion yuan in the first nine months of 2026. Guo is leading a second round of talks with some creditor banks. For now, banks aren't expected to call in loans but will adjust lending strategies and reduce exposure, leaving Huafa's immediate stability dependent on creditor agreements and its longer-term recovery dependent on whether asset restructuring can do more than move the burden around.
