A tough job market is pushing record numbers of Chinese college graduates toward low-paying grassroots community jobs, yet local governments are closing this once-reliable safety net. For example, Liu Min, a 2024 art master’s graduate, left a private-sector job after struggling with overtime and family pressure to find a government-affiliated position. After a month of full-time studying, she applied for three off-budget roles in Anhui; only a community worker position advanced her to an interview, with roughly 100 applicants per vacancy.

Ministries issued joint guidelines to channel graduates into urban and rural community posts, the first such specialized documents in years. However, recruitment has slowed: Offcn Education Technology data show about 68,067 community workers were hired in 2025, a 42.4% drop from 118,290 in 2020, and 44,177 in the first eight months of 2026. Researcher Li Qian said hiring could rebound after the guidelines, but the national civil service exam remains the bellwether.

The 2026 national civil service exam plans to recruit 38,100 people, down 1,602 positions year-on-year and the first contraction since 2019. Applicants jumped to 3.72 million, pushing the competition ratio to about 98-to-1, the seventh consecutive yearly increase. Despite shrinking vacancies and intensifying competition, graduates remain desperate for grassroots jobs.

Chen Huan, a 2022 social-work master’s graduate from a top law school, returned to her Jiangsu hometown as a full-time community worker earning 4,000 yuan ($595) per month. She found the job did not match her degree; she handles party-building, propaganda, and grid-based complaints, with fragmented and mentally draining tasks and occasional overnight duty. She initially saw it as a stopgap but failed civil service exams, found few transferable skills, and observed almost no one resigning; upward mobility is extremely narrow, and she has almost no savings after rent and food.

Community-worker pay varies sharply by region: 4,000–5,000 yuan monthly in Shandong, Liaoning, and Jilin; 6,000–9,000 in Shanghai; and Beijing’s average annual pay reached 100,000 yuan since 2018. Liu Min’s middle-tier district pays modestly after social security deductions, but she is unconcerned because her family is financially secure. She values the supportive office atmosphere and better physical and mental health, and plans to stay while studying for public institution exams.

The policy push began with a 2022 State Council directive to expand grassroots employment, followed by a 2023 multi-ministry document. A 2022 urbanization plan set a target of 18 community workers per 10,000 urban residents by 2025, fueling a hiring boom: community hiring grew 16% between 2018 and 2020, and 114,600 urban community jobs were posted by September 2022. But recruitment has since slowed markedly: Chen’s office has had no new hires in two years, and Liu observed that a district hiring about 100 people may not recruit again for three years.

Competition is brutal. In Hefei’s High-tech Zone, 11,808 people applied for 96 community jobs in 2026, a ratio of 101.7-to-1, with one vacancy drawing over 210 applicants. Academic credential inflation is also visible: Chen was the only master’s graduate in her intake while older colleagues mostly had associate degrees; Liu noted older workers sometimes had only middle-school educations, but new hires are all university graduates, including some from elite institutions.

Another grassroots channel, the “Three Supports and One Assistance” program, is shrinking too. Across 29 provinces disclosing 2026 plans, 16 reduced quotas, cutting a combined 1,659 positions; Shandong, Yunnan, Anhui, and Jiangsu each cut over 20%, while Xinjiang expanded. Total recruitment was about 42,000, down 183. In August 2026, a large-scale cheating scandal emerged in Henan’s exam, where 3,082 positions drew 141,158 applicants—an admission rate below 2%.
