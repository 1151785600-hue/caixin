After losing both legs, snowboarder Neil Tsui sets his sights on the Paralympics

SCMP | FREE | 1376 words | 2026-10-10

I was born in 1981, in Surrey, south of Vancouver, in Canada. I’m the youngest of four – three boys and one girl. My father is Chinese, originally from Shanghai. He left during the communist revolution and moved to Macau and then Hong Kong. My mother is Brazilian, originally from just outside São Paulo. They met in Brazil and immigrated to Canada in 1970. My upbringing was very outdoors. Everywhere you go, there are mountains and ocean. I got into snowboarding when I was about 13 years old. It’s my favourite thing to do.

I went to Earl Marriott Secondary School, and didn’t go to university, much to my parents’ frustration. I loved carpentry, was a petrolhead and enjoyed fixing cars with my brothers. Then, I worked in sales. I had a great life, friends, family. But as I approached 30, I felt that if I didn’t leave, I was never going to go. I went to visit my sister, who was living in Hong Kong, and just like everyone else, I fell in love with the city. So, I got a job working in business development and marketing for professional services.

From 2011, for three years, I lived in Mid-Levels and was a recruiter and really enjoyed it. I was then approached about a technology role within recruitment. It was a start-up, an American firm, and that was a great experience. The start-up was based in San Francisco, but we launched it in Asia. I later worked for another tech company, Phaidon. In 2019, my sister and her family left Hong Kong, and I felt ready for my next move.

I moved with the company to Singapore in September 2020. I’d met my (future) wife, Sarah, on a dating app in 2019. We finally met in person in May 2021 and an attraction was that she loved wakeboarding, like me. We got married in 2023. Six weeks after we arrived back from our honeymoon, I had an eczema spot on the back of my leg. My dermatologist gave me a cortisone jab but made me get a blood test as I had also been taking Olumiant, a strong drug for alopecia, for six months. I then went wakeboarding on Sentosa beach, where the water is filthy, and, in the evening, started getting chills. My fever got worse and I collapsed on the bathroom floor. We got a taxi to the hospital. My blood pressure was spiking and I passed out.

The following day, my wife told me I had developed sepsis and gone into septic shock. I had necrotising fasciitis, which is a flesh-eating disease. My whole body was shutting down. I was put into an induced coma, so that my body could fight to stay alive. My sister flew out from Amsterdam and my mother-in-law came from Britain. For three days, my wife just held my hand, staring at a heart monitor, not knowing if I was going to make it. Miraculously, after four days, I started turning around – my heart, my kidneys on dialysis, everything was starting to turn around. They woke me up 10 days later. My legs were covered in bandages. And all I could see were my feet, which had gone this kind of blue-purple colour. Three of my doctors came in and told me they had given me an inotrope drug – it’s what they give anyone who goes into serious septic shock. It redirects all the blood flow to your torso, to preserve your vital organs. As a result, your extremities – such as hands, feet, even your face, your nose – get cut off from adequate blood flow. So, they stood by the bed and explained all of this, and then said, “We’re going to give you a bilateral amputation. We’re going to amputate both your legs below the knee.”

All I could picture was my wife, who I’d been married to for six weeks, pushing me in a wheelchair. And then I thought about my dad. My parents started out as broke immigrants. And my dad’s outlook is: if you fall, it’s up to you to pick yourself up. In my head, it just clicked – “OK, you’re going to own this” – and I opened my eyes and looked at the doctors and said, “Can I still snowboard?” And they said yes. I was lucky to be alive.

I typed into Instagram: “Amputee Snowboarding”. And it came up with a Canadian athlete named Tyler Turner. He’s the first Canadian to win a gold medal for snowboarding, at the 2022 Beijing Paralympics. And he was the same as me – a double amputee below the knee. I wrote to him and then we talked for an hour and a half. He told me to harness the snowboarding as that would get me through my recovery. As a joke, he said they were always looking for double-amputee athletes for the Paralympic team. In my head, it clicked – getting on the team would be my North Star.

Four days later, I had the first leg amputated. (Tsui has undergone 17 surgeries in total.) A couple of months later, I got my first set of prosthetics. I felt so tall! I had an incredible physiotherapist, who worked with me on my core, and knew about my snowboarding goal. I just kept building up my strength and balance, and started playing tennis again and riding a bike. I met Tyler in the summer of 2024 and we went surfing together in Tofino, on Vancouver Island; it was an instant bond.

He asked me if I wanted to go to the Netherlands in November 2024, to get my classification for the Paralympics and, since I was living in Singapore, I hadn’t even been on a snowboard yet. I went to Trifecta Singapore, a sports facility with an indoor Snow Arena for practising snowboarding and skiing using hi-tech simulators. I couldn’t even balance. I was there for an hour and I couldn’t let go of the bar long enough. When I got home, I ripped my legs off and was on the floor, absolutely broken. Sarah encouraged me to go and meet Tyler and get the classification.

Two weeks later, I was in the Netherlands, at SnowWorld Landgraaf, an indoor ski resort. I strapped my snowboard on and we went up in the chairlift and Tyler filmed. As soon as I started going, it just clicked. I did a big carve right, a big carve left, and then I just ripped all the way to the bottom without falling. I was screaming and hooting. The video is quite ridiculous; I’m literally whooping the whole way. And I’m crying. I was so elated and, all the way down, I was saying, “We did it!” I was just thinking about Sarah and how this was for us – this is what had got us through a hellish year, to get to this moment. It was 366 days after my right leg was cut off. I did two runs and went to get my classification.

We now have a son, he’s nine months old, and we are moving back to Canada next year. Canadian Olympic snowboarder Mark Fawcett said I need to spend as much time on snow as I can. I’m still aiming for the Paralympics in 2030. We bought a house on Vancouver Island. By the time of the next Winter Paralympics, in 2030, in the French Alps, I’m going to be 49. Three years ago, I was getting my legs amputated and I’m now snowboarding with Mark, one of the godfathers of snowboarding.

I now do public speaking at corporations and at schools. I’m not trying to be some sort of inspirational person. I am just trying to be the old Neil, who just loves activity. But, obviously, I know there is a lesson in there. The irony is that it took me losing both of my legs to feel limitless. I won’t know until literally a month or two before the actual Games if I’m on the team. It’s a lofty goal and a total gamble, but you need to stay the course.
