Why I am grateful for Asean’s endless meetings

SCMP | FREE | 1010 words | 2026-09-18

I remember 2011 well. Indonesia was chairing the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, and I was serving as deputy foreign minister for Asean cooperation. I learned a great deal, although if I had to summarise the experience in one word, I would say: meetings.

Asean members meet. They talk. They disagree. They issue statements. Then, quite remarkably, they meet again. We meet in conference rooms, coffee shops and restaurants. We speak by telephone and online. And when matters become particularly serious, there is always durian.

To outsiders, Asean consensus can appear tedious. Where is the breakthrough? Why does everything take so long?

Yet the longer I work in diplomacy, the more I appreciate the willingness to meet again, especially after a disagreement. Agreement is important, but it is not the only measure of successful diplomacy. Sometimes, the achievement is simply that disagreement does not end the conversation. This is what I understand as the habit of dialogue.

Working in Beijing, I see something familiar around Chinese dinner tables. A round table has no visible head, creating a sense of equality. Yet it does not remove order. Everyone has a place and everyone is expected to contribute.

Those who have conducted business in China will understand that many important decisions are shaped around such tables. Trust is built gradually, through conversation, shared food and the willingness to spend time understanding one another.

I wonder whether this habit offers a modest lesson for a world accelerating towards multipolarity. I use the word “accelerating” because the changing distribution of global power appears to demand immediate adjustment. Yet multipolarity does not tell us how power will be exercised or what kind of order will emerge.

China’s vision of an equal and orderly multipolar world recognises this challenge. Its Global Governance Initiative similarly places sovereign equality at the centre. But how can sovereign equality become a working practice? How can countries create order without simply recreating hierarchy?

Perhaps Asean can offer part of the answer.

International order needs power to sustain it, but it also needs limits on how power is used. Institutions make those limits more durable. Countries accept restraint because predictability benefits everyone: they know broadly what others may do, what they are unlikely to do and how disagreements will be managed.

The difficulty arises when power shifts. Established powers may fear that restraint gives rivals room to advance. Rising powers may question rules they had little opportunity to shape. When countries no longer believe restraint will be reciprocated, rules begin to give way to transactions. Every concession has a price. Every act of self-restraint starts to look like a favour that must be repaid.

Cooperation does not require countries to agree on everything. It requires them to preserve the means of working through what they cannot agree upon

This is especially complicated because power today takes many forms. One country may possess greater military strength, another greater manufacturing capacity, another financial influence, advanced technology or essential mineral resources. Strength in one area often exists alongside dependence in another.

Countries will not always share the same interests. But perhaps they can develop a shared understanding of how their different forms of power should be exercised.

Indonesia has Asean’s largest population and economy. By accepting consensus, Indonesia accepts a restraint on its weight. This restraint is useful because, as Bilahari Kausikan, a good friend of mine, has explained, consensus reassures smaller members that Indonesia cannot impose decisions upon them. It also reassures Indonesia that smaller members cannot simply combine to outvote it.

Each accepts limits in exchange for reassurance. No one gets everything but no one is expected to surrender everything either. This matters in a region of different political systems, cultures, levels of development and national interests. Such diversity complicates collective decisions. It also makes Southeast Asia difficult for any outside power to capture entirely behind a single agenda.

Its members may not converge on every interest, but they can converge on certain procedures, protocols, principles or concepts, for example, the non-use of force, a zone of peace, freedom and neutrality, a nuclear-weapon-free zone, Asean centrality and Asean-plus.

We have learned, imperfectly, how to disagree without immediately reaching for the divorce papers.

There are, of course, limits to Asean’s experience. Consensus can take time. Dialogue can become repetitive. Meetings can produce more meetings, sometimes followed by preparatory meetings for the next meeting. I would therefore hesitate to present Asean as a complete model for the global order. Its lesson is more modest: cooperation does not require countries to agree on everything. It requires them to preserve the means of working through what they cannot agree upon.

The habit of dialogue helps sustain this process. Equality gives every country a voice. Humility helps each country listen and recognise that it may not possess the whole answer. Repeated interaction gradually creates predictability.

Admittedly, habitual dialogue cannot erase power differences. It cannot resolve every dispute. But it can do something essential. It can create time when decisions are moving too quickly. It can lower the temperature when tensions are rising. And it can help countries adjust to power shifts without allowing every disagreement to become a crisis.

This is why I view the first Indonesia-China comprehensive strategic dialogue, held in Jakarta last month, with hope. Its importance lies not only in what was discussed but in the habit that made the discussion possible.

And there is quite a momentum to protect. Since 2015, the bilateral trade volume has tripled and Chinese investment has risen over 12-fold. At this pace, US$300 billion of trade in the next four to five years is within reach.

Through my years in China, I have walked its streets, met its people and spoken to many who will inherit whatever we build today. I do not know whether I have done enough. But I take some comfort in believing I have helped nurture the habit of dialogue. As long as we remain willing to meet again, we will always have something on which to build.
