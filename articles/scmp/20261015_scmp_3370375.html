Chinese brokers work to crack the final code: win clients on global stage

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2082 words | 2026-10-15

China’s investment banks spent decades trying to emulate Goldman Sachs, the Wall Street powerhouse that became a benchmark for the country’s financial ambitions and whose alumni included senior US policymakers like former Treasury secretary Henry Paulson.

Today, Beijing’s goal has evolved beyond building a Chinese version of Goldman.

At the Central Financial Work Conference in 2023, President Xi Jinping called for cultivating first-class investment institutions as part of China’s ambition to become a financial superpower with distinct Chinese characteristics.

Many analysts and industry players believe the foundations are already in place. China now operates the world’s second-largest capital market after the US, while direct financing through stocks and bonds for the first time surpassed bank lending in 2025, ushering in a golden era for securities firms, they said.

The next challenge lies beyond mainland China. As Hong Kong’s role in China’s capital markets expands and mainland companies invest more aggressively overseas, Chinese brokerages are increasingly pushing into international markets.

“Overseas business, particularly in Hong Kong, is one of the key growth drivers for Chinese securities companies as they expand beyond their home market,” said Franco Lam, a director at Fitch Ratings.

From apprentice to contender

Compared with Wall Street and the City of London, China’s capital markets have a relatively short history.

When China International Capital Corporation (CICC) was established in 1995 as the country’s first Sino-foreign joint-venture investment bank, its ambitions were global from the outset. Yet its bankers had little experience to draw on.

Fang Fenglei, one of CICC’s founding executives, in an article published in 2018 in China Securities Journal, recalled how Morgan Stanley, one of the venture’s founding shareholders, arranged study visits for him to New York and London to learn the operations of Western financial markets, as part of what he described as a journey to “learn from the West”.

The learning curve was steep. Paulson said in his book, Dealing with China, that Goldman Sachs bankers gave CICC executives and Chinese officials what amounted to a crash course in taking companies public as they prepared China Mobile’s predecessor for its Hong Kong and New York listing in 1997.

The deal raised US$4.2 billion, offering an early glimpse of how quickly the gap between Chinese and Western investment banks could narrow.

At the time, the relationship between Chinese and foreign investment banks was heavily tilted in favour of Wall Street, whose firms brought technical expertise and access to international institutional investors that Chinese brokerages lacked.

Over the following two decades, Chinese securities firms moved beyond simply learning the mechanics of international investment banking to acquiring the networks and capabilities needed to compete overseas.

Citic Securities took a major step in 2013 by acquiring brokerage CLSA from French bank Credit Agricole, gaining an established research, sales and trading platform spanning major global financial centres. More than a decade later, the group is increasingly adopting the Citic Securities International, or CSI, identity across its global operations as it gradually phases out the CLSA brand.

Industry veterans liken the transition to one from apprentice to competitor.

Wang Junhong, deputy CEO of Guotai Junan International, where he has worked since 2012, said Chinese brokerages spent their early overseas years as “apprentices”, often playing secondary roles on deals led by foreign banks.

But as Chinese bankers accumulated experience and recruited internationally trained talent, they progressed from taking part in underwriting syndicates to leading them, Wang said.

“You go from being a participant in the underwriting syndicate to becoming the lead underwriter,” Wang said in a recent interview with the South China Morning Post. “The apprentice graduates. You become capable of standing on your own, and in some businesses you may even do better.”

Today, Chinese investment banks were “at least on an equal footing” with their Western rivals in much of Hong Kong’s initial public offering (IPO) market, and led in some areas, Wang said.

The rise of Chinese companies had also changed the equation, he said. As mainland businesses grew larger and more familiar to global investors, Chinese brokerages’ understanding of their home market became increasingly valuable. At the same time, the growth of mainland institutional investors and private wealth had created a much deeper pool of Chinese capital to support offshore transactions, Wang added.

Beijing’s push for global champions

Beijing is giving new momentum to a decades-old ambition as it pursues its broader goal of becoming a financial superpower.

China’s securities regulator has set a goal of having two to three investment banks and financial institutions with international competitiveness and market leadership by 2035.

Wu Qing, chairman of the China Securities Regulatory Commission (CSRC), said in December that a first-class investment bank should “not merely be large”, but should have a voice in global capital allocation and asset pricing while attracting top financial talent.

The renewed policy push has been accompanied by a wave of fresh capital. At least nine mainland Chinese securities firms had announced or completed plans to inject a combined 51.7 billion yuan (US$7.7 billion) into their Hong Kong operations as of the end of August, according to a calculation by the SCMP.

Among the largest commitments, Citic Securities raised 16 billion yuan to support its international business, while Guotai Haitong Securities and Huatai Securities announced plans to inject 9 billion yuan and up to HK$9 billion (US$1.1 billion), respectively, into their offshore units to support overseas expansion.

By the end of 2025, 34 Chinese securities firms had established 36 international subsidiaries with combined assets of HK$1.94 trillion, up nearly 32 per cent from a year earlier, according to data from the Securities Association of China.

But Wang said the industry’s recent capital injections should not be viewed simply as compliance with Beijing’s policy directives.

“When you have a good business foundation and want to do more business, you need more capital to support it,” he said, describing much of the fundraising as “proactive” expansion rather than a sign of weak overseas operations.

The timing has also been favourable. A revival in Hong Kong’s IPO market and growing demand from mainland clients for offshore diversification had created new growth opportunities, especially as the recovery in mainland capital market activity remained gradual, Fitch’s Lam said.

Hong Kong: the proving ground

For most Chinese securities firms, internationalisation has started in the same place: Hong Kong.

Wang described the city as both a “bridgehead” and a “core hub” for the industry’s overseas expansion. Its proximity to the mainland, bilingual business environment, and status as the principal offshore financing centre for mainland companies made it the natural first stop, he added.

Hong Kong also provided Chinese investment banks with a market where their biggest competitive advantage – deep knowledge of Chinese companies and investors – mattered most. Mainland companies now accounted for about 78 per cent of Hong Kong’s stock market capitalisation and 90 per cent of trading turnover, according to Fitch Ratings.

While mainland markets had stabilised in the first half of 2026, profitability growth across the securities sector could moderate in the second half because of a high comparison base and softer market sentiment, according to Fitch.

IPO activity on the mainland also remained below levels seen in 2022 and 2023, Lam said. Hong Kong, by contrast, offered access to mainland clients through cross-border links, a broader range of investment products, a diverse international institutional-investor base and global market reach, he said. Changes to listing rules had also enhanced the city’s appeal as a listing venue, Lam added.

Those advantages have translated into stronger balance sheets and a growing pipeline of capital-market business.

Citic Securities generated 15.86 billion yuan in revenue outside mainland China in the first half of 2026, up 45.5 per cent from a year earlier, while net profit at its international arm more than doubled to US$829 million, according to its interim report.

CICC’s overseas revenue rose 45 per cent to 9.19 billion yuan over the same period, accounting for about 35 per cent of its total revenue.

Hong Kong subsidiaries of Chinese securities firms accounted for 50.6 per cent of IPO business among the city’s top 30 brokers in the first eight months of 2026, up from 35.3 per cent in 2022, according to Fitch data. The share held by foreign securities firms fell to 26.5 per cent from 29.3 per cent over the same period.

Chinese brokerages were also seeking to diversify beyond the more volatile investment banking and trading businesses by expanding wealth and asset management operations, while cross-border investment schemes were creating additional opportunities over the medium term, Lam said.

“If you see more opportunities coming ahead, definitely they want to put more capital into areas where there is more growth potential,” he added.

Their success in Hong Kong also points to the next challenge: whether advantages built around Chinese companies, Chinese capital and a China-linked financial centre can be replicated in markets where those advantages are less pronounced.

Charting a new path

The next experiment is already under way. Chinese securities firms are extending their reach beyond their traditional Hong Kong base, with Southeast Asia and the Middle East emerging as key growth markets.

China Securities established a regional office in the Dubai International Financial Centre (DIFC) in late September to pursue opportunities in equity and debt capital markets, investment banking, asset management and institutional business.

CICC opened its own DIFC branch a year earlier, positioning Dubai as a gateway between China and the Gulf region.

Southeast Asia has become another testing ground. China Galaxy Securities has built one of the industry’s broadest regional networks, spanning Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand and Indonesia.

However, the expansion abroad has also exposed firms to the risks of operating beyond familiar markets.

Everbright Securities’ investment in European sports-rights agency MP & Silva and Haitong International’s heavy losses during the offshore bond market downturn offered costly lessons. Haitong International lost a combined US$1.7 billion in 2022 and 2023 after defaults swept through the high-yield debt market, where it had become a major player.

Winning local trust

“There is still a long way to go. The 2.0 version has yet to arrive,” said Chen Zhiwu, a finance professor at the University of Hong Kong, referring to the next stage of internationalisation in which Chinese securities firms build substantial businesses among local, non-Chinese clients.

“Internationalisation is not just about setting up an office in New York or London. That is relatively easy,” Chen said.

The harder test, he said, was whether a company in Europe, Southeast Asia or Latin America, presented with a choice between a local bank, a Wall Street institution and a Chinese broker, would choose the Chinese firm to lead its IPO or advise on an acquisition.

The same question applied to wealth management: would a wealthy local family be willing to “entrust millions of dollars to a Chinese institution”? Chen asked. “It means that you can make them feel you are one of us or you can be one of us.”

Guotai Junan’s experience in Vietnam illustrates the challenge. Its local brokerage, acquired in 2019, ranked about 40th among roughly 80 securities firms in the country and was aiming to break into the top tier over the longer term, according to Guotai Junan’s Wang.

“We want to accelerate our growth, and given our client base, capital scale and market expertise, we could become much bigger very quickly. But there are still some constraints,” Wang said.

The veteran banker, who also chairs Guotai Junan’s operations in Vietnam and Macau, said an outsized presence by Chinese companies could stir local sensitivities in some overseas markets such as Vietnam.

“If a Chinese company became one of the dominant players, they might feel some pressure,” he said.

For Wang, Chinese investment banks would only become “truly global” when they could occupy a position in London or New York similar to the one Goldman Sachs and other global banks enjoyed in Hong Kong, and when their client base was no longer predominantly Chinese.

“Ultimately, you need to serve local companies with global presence,” he said. “Can you serve an American company like Apple? Can you serve Google?”

If that was the case, Wang said, “then I think you could say you have truly become global”.
