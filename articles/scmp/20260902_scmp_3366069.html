Exclusive: The Boar brings authentic British pub experience to Hong Kong’s Central

SCMP | FREE | 1216 words | 2026-09-03

If a good pint is what you’re after, Matt Richardson may have a solution. As one of the brains behind The Boar, a new British public house (where the shortened form and better-known term, “pub”, comes from) and restaurant in Central, he has been working to bring an authentic watering-hole experience to Hong Kong – even travelling to Ireland to perfect the Guinness pour in time for the establishment’s September 1 opening.

“I’m going to Dublin in two weeks, just to study it,” Richardson tells me about his late-August plans to visit the Guinness Storehouse. We’re sitting inside his office on Elgin Street, just one step from his latest venture. “Between me going right to the source and us [ensuring] that we’ve got the best beer lines, coolers and cleanliness … that’s the best we can do, that’s what makes a great Guinness. Every little bit counts, because the liquid in the barrel is not even the main element.”

Originally from Milton Keynes, northwest of London, Richardson has kept himself busy in the nine years he’s been in Hong Kong. He is the founder and managing director of Big Top Hospitality, the group behind Australian restobar Bourke’s, American-style saloon Honky Tonks Tavern, Mediterranean seaside terrace Terracotta Lamma, fresh pasta bar Twist in Sheung Wan, and bagel joint and delicatessen Mendel’s.

With five restaurants already in his portfolio, the British restaurateur is finally opening something that connects him to his roots, but with a name that pays homage to his time in both Hong Kong and Britain. “Boars are the largest land mammals in Hong Kong, but you can also find them all around the UK,” explains Richardson. “Here, they’re kind of loved, aren’t they? You walk up The Peak, and people are super excited to see a little family of boars walking around. I quite enjoy it.”

Behind the pass, he has brought on two British chefs with fine-dining pedigrees: executive chef Graham Long, who has worked in Michelin-rated kitchens across London and launched culinary concepts for luxury hotels in Hong Kong. Day-to-day kitchen operations are run by head chef Jake Bennett, who cut his teeth at several Simon Rogan restaurants, including three-Michelin-star L’Enclume in Britain’s Lake District. Most recently, he was at The Baker and The Bottleman, in Wan Chai.

“I jumped on board as soon as I found out [Richardson was opening a pub],” says Long. He recalls how the pair began brainstorming the making of The Boar over a few pints.

“I’ve always felt that pubs were missing in Hong Kong to a degree. You’d think there would be more, given Hong Kong’s history and the expat community that’s here.”

Despite its name, however, the pub and dining room will not be serving wild boar. In Hong Kong, it’s illegal to kill or capture the animal. In Britain, they were hunted to extinction several hundred years ago, before being reintroduced into the ecosystem in recent decades by farms. “I’ve cooked it professionally before. It’s not that common any more, but we used to get it from our game dealer. I actually prefer normal pork, to be honest, but it’s nice,” Long admits.

So, if boar is not on the menu, what is? Classic pub grub, of course, including Welsh rarebit, sticky toffee pudding and pork cheek and lobster pie, as well as courses that speak to British sensibilities, such as Eton mess and coronation chicken salad.

With both chefs coming from haute-cuisine backgrounds, the dishes reflect their technical discipline. “We want to make the best version of everything. Yes, we’re going to do fish and chips, but we want to make sure it’s the best fish and chips we can possibly do,” Long explains.

As for their Scotch eggs, these will be made with British sausage meat, black pudding and Japanese eggs, then fried to order. “You know, nothing beats a Scotch egg. Hopefully, nothing will beat our Scotch egg,” says Long.

The Boar will offer two menus, with “downstairs” serving casual bites such as prawn cocktail toast, the signature BLT sandwich, Guinness and treacle bread with salted English butter, broccoli and Stichelton tart, and more, while “upstairs” hosts a more formal dining format, with celeriac and mushroom Wellington, roasted turbot chop, baked Chilean sea bass and chargrilled rib-eye steak to look forward to. (Sunday roasts are in the works, too.) “It’s where we get to have a little bit more fun, be a bit more chef-y, have a few more luxury ingredients and a more sit-down experience,” says Long.

One of the highlights of the upstairs menu is the roasted duck, dry-aged for two weeks in-house – no short cuts, all processed by the Boar team – and served with buttered turnips, carrots, a sauce of port and citrus, and toller’s pie, a play on the timeless shepherd’s pie in reference to the Nova Scotia Duck Tolling Retriever, a medium-sized gun-dog breed traditionally trained by hunters to find, flush out and retrieve shot game.

Moving into Flaming Frango’s old space on Elgin Street, the two-storey location, with an expansive ground floor and quirky structural nooks, has just the right bones to make it feel like a British pub – but Richardson has a fresher take in mind.

“We want it to feel more like a modern pub, because we feel it would be unjust to try and transport something you’d find from the laneways in London [to Hong Kong] – that would be too theatrical,” says Richardson. Instead, he explains he was inspired by his favourite pub, The Pelican, a Michelin Bib Gourmand spot in London’s Notting Hill neighbourhood.

“It’s a totally different experience from what people might know of the pub world, but the aesthetics, the feel, is that,” he says. “So, downstairs, think brighter colours and nice wood textures, but lots of nods to the things you might recognise from a pub back home, like the way we do the edgings on the tables and footrests and textures. And then when you move upstairs, this will have a different feel – more green, a bit more opulent.”

For Long and Richardson, The Boar is more than just another restaurant opening. “I spent most of my adult life in London and, generally, when I wasn’t working in a kitchen, I was in a pub,” recalls Long, whose time in the city included a stint at two-Michelin-star Pied à Terre, then helmed by Shane Osborn.

“Wherever you grow up, everyone has their local pub,” Long says. “[A pub is] shaped by the community that it’s in.”

Richardson agrees: “When I’m back home [in Britain, the go-to pub is] just wherever my friends are.”

Creating that sense of community here in Hong Kong – where both restaurateur and chef have settled, and for not an insignificant amount of time – is top of their minds when it comes to shaping the destination. “We let the food, the drinks and the genuine hospitality speak for themselves,” says Richardson, adding that every visit to The Boar “feels like coming home”.
