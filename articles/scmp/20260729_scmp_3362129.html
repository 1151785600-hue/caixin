Can China and Indonesia’s strong financial ties withstand ‘resource nationalism’?

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2037 words | 2026-07-29

Back in 2009, when China’s economy was expanding at about twice today’s pace, a little-known private firm made a quiet bet on uncharted territory: Indonesia.

The company was on a quest: to secure nickel, a vital industrial metal used in stainless steel production and, increasingly, to power advanced batteries.

That bet proved lucrative. By leveraging Indonesia’s vast nickel reserves – the world’s largest – and securing greater control over upstream supplies, Tsingshan Group has grown into a leading global stainless steel producer. It joined the Forbes Global 500 in 2021, and in 2025 generated 432 billion yuan (US$63.8 billion) in revenue, with operations spanning from Zimbabwe to the United States.

The company, founded in Wenzhou in eastern China’s Zhejiang province, is one of a growing number of Chinese enterprises helping to forge deeper trade and industrial ties between the world’s second-largest economy and Southeast Asia’s largest.

What began as a push to secure raw minerals has evolved into a broader commercial relationship spanning resources, downstream metal processing, manufacturing and, most recently, financial services – though one increasingly shaped by Jakarta’s shifting domestic priorities.

“Indonesia provides the resources, while China contributes capital, technology and manufacturing expertise, creating an increasingly integrated economic ecosystem,” said Zhao Xijun, a finance professor at Renmin University of China in Beijing.

Chasing the battery boom

Indonesian President Prabowo Subianto’s administration has prioritised capturing greater added value from the nation’s mineral deposits since taking office in late 2024, said Hu Jie, executive director of the Southeast Asia Centre at the Shanghai Advanced Institute of Finance.

Among the most important is nickel: a critical input in electric vehicle (EV) manufacturing and lithium-ion batteries, leading Jakarta to court foreign capital in downstream processing, said Hu, who keeps close ties with Indonesian officials and provides advisory services to Chinese firms looking to invest in the country.

This ambition has quickly translated into aggressive domestic targets. Indonesia has set a goal of deploying 2 million EVs and 12 million electric two-wheelers by 2030. According to data from its Ministry of Energy and Mineral Resources (MEMR), the nation currently has about 11 million active cars on its roads.

To meet this demand, Chinese car manufacturers are expanding local production facilities. In April, local media outlet Jakarta Globe reported that BYD’s factory in West Java – designed to deliver up to 150,000 EVs annually – was scheduled to begin operations and produce its first vehicles this year, with a total investment of 11.2 trillion Indonesian rupiah (US$616 million).

China’s industrial footprint in Indonesia has also shifted further downstream along the nickel supply chain, with technology-focused players taking centre stage. Earlier this month, Shenzhen-listed Azure Corporation unveiled plans for a US$290 million lithium battery manufacturing facility there. The project is expected to have an annual production capacity of 5 gigawatt-hours for a range of lithium batteries.

However, industry analysts stressed that attracting foreign investment formed part of a much larger Indonesian blueprint. The International Institute for Sustainable Development, a Canada-based think tank, wrote in a 2025 research report that “the ambition is not only to create a market for EVs – the country has the largest nickel reserves in the world and aims to become a leading producer of EV batteries.”

According to the Jakarta Globe, EV sales in the country surged 141 per cent year on year in 2025 to more than 100,000 units, hitting a record in December.

“Chinese manufacturers and Indonesia’s market are naturally aligned. Faced with weak domestic demand and industrial oversupply at home, Chinese producers are driven to deepen their presence in Indonesia,” said Hu from the Shanghai Advanced Institute of Finance.

And as Chinese businesses become more deeply embedded in Indonesia’s industrial ecosystem, a parallel financial architecture is also taking shape.

Alternative payment channels

In June this year, the People’s Bank of China designated Bank of China (Hong Kong)’s Jakarta branch as Indonesia’s official yuan clearing bank, while Bank Mandiri became a direct participant in the Cross-border Interbank Payment System (CIPS) – Beijing’s payment infrastructure often seen as an alternative to the Western-dominated Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication, commonly known as Swift.

That same month, the Hong Kong Monetary Authority, the PBOC and Bank Indonesia, the Southeast Asian country’s central bank, also signed a trilateral memorandum to ease the use of the rupiah and offshore yuan in transactions between Indonesia and Hong Kong. This followed the PBOC and Bank Indonesia expanding a 2021 bilateral local-currency settlement framework last year to cover all balance-of-payments transactions.

This institutional push builds on the rapid growth seen in 2025, when bilateral local-currency settlement between the two countries more than doubled to US$13.19 billion from US$4.9 billion in 2024, according to Indonesia’s central bank, as cited by Chinese state news agency Xinhua.

“A mutually reinforcing ecosystem is gradually taking shape,” said Matteo Giovannini, a senior finance manager at the Industrial and Commercial Bank of China.

“Resource cooperation supports industrial investment, which in turn creates greater demand for cross-border financial services, local currency settlement and trade financing.”

Deeper trade and investment ties, he added, would make the yuan “naturally more attractive” by lowering foreign-exchange costs and reducing reliance on third currencies.

Indonesia has also begun tapping China’s capital markets through its inaugural 7 billion yuan issuance of panda bonds – yuan-denominated debt securities sold in China’s domestic bond markets.

Giovannini, also a non-resident associate fellow at the Centre for China and Globalisation, said these recent developments underscored Indonesia’s growing role in Beijing’s push to internationalise its currency.

“Indonesia represents an important testing ground for the gradual expansion of the yuan’s use in [the Association of Southeast Asian Nations], particularly if local-currency settlement mechanisms continue to mature,” Giovannini said, though he acknowledged the US dollar would remain dominant for the foreseeable future.

This growing financial alignment, however, is unfolding against a shifting domestic backdrop in Indonesia, where operations on the ground are running up against political currents.

Tightening the screws?

What many Chinese scholars describe as a wave of “resource nationalism” has swept across Indonesia this year. The trajectory of domestic companies, especially Tsingshan Group, captures both sides of the story: a company that built its global dominance on the country’s nickel riches, only to face a more challenging policy landscape years later.

In its “Macroeconomic Policy Report on Indonesia” published in June, the Shanghai Academy of Social Sciences said “policy overhauls targeting nickel mining, in particular, have dealt severe blows to foreign investors, with Chinese enterprises already bearing the brunt of the fallout.”

As part of this shift, Indonesian President Prabowo has adjusted domestic policies on nickel production. Reflecting a tightening regulatory grip, Indonesia’s MEMR has capped the national production quota for 2026 at 260 million to 270 million tonnes, implying a 30 per cent year-on-year decline.

Weda Bay Nickel in Indonesia – the world’s largest nickel mine – is a joint venture formed by Tsingshan Group, French miner Eramet and local firm Antam, with Tsingshan holding a majority share. In June, Eramet told Mining Weekly, a South African trade magazine, that production there had been suspended since late May and that the company had reduced its workforce and entered a maintenance phase.

“The mining output quota has been exhausted,” said Jerome Baudelet, chief executive of Eramet Indonesia.

Such operational halts reflected a calculated strategy, analysts said. “Jakarta hopes to wield its regulatory toolkit as an effective policy lever to underpin nickel prices. Global benchmark nickel did rally to a peak before pulling back, leaving it with gains of roughly 20 per cent from the late 2025 level,” said Xu Yu, analyst at Orient Futures.

“The country also raised relevant tax levies. The rationale behind this regulatory tweak is plain to see – elevated nickel prices would lift fiscal receipts and fuel broader economic expansion.”

According to the Indonesian government, the nation’s budget deficit is expected to widen to 2.85 per cent of gross domestic product this year, surpassing both its prior forecast of 2.68 per cent and last year’s reading of 2.81 per cent.

At the same time, blowback from the Iran war is spilling over into the domestic economy while pressure from the United States is mounting. Faced with challenges at home and overseas, the Indonesian rupiah has sustained a steep depreciation.

Against this backdrop, the market’s earlier trailblazers could be weighing a pullback. In June, Reuters reported that Tsingshan Group was considering a major development in Madagascar, while Hong Kong-listed Lygend Resources – a Chinese operator offering services across the full nickel value chain – was mulling a project in Tanzania.

“President Subianto’s [Prabowo’s] policies – marked by resource nationalism and protectionism favouring domestic enterprises – have left some Chinese investors more cautious about committing capital today”, said Hu of the Shanghai Advanced Institute of Finance.

At the same time, “Chinese capital remains pivotal to its economic development, a reality well acknowledged by their government officials,” Hu said.

The future of the partnership

Despite Jakarta’s push for self-reliance, the numbers reveal a stark truth. According to the Chinese embassy in Jakarta, China has remained Indonesia’s largest trading partner for 13 consecutive years and had been among its top three sources of foreign direct investment over the past decade.

Bridging that dependence with domestic ambition, analysts noted that recent policy tightening marked an attempt to leverage Indonesia’s natural resources to bolster the domestic economy, rather than simply laying the groundwork for others to reap the benefits.

“Chinese enterprises expanding into Indonesia are undergoing a profound shift: their strategy is evolving from merely ‘going global’ to deeply embedding themselves within local industrial ecosystems,” said the Shanghai Academy of Social Sciences in its June report.

“In the near term, Indonesia will no longer offer a low-cost and low-threshold investment destination.”

Aaron Ang, non-executive director at Hong Kong-based cybersecurity firm Edvance, also flagged investment hurdles in Indonesia, including “a tangled regulatory landscape and skilled talent shortages”.

“Our strategy centres on collaborating with local commercial partners instead of competing against them. This approach will allow us to establish a lasting foothold there,” Ang said.

As Chinese firms recalibrate their local strategies amid mounting challenges, policymakers in Beijing and Jakarta are also stepping in to keep bilateral cooperation on track.

Responding to concerns about whether Chinese firms were becoming less welcome in Indonesia, Chinese foreign ministry spokesman Lin Jian said on Tuesday that Jakarta had “on multiple occasions expressed readiness to deepen cooperation with China on industrial and supply chains, and welcome Chinese enterprises to invest in the country”.

“We believe that Indonesia will continue to protect the lawful rights and interests of Chinese companies and foster a sound environment for investment and operation there,” Lin added.

During the World AI Conference in Shanghai earlier this month, Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi met Airlangga Hartarto, the Indonesian coordinating minister for economic affairs. The Southeast Asian nation became one of the founding members of the World AI Cooperation Organisation, a new China-led initiative promoting global artificial intelligence governance.

Wang said he hoped Indonesia would provide a fair and equitable business environment, while Airlangga stated that Indonesia had continued to welcome Chinese enterprises to invest.

“Over the long run, the key determinant of Chinese firms’ sustainable success in the country is to become a co-developer rather than remaining mere product exporters,” said the Shanghai Academy of Social Sciences in its report on Indonesia.

But navigating Jakarta’s evolving domestic priorities is likely to remain a delicate balancing act. Zhao from Renmin University said China and Indonesia’s complementary strengths had fostered an increasingly integrated economic partnership but cautioned the relationship was not without friction.

“Chinese enterprises must evolve to manage policy risks and adapt to the shifting strategic interests of the host country – this is a lesson every company going global must learn,” he said.
