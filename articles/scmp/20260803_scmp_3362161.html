The Philippines, sovereignty and the Pax Silica Industrial Hub – how US’ Helberg sees it

SCMP | FREE | 1291 words | 2026-08-03

The struggle between the US and China over who will lead in artificial intelligence is one of the defining competitions of our time. In the third part of our exclusive interview with a key player defining Washington’s policy on AI, Jacob Helberg, US undersecretary of state for economic affairs, gives a commitment to the Philippines on the Pax Silica Initiative.

Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos Jnr used his State of the Nation address in Manila on Monday to staunchly defend a proposed US-backed artificial intelligence hub, promising it would create “quality jobs” and inject new life into the economy.

The venture is the flagship project in a US-led coalition seeking to build China-free AI supply chains spanning critical minerals, semiconductors and logistics.

“A product of the 23-member strong and US-led Pax Silica Initiative, the Pax Silica Industrial Hub will create an ecosystem that will have AI at its core,” Marcos said.

“It will bring quality jobs to our people, accelerate our industrial competitiveness, and revitalise our economy.”

The promise is to establish an advanced manufacturing and logistics centre in the global AI and technology value chain.

But critics warn that the project could erode Philippine sovereignty, displace farming communities, and cause environmental harm.

In an exclusive interview with the South China Morning Post earlier this month, one of the US government architects of the Pax Silica Initiative rejected suggestions that US interests within the zone would have special treatment.

“We will ensure that the zones will uphold the sovereignty of the host country,” said Jacob Helberg, US undersecretary of state for economic affairs.

Helberg said the US administration was “still in the process of negotiating the shape of these long-term agreements” but the concerns of the Philippines and the investors would be met.

The new economic zone would provide “international investors with the kind of policy and regulatory certainty that they need in order to allocate large pools of capital to do a significant industrial buildout”.

The hub, he added, would also help “American companies secure strategic inputs that they need for their supply chains”.

We will ensure that the zones will uphold the sovereignty of the host country

Jacob Helberg, US undersecretary of state for economic affairs

Manila, a close US ally, joined the Pax Silica Initiative in April as repeated confrontations with Beijing in the disputed South China Sea worsened already-frayed relations between the two Asian nations.

The initiative is the framework for the proposed 1,619-hectare (4,000-acre) industrial hub in New Clark City, 100km (60 miles) north of Manila, the anchor project of the Luzon Economic Corridor – a trilateral connectivity project led by the United States, Japan and the Philippines.

The hub plans reflect deepening economic cooperation between Manila and Washington as they seek to reduce their dependence on Beijing for critical supply chains, including in rare earths, an area that Beijing dominates and is increasingly willing to leverage.

In May, Philippine officials said American officials had requested that the zone be placed under US jurisdiction, but they “did not agree to that”.

Helberg’s assurances were welcomed by Philippine ambassador to the United States Jose Manuel Romualdez, who said they affirmed that the “proposed economic security zone will uphold Philippine sovereignty as its host country”.

“This is fully consistent with the Philippine government’s position that all development initiatives within the country operate under the Philippine Constitution, Philippine laws, and Philippine jurisdiction.”

Retired American diplomat Edgard Kagan said he took Helberg “at his word” on sovereignty, adding that Washington fully understood the importance of “full respect” for Philippine jurisdiction.

“There is neither interest in nor precedent for the United States seeking sovereignty in an economic zone,” said Kagan, the US ambassador to Malaysia from December 2023 to February this year.

The economic zone also featured last week during US Secretary of State Marco Rubio’s visit to the Philippines for the Association of Southeast Asian Nations foreign ministers’ forum.

“From the South China Sea to Pax Silica and the Luzon Economic Corridor, the US-Philippine Alliance makes both our countries stronger, safer and more prosperous,” Rubio said after his meeting with Marcos in Manila.

As part of its commitment, Washington would deliver US$100 million in new foreign assistance for the Luzon corridor to upgrade infrastructure and advance logistics and energy projects, the State Department said.

“Secretary Rubio and President Marcos also discussed opportunities related to the Philippines’ membership in Pax Silica and hosting the initiative’s first Economic Security Zone in Clark,” the department said.

Romualdez said both sides were “committed to developing a framework under Pax Silica that advances mutually beneficial economic objectives”.

“The focus of both governments is to establish an investment environment that is competitive, transparent and predictable, enabling high-value industries to flourish and bilateral trade relations to thrive better than ever before,” he added.

Wendy Cutler, a senior vice-president at the Asia Society Policy Institute, said the plan suggested that the Donald Trump administration was “serious about experimenting with different and innovative approaches to build secure supply chains particularly when it comes to AI”.

“Unlike free trade agreements, this zone covers only a limited geographical area, does not include tariff-free reciprocal access, and seems to be limited in scope in terms of lifting restrictions and regulations,” she added.

Zack Cooper, a senior fellow at the American Enterprise Institute, said the details about the project were “still coming together” and he expected there would be “robust support from US policymakers to ensure that the Philippines benefits from this arrangement”.

“I think the critical thing here is that the US government has been consistent from [Joe] Biden to Trump in trying to invest in the Philippines to help demonstrate the value of the alliance in both Manila and Washington,” he said.

Despite assurances of a win-win partnership, the proposal has drawn criticism from opposition parties, environmental groups and farmer organisations.

“The dynastic government is just ripping us off. They brought in Pax Silica. They are seizing more than 4,000 hectares from farmers in Central Luzon,” labour rights leader Leody De Guzman was quoted as saying by Philippine news outlet Inquirer.net on Monday.

Activist groups say the project will displace farming communities, reduce agricultural land, and strain local water resources.

Supporters of the project, including the Philippine ambassador to the US, who is also Marcos’ second cousin, argue that it has the “potential to create hundreds of thousands of jobs over time while reinforcing the country’s long-term industrial competitiveness”.

“The AI-native industrial acceleration hub represents an important opportunity to strengthen the Philippines’ position in the global value chain for semiconductors, critical minerals processing, advanced manufacturing and artificial intelligence-enabled industries,” Romualdez said.

Neither side asks the harder question: not whether Pax Silica is desirable, but what is traded for it

Jewellord Nem Singh, Stimson Centre

The Philippines, the world’s second-largest nickel producer and biggest exporter of raw nickel ore, sends the bulk of its output to China and Chinese-owned processing facilities in neighbouring Indonesia.

The government hopes that the project will help the country move beyond exporting raw ore to China at low prices by developing domestic processing capacity, creating more value at home and supplying the US market.

However, in a Substack post on Monday, Jewellord Nem Singh, a non-resident fellow at the Stimson Centre, said one big issue was still to be addressed.

“Neither side asks the harder question: not whether Pax Silica is desirable, but what is traded for it,” Singh wrote.

“Used well, it could anchor the productive base the state has yet to build for its industrial goals. Used badly, as critics point out, it could simply become an export platform, for allied rather than Chinese buyers.”
