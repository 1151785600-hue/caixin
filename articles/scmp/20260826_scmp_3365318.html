China’s Xi Jinping expected to visit India after 7 years. What’s on the agenda?

SCMP | FREE | 1373 words | 2026-08-26

After two years of a cautious thaw, China and India are heading towards a pivotal test: can the relationship move beyond managed optics to meaningful engagement?

President Xi Jinping and Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi are expected to cross paths next week at the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) summit in Bishkek, Kyrgyzstan. Sources expect a short, tightly managed encounter on the sidelines of the August 31-September 1 summit that would set the stage for the main event – in India.

The Chinese foreign ministry confirmed on Wednesday that Xi would attend the SCO summit, while Modi is also expected to participate.

The fuller sit-down is planned for New Delhi from September 12-13, when Xi is expected to attend the Brics summit with a reported delegation of around 400. This will be the Chinese leader’s first visit to India in seven years and both sides are working hard to churn out deliverables.

The disputed China-India border is expected to be high on the agenda when Xi meets Modi in the Indian capital, alongside trade, investment and people-to-people ties, including visas, according to people familiar with the negotiations.

Beijing has not officially confirmed Xi’s India visit, saying only that China attaches importance to Brics cooperation and supports India in hosting the summit.

Beijing border talks pave the way

On Tuesday, Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi, Beijing’s special representative on the boundary question, co-chaired the 25th round of talks on the Himalayan border dispute with Modi’s principal security adviser and confidant, Ajit Doval.

In his opening remarks, Doval told Wang that the latest special representatives’ talks on the boundary question were “very important, particularly in the wake of the Brics summit to happen shortly”, referring to the grouping of major emerging economies.

Modi and Xi had already given “clear and positive direction” to the relationship at the Kazan Brics summit in October 2024 and the Tianjin SCO summit a year ago, Doval added.

The return of “normalcy”, Doval argued, was “a direct result of both sides maintaining peace and tranquillity in the border areas”.

The Indian foreign ministry later said the talks were “candid and in-depth” and that both sides agreed to keep seeking a political settlement “in the context of their overall and long-term interests”.

According to a read-out from the Chinese side, Wang told Doval that both sides should “place the border issue in its proper place within bilateral relations”, while urging that they “maintain forward momentum without pausing, regressing, or veering off course”.

Ahead of the border talks, Doval met Chinese Vice-President Han Zheng in Beijing. According to China’s state news agency Xinhua, Doval said the two countries, home to the world’s largest populations and among its fastest-growing major economies, were facing a “historic opportunity” to deepen cooperation.

The deliverables: border, trade, investment, visas

Sources say that when Xi sits down with Modi in New Delhi, the deliverables on offer are likely to be incremental, not a grand settlement.

“Delimitation”, or agreeing on paper where the boundary runs rather than simply managing troops along the Line of Actual Control – the disputed China-India border – could be among the issues discussed. Both the Indian embassy in Beijing and the Chinese foreign ministry used the term in referring to the talks between Doval and Wang.

In a social media post, the Indian embassy said that the two sides discussed making “progress on boundary delimitation”, while the read-out from Beijing said they discussed “advancing boundary delimitation negotiations”, alongside border management, improving institutional mechanisms, and cross-border cooperation.

The Indian ministry, however, used the broader formulation of seeking a “political settlement of the boundary question”.

Missing from this week’s readouts was “early harvest”, last year’s strategy of prioritising the less contentious stretches and putting off Ladakh and Arunachal Pradesh – which Beijing calls Zangnan or “south Tibet” – for later.

At the 24th special representatives’ round in New Delhi last August, Doval and Wang agreed to set up an expert group under the Working Mechanism for Consultation and Coordination on China-India Border Affairs to explore an “early harvest in boundary delimitation”.

The economic track, however, could offer more tangible wins. Under newly relaxed Indian rules, investors from countries sharing a land border with India – including China – can acquire non-controlling stakes of up to 10 per cent without government approval, barring specific restricted sectors.

A Xi-Modi summit could deliver political momentum to efforts to boost trade and investment and address the glaring trade deficit.

While tourist visas and direct flights have resumed, the return of Chinese journalists to India remains the last major piece on the negotiating table.

On visas, New Delhi has moved more slowly than Beijing, and sources said any restoration was expected to be tightly controlled and reciprocal, not a blanket move.

Managing expectations

Zhu Yongbiao, dean of the school of politics and international relations at Lanzhou University, described the Wang-Doval talks as a “positive signal” but called for sober realism without high expectations, noting that concrete actions from India mattered far more than its rhetoric.

On the border issue, Zhu remains considerably less optimistic. He expects the situation at best to “freeze or revert to the prior status quo”, and that “breakthrough progress remains unlikely”.

Substantive breakthroughs were unattainable without a “sober, objective shift” in India’s “self-perception” and regional strategy, he added.

Manoj Kewalramani of the New Delhi-based Takshashila Institution said: “Anyone looking for a breakthrough on difficult issues like the boundary is likely to be disappointed.

“A joint read-out would be noteworthy, but is very unlikely. The last two times that Modi and Xi have met, there has been no joint statement.”

He noted that although the two sides agreed in October 2019 to establish a high-level economic and trade dialogue, the initiative was derailed first by the pandemic and later by their deadly 2020 border stand-off.

“So some sort of new, formal mechanism for trade, investment and green energy technologies might be useful,” Kewalramani said, while ruling out any grand bargain.

“This is not the moment for grand bargains for either side,” he said. “What one can pursue is stability and building some linkages between the two countries that can serve the narrow basket of common interests.”

Antara Ghosal Singh of the Observer Research Foundation in New Delhi said that for India, a successful meeting would mean a stable border, secure upstream manufacturing supply chains, greater leverage in negotiations with the United States and more room to advance “Make in India” – a national initiative to become a design, innovation and manufacturing hub.

For China, she said, high-level contact with India could weaken US efforts to check and balance Beijing.

According to Singh, two key factors drive India’s stance – its deep supply-chain dependency on China and the unpredictability of Trump 2.0.

For China, she said the critical challenges were domestic economic headwinds, export dependence and deteriorating ties with major trade partners, from the US and European Union to India.

“Overall, the conditions are ripe in both New Delhi and Beijing for a deep reset in bilateral ties,” she said. “But both sides will be seeking to maximise their gains while reducing concessions. Surely, it is going to be a difficult negotiation.”

This week’s China-India talks reflected a broader thaw following tensions stemming from a clash in Ladakh’s Galwan Valley in 2020, in which at least 20 Indian soldiers were killed. China said four of its soldiers died in the fight, which triggered a prolonged military and diplomatic stand-off along the Line of Actual Control.

Relations began to improve after the two sides reached an understanding in October 2024 to resume patrolling in parts of their disputed border. Apart from the resumption of direct flights and visa arrangements, steps to restore broader exchanges have included the revival of the Kailash Mansarovar Yatra, the annual pilgrimage to Tibet for Indian nationals.

Last year, Modi met Xi on the sidelines of the August 31-September 1 SCO summit in Tianjin – the Indian leader’s first visit to China in seven years. The two leaders said China and India were development partners rather than rivals and agreed that the boundary issue should not define the broader relationship.
