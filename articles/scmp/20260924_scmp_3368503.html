Pacing problem: can AI fears overcome US-China race dynamics and force a slowdown?

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2306 words | 2026-09-24

When the presidents of the United States and China meet in Washington, the world will be watching to see whether their two countries can make good on their agreement to work towards “constructive strategic stability”. In the sixth part of a series, Chong Ming Lee and Vincent Chow examine what’s behind the call for “pacing” by American AI giants – effectively a slowdown in development – and how the phenomenon is being viewed in China.

After years of rapid development, leading US AI executives are proposing what would have been unthinkable even a year earlier: that it may be time to slow down.

In recent weeks, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, OpenAI chief Sam Altman and SpaceXAI founder Elon Musk have all called for “pacing” the development of frontier artificial intelligence over purported fears that its capabilities were advancing faster than the safeguards designed to control them.

On Wednesday, Amodei pushed further for a slowdown when he addressed the UN Security Council by video link, warning that mismanaged AI posed an existential threat to humanity and urging nations to unite on global safety standards.

These public statements come ahead of a highly anticipated summit between US President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping in Washington on Thursday, after US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Chinese Vice-Premier He Lifeng met in New York City on the weekend to discuss AI safety, among other issues.

Yet despite converging on the language of restraint, Silicon Valley’s AI leaders remain locked in a high-stakes and costly tech race against each other – and with China – with no clear winner in sight.

These dynamics, both domestically between industry players and globally between the world’s two AI superpowers, make it highly unlikely that a slowdown will happen any time soon, analysts told the South China Morning Post.

“Even the US companies themselves cannot agree on that,” said Victor Shih, a professor at the University of California San Diego and an expert on Chinese elite politics. “So certainly the US and China will not agree on that.”

It has been four years since Washington imposed wide-ranging controls on the export of advanced semiconductor technology to China, citing national security concerns. Yet Chinese AI models from the likes of DeepSeek and Moonshot AI continue to be competitive with their US counterparts in price and capability.

This, in turn, threatens the trillion-dollar valuations of OpenAI and Anthropic as they head towards initial public offerings, as well as challenging US claims of global AI leadership – an explicit priority of US President Donald Trump, who outright dismissed the calls for a slow down in AI development.

“AI taking over the world, destroying humanity, and all other things bad, is a hoax,” he wrote on social media last week, raising questions about whether the world can pace AI without the support of one of its most powerful leaders.

What is driving calls to slow down?

There have long been concerns about the existential risks of AI. Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio, winners of the Turing Award computer science prize and widely regarded as the “Godfathers” of AI, have both warned that the technology could eventually displace all humans, with Hinton saying earlier this month that a 10 per cent chance of AI killing all humans within a decade was not an “unreasonable” estimate.

While such concerns are legitimate, what sets apart the recent statements by industry leaders such as Amodei and Altman was that their AI safety proposals also served commercial and geopolitical ends, said Sun Chenghao, a fellow at Tsinghua University’s Centre for International Security and Strategy in Beijing.

Sun pointed to Amodei’s recent essay “We Must Pace the Frontier”, in which the Anthropic founder warned that a swarm of AI agents could take over the entire internet within six to 12 months, potentially causing “hundreds of billions of dollars in damage”.

While the essay laid out why Amodei believes pacing is needed, it also devoted a large part to advocating for tighter restrictions on China’s AI industry. These included US semiconductor chip export controls and a “crackdown on unauthorised distillation by companies” in “autocratic” China.

“Amodei explicitly connects pacing with preserving a US lead over China,” Sun said. That made it “difficult” for Chinese policymakers to view the move as “politically neutral”, the Tsinghua fellow added, even if Amodei acknowledged that “[global] pacing will require cooperation with China”.

Reaction in China to Amodei’s essay was dismissive, with many accusing the tech CEO of using the guise of AI safety to shape the rules governing the sector and to entrench Anthropic’s position as an industry leader.

Last week, the official newspaper of the Communist Party, People’s Daily, published a commentary accusing the US of seeking to monopolise frontier AI development, while a DeepSeek engineer invoked Nazi Germany in a blog post that criticised OpenAI and Anthropic for their latest pacing calls.

China was likely to draw a sharp distinction between AI safety and efforts it viewed as “AI containment”, according to Wei Sun, principal analyst for AI at Counterpoint Research. Beijing has recently unveiled new AI initiatives that call for greater AI safety measures and international cooperation on AI governance, including a new “AI Safety Governance Framework” standards document for the industry released last week.

Oskar Galeev, a researcher at Beijing-based AI safety consultancy Concordia AI, said the Chinese government had demonstrated that it increasingly cared about AI safety. But it would still require the US to deal with it on an “equal footing”, he said, rather than as a subordinate being dictated to by a stronger power.

“The more pacing is framed as a way to win geopolitical competition, the harder it becomes to cooperate on the risks both sides share,” he said.

Existential risks, practical realities

Geopolitics aside, even getting the US to pace its own industry would be a challenge, according to UC San Diego’s Shih. While the Anthropic and OpenAI founders have raised alarms about the existential risks of AI, they have also been rebuffed by fellow industry titans including Meta Platforms’ Mark Zuckerberg and Nvidia’s Jensen Huang, who dismissed such worries as “complete nonsense” at an event last week.

Huang dismissed such “doomerism” while on a New York Times podcast on Wednesday , arguing that fearmongering only slowed down industry adoption.

There are questions over how a pacing mechanism would be enforced given high levels of mistrust between US AI companies.

As Karen Hao chronicled in her award-winning 2025 book Empire of AI, Altman and Musk helped found OpenAI in 2015 because they did not trust Google to develop AI safely. Six years later, Amodei and other former OpenAI executives and researchers left to start Anthropic after losing trust in Altman to develop AI safely.

Musk, founder of Grok model developer SpaceXAI – formerly xAI – took Altman to court earlier this year after having long accused the OpenAI CEO of prioritising profits over AI safety.

Qian Zilan, a research scholar at the independent AI governance think tank GovAI, said US AI leaders claimed that they wanted to build safe AI but did not trust others to do the same. This created pressure to continue building powerful AI systems, driven by fears that slowing down would cede ground to a rival who would deploy a system with far fewer safeguards.

Further intensifying these dynamics are the massive investments being made by US and Chinese companies in underlying AI infrastructure, such as chips and data centres, with the pace of spending not slowing down.

According to a Morgan Stanley report published earlier this month, capital expenditure by Chinese AI hyperscalers was forecast to jump 121 per cent year on year in 2026 to 995 billion yuan (US$142 billion), outpacing the 41 per cent growth among US peers.

China has long been a laggard in terms of AI investment, but recent successes, including DeepSeek’s breakthrough moment in January 2025 and growing policy support from Beijing, have spiked investment levels.

Still, even as the gap narrowed, China was set to spend only around 17 per cent as much as the US on AI infrastructure over the five years to 2030, Morgan Stanley estimated. Given that China’s AI spending was a much smaller share of its economy than the US, it would not likely slow down its investment momentum, Lu Ting, managing director and chief China economist at Nomura Securities, said at a Fortune event in Guangzhou last week.

“I think the key question in China is whether it can obtain chips, how many chips it can get, and how many Nvidia chips it can buy,” he said.

With China not showing signs of slowing down, the US want to maintain its pace over fears of China fully catching up, said Shan Hui, chief China economist at Goldman Sachs, at the same event. “The next 12 to 18 months will not slow down, and may even accelerate, especially in terms of AI infrastructure,” she said.

Adding to investor frenzy is the rush from both leading American and Chinese AI companies to go public. Despite its high-profile safety warnings, Anthropic itself was still expected to go public later this year, with a targeted valuation of around US$2 trillion, Axios reported earlier this month, citing multiple sources.

In China, Kimi developer Moonshot AI has reportedly been valued at US$50 billion in an ongoing funding round and confidentially filed for a Hong Kong initial public offering, people with knowledge of the matter told SCMP earlier this month.

Meanwhile, GLM developer Z.ai, which is listed in Hong Kong, announced plans earlier this month to raise an additional US$5 billion through a combination of new shares and convertible bonds, just two months after raising US$4 billion in a share placement.

According to Tsinghua’s Sun, the new influx of capital and unprecedented investor interest made a collective slowdown difficult. “If one frontier company deliberately slows the development of its most capable models while its competitors continue to move ahead, it risks losing market share, capital, talent and technological leadership,” he said, adding that this also applies to countries.

Genuine safety fears

While there were legitimate concerns about the feasibility of pacing AI development, recent incidents involving AI agents demonstrating unprecedented cyber hacking capabilities had “captured the attention of both policymakers and the public”, said Scott Singer, co-director of the China AI Initiative at the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace.

Both OpenAI and Anthropic have in recent months released details about how their models hacked into other companies’ internal systems through “agentic swarms” that went undetected for weeks.

Besides industry warnings, calls for greater safeguards have also intensified among US and European politicians after former Anthropic and OpenAI researcher Jacob Coxon resigned from his role at Anthropic earlier this month and accused the companies of “gambling with our lives” with their plans to accelerate AI development.

Central to their plans was recursive self-improvement (RSI), Coxon warned, which involved automating the AI research and development process itself with the use of AI – a controversial practice that some experts say could lead to humans losing control of AI entirely.

Still, both American and Chinese AI industry leaders have in recent months described RSI as imminent. Given the AI hacking incidents, calls for restraint from industry should not be dismissed as “regulatory theatre,” said Counterpoint Research’s Sun, as the arguments for AI safety had become “much more concrete.”

According to Singer, AI’s growing threat to both US and Chinese interests and humanity as a whole could ultimately be what overrides geopolitical and commercial incentives to continue racing ahead.

“At this stage, voluntary pacing isn’t just feasible, it’s pragmatic [in] the face of serious risks,” Singer said. “Regardless of whether a model is developed in San Francisco or Shanghai, they can create risks that transcend borders.”

Even in its attacks on Amodei and Washington’s hawkish tech policy towards China, the People’s Daily commentary nonetheless called on Washington to work with Beijing to “manage risks through dialogue and communication” and “promote AI to benefit mankind”.

Wang Shengyu, a research assistant at the Asia Society Policy Institute’s Centre for China Analysis, said China’s dominant national security apparatus may be more sympathetic to calls for a slow down given the risks of AI expanding from cyberattacks to bioweapon development.

Last week, China’s top intelligence official Chen Yixin called for robust risk prevention frameworks and stronger global governance of the emerging technology in an article published in a journal run by the country’s Cyberspace Administration.

“Once the benefits and risks of frontier AI become concrete enough to create real policy trade-offs, the final judgment is likely to rest with President Xi [whether to slow down AI development]”, Wang said.

As the Xi-Trump meeting nears, experts cautioned against expecting a sweeping agreement on AI, saying any breakthrough would focus on narrower areas where US and Chinese interests overlapped.

A good deliverable from the summit would be for the two sides to agree to have more AI dialogue in future, while finding out what the common concerns over AI risks were, said Tsinghua’s Sun.

There are early signs that this may be possible. Following Sunday’s meeting, US Treasury Secretary Bessent said that the US and China had discussed a “US-China AI dialogue” mechanism and further meetings to discuss AI-related issues.

He added that the US side would “want a shared vision of common goals and threats” with China, including a proposed “notification mechanism” between the two countries.

“Just like any cross-border activity, moving from opaque to more transparency between the No 1 and No 2 AI powers in the world is very important,” Bessent said.
