Escape Manila’s heat for northern Luzon’s pine-scented hills, hanging coffins and other hidden wonders

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1555 words | 2026-09-21

Travellers rolling north through Metro Manila along the Skyway are treated to an elevated perspective of the Philippine capital. The land is pancake flat but the buildings – from bric-a-brac slums to gilded high-rises – reach for the heavens.

My partner and I, too, are headed higher. Our aim is simple – to escape the city heat and exchange the pervasive congestion for nights that aren’t polluted by light, and days by smog. The most obvious solution would be to take a ferry to some exotic isle – after all, the Philippines has 7,000 of them – but we’ve resolved to head in the opposite direction to the Boracay-bound sun-seekers, to the pine-scented hills of Luzon.

The first quarter of the day is spent on a Victory Liner coach, staring at an undulating sea of rice, with only the volcanic profile of Mount Arayat suggesting there may be mountains ahead.

Once we pass the city of Tarlac, lowland farms begin to submit to hill country, as the coach lurches from a cane-straight highway to a winding road that traces the contours of the land. The temperature cools with the altitude and the natural world evolves in tandem. Brilliant wildflowers line the passage north and menacing raptors circle overhead.

Eventually, we encounter the strangest of things in the tropics – forests of conifers, which dominate the slopes on the outskirts of what’s been aptly dubbed the City of Pines. Baguio, where we plan to spend the night, is a little over 200km (124 miles) from Manila. But it’s the altitude, not distance, that distinguishes it most from the capital.

At 1,400 metres (4,600 feet) above sea level, the Americans found a cool spot on a former Spanish ranchería to build a summer capital in the vogue of Shimla, in British India, and duly designed one of the country’s most pleasant cities. Lungfuls of pine-scented air explain why administrators from Boston and Chicago chose to escape here from the muggy oppressiveness of Manila during the worst months of the year.

As we circumvent Burnham Park – a public lake garden designed by architect Daniel Hudson Burnham that remains the city centrepiece – the laid-back atmosphere of Baguio eases our work-frenzied minds. Although many of the finest old houses have been lost to war and the catastrophic earthquake of 1990, a few period buildings – log and stone constructions designed to blend with the mountainous pine forest – still dot the neighbourhood.

Drawn northwest of the park by the imposing City Hall, we stumble upon Café by the Ruins, an American house bombed during World War II only to be reconstructed by local artists using indigenous materials such as bamboo and nipa, the sense of place enhanced by photographs of mountain tribes on the wall and a menu that mixes classic Filipino fare with international dishes. Tortang talong (grilled aubergine omelette with carabao cheese and tomatoes) washed down with tapuey – the local rocket fuel, made with rice, roots and ginger – kindles a satisfied glee at the end of a hard day’s travel.

About 1.5km (one mile) to the north, the Baguio Village Inn is a prim wooden lodge overlooking the city suburbs, which carpet a series of steep hills, resulting in topsy-turvy, chaotic little districts catering to a quickly expanding population.

The following day, morning light is creeping over the hills when we hop a Jeepney to the City Market in search of breakfast. We get more than we bargained for; mountain-grown strawberries are piled outside shop fronts throughout the labyrinthine bazaar, where peanut brittle, sweet wine and honey are offered for sale along with textiles from the ethnically diverse mountains stretching ahead of us for more than 320km (200 miles) and over six provinces.

A brass band appears, and marches through the throng, the trumpets and drums cultivating the feel of a fiesta, recalling the Spanish influence in the Philippine cultural mosaic. However, Iberian colonists didn’t make it far into the cordilleras and mountain peoples were largely spared the corrupting influences of the outside world until the completion of the American-made Halsema Highway, in 1930.

It is along this 96-year-old road that we embark on the next leg of the journey. You can’t pre-book, we just have to wait for a minivan ride at the Sto. Niño Bus Terminal.

Although, not for long; we’re soon travelling through villages in eyeshot of the terraced hills that turn whole mountainsides into a laddered patchwork of rice fields. It’s a stunning vista, but our driver – a 20-something heavy smoker with a novelty skull dangling from the rear-view mirror – handles the minivan as though he’s driving in the Macau Grand Prix. It’s a roller-coaster trip that ends only after six hours of bouncing and clinging on for dear life, when we’re unceremoniously deposited roadside in Sagada.

This remote mountain settlement found its way onto the hippie trail with the arrival of electricity, in the early 1970s, after which intellectuals flocked here to write, paint and make merry on tapuey. Western beatniks followed, “and the artistic influence has left its mark in the form of little boho cafes and inns and a distinctly laid-back atmosphere”, according to my guidebook.

Grandma’s Yellow House is not quite “boho”, but homely and convivial nonetheless. Members of the owning family are cooking and offer us food when we arrive. We have a second-floor room with thick blankets on the beds – it gets cold at night – and our own bathroom, with running hot water some of the time. It’s not the Ritz, but not bad given the remoteness of Sagada.

The town, it turns out, is no longer the getaway for scribes and bohemians it once was. What Sagada can claim to be, though, is blissful, the alpine cool and whispering pines a balm for travellers with stiff limbs and sore buttocks. The town is dotted with weaving houses - selling everything from traditional scarves to guitar straps - and cafes serving local specialities, notably lemon pie.

As the sun sets, the plummeting temperature impels us to return to Grandma’s Yellow House for an early night.

The following day, having risen with the cock crow – literally – we rendezvous with Wihlis Talanay on the terrace of the building that houses Saga Tours a little after sunrise. Inside, our guide for the day offers us a variety of hikes, cave tours and terraced mountain viewings. We opt for a tour to Echo Valley and its famous hanging coffins.

After compulsory registration with the tourist office, he walks us to the church that overlooks Sagada, a not uncommon sight in a Catholic country, except that this one, the Church of Saint Mary the Virgin, “is Anglican, the Spanish never got here”, explains Talanay.

“It’s the Americans who introduced Christianity, this church was built in 1904.”

A Protestant church might be out of the ordinary but the traditional burial sites set on the cliffs of Echo Valley are truly extraordinary.

“The hanging coffins were hung up high as it was believed the higher the dead were placed, the greater chance of their spirits reaching a higher nature in the afterlife,” Talanay explains of a tradition that is fast disappearing.

The coffins are those of distinguished members of the Kankanaey (an ethnic grouping of the Igorot people) and are in various states of repair. They are jammed into crevices, roped to outcroppings and balanced on brackets on sheer cliff faces as if floating. Some have been broken into by foraging animals, the skulls and bones visible as if the dead were preparing to rise.

Our walk across the rooftop of the Philippines proceeds along a mountain track deep in the forest, which is home to cloud rats, forest mice, warty pigs and deer, according to Talanay. Hunting and timber were the economic mainstays in Sagada but recent decades have seen the introduction of cash crops, most notably coffee.

“Welcome to Sagada’s Starbucks,” says Talanay, pointing to the thatch-roofed Baw-eng Coffee Shop, which has a backdrop of vegetation, stands on a coffee farm and serves mountain-grown brews to thirsty hikers. We pair ours with lemon-cream cookies.

Fortified with caffeine, we trek on into the forest. The path climbs then descends to follow a stream, leading through dark caves and patches of upland meadow until it opens onto a clearing drenched in midday sunlight. Butterflies flit everywhere, birdsong chorusing from the tree canopy.

We could walk indefinitely in temperatures this conducive and surroundings this seductive, the pull of nature impelling us towards wherever it is Talanay is leading us, for we have no destination in mind. The act of walking is enough.

Like so many Filipinos, Talanay once travelled overseas to find work, in his case to Britain. No doubt it was hard to make a living in Sagada but, as the last leg of the hike takes us out of the forest to cultivated land marked by a wooden fence, the mud path becoming a narrow stone road, I begin to understand why he returned to this pristine corner of Earth.

We conclude our hike, as all good hikes should end, in a pool beneath a waterfall – feeling a lot farther from Manila than the 400km a crow would fly.
