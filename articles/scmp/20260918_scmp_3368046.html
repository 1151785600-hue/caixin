Singapore minister Chan Chun Sing’s Mandarin diplomacy strikes China chord

SCMP | FREE | 1011 words | 2026-09-18

Singapore Defence Minister Chan Chun Sing’s use of Mandarin and Chinese philosophy in his speech at a major security forum in Beijing has won him praise on Chinese social media, while shedding light on how cultural familiarity and language can be diplomatic tools for the city state.

Videos of Chan at the Beijing Xiangshan Forum quoting ancient philosopher Mencius, or Mengzi in Mandarin, have gone viral on platforms such as Weixin and Baidu, with users hailing his command of the language and knowledge of Chinese thought.

Observers say that through resonating with the hosts, Chan managed to convey Singapore’s sterner message of a call for major powers to build trust instead of coercion, though there is a risk that linguistic bonds may raise the expectation for Singapore to sympathise with Beijing’s interests.

At the summit on Wednesday, attended by delegates from nearly 100 countries and helmed by Chinese defence chief Dong Jun, Chan spoke mostly in English but switched to Mandarin occasionally and cited Mencius, saying: “Power may compel others to comply. But in the end, it is trust that truly wins others over.”

Social media users also drew comparisons between Chan and US Secretary of Defence Pete Hegseth, praising the former for displaying wisdom which exceeded that of a counterpart helming the military of a much larger country.

On Chan’s use of Mandarin, a Weixin user commented: “This minister is not an ordinary person; he knows Chinese culture like the back of his hand.”

Many also said Chan’s Mandarin was proof that the language and Chinese culture were gaining traction on the global stage.

The Singapore minister also urged superpowers to shoulder higher expectations from smaller states, stressing that countries should strengthen their own security but not at the expense of others.

“The more responsible major powers are, the less concerned small countries will be,” he said in Mandarin.

Singapore welcomed China’s aspiration to be a “responsible major country”, Chan said, adding that Beijing’s “reform and opening up” policy had contributed greatly to regional and global prosperity.

“As the People’s Liberation Army approaches its centenary, there is much potential for it to make a positive contribution to global stability and security,” he said.

Diplomatic move?

Observers hail Chan’s use of Mandarin and historical references as a clever diplomatic move, showcasing an appreciation and understanding of China and its culture.

Stefanie Kam, an assistant professor at S. Rajaratnam School of International Studies (RSIS), described Chan’s switch to Mandarin as deliberate and a “clear effort to show cultural awareness and diplomatic tact”.

“For a defence minister to quote an old Chinese classic like this is quite strategic; it makes it hard for the Chinese audience to disagree because they would be disagreeing with their own classic,” she said.

Adam Liu, a political scientist and assistant professor at the Lee Kuan Yew School of Public Policy, pointed out that Chinese leader Xi Jinping often quoted ancient Chinese teachings.

“Speaking in Mandarin and drawing on Mencius is a way of saying: I am addressing you in your own idiom, on your own terms. That tends to land well,” he said.

While Chan’s Mandarin switch-up was largely well received, some netizens were unimpressed, arguing that it should be expected that Chan – who hails from a country where 74 per cent of citizens were of Chinese ethnicity – would be fluent in Mandarin.

As English strengthens its hold as the main language for Singaporeans, the government has reinforced the importance of maintaining a bilingual edge, implementing measures such as public speaking contests and cultural workshops. According to a 2020 census, the use of Mandarin as the language most frequently spoken at home fell from 35.6 per cent in 2010 to 29.9 per cent in 2020.

Dylan Loh, associate professor of foreign policy at Singapore’s Nanyang Technological University, said the speech showed Singapore’s bilingualism and an ability to code-switch.

“Beyond citing Mencius specifically, the broader context in which it was used is also important,” he said. “The message was respectful and paid compliment to Chinese history and philosophical heritage.”

But Loh said the code-switch was not necessarily a strategic move in itself, noting that leaders had in the past done the same at various forums.

Chong Ja Ian, an associate professor of political science at the National University of Singapore, warned that the Chinese public could view affirmations of culture and ethnic similarities as synonymous with political alignment.

While using a Chinese philosophical expression “may be tactically helpful to smooth ties”, Chong said it could also “create disappointment when a person or the entity they represent take positions that diverge from prior expectations associated with race, culture and politics”.

On Chinese social media, some users interpreted Chan’s speech as veiled criticism of the United States, though observers note the script was vague and aligned with Singapore’s foreign policy.

In his speech, Chan said: “We must work even harder to strengthen our domestic fundamentals, rather than try to externalise our domestic issues.” He also illustrated Singapore’s journey to the global stage since its independence in 1965, through which “leaders avoided exploiting foreign policy for political gain”.

While Chan did not explicitly name a country, some online users who appraised Chan’s speech likely interpreted the reference to winning by force as implying American actions, Kam said.

Benjamin Ho, an assistant professor and coordinator at the China Programme at RSIS, said Chan’s speech was broad and in line with the city state’s “pro-Singapore” policy.

“It’s not surprising the Chinese read this as a reference to the US, but the case that China’s domestic issues are not absent in its foreign policy can also be made,” he said. “If you look past the idioms, which are obviously for the Chinese audience, it’s general and mostly speaking on principle.”

Liu said Mencius’ quote was consistent with Singapore’s non-alignment. “The hope is for an international order built on trust, rules and moral principles, which is a position one can hold without pointing at anybody.”
