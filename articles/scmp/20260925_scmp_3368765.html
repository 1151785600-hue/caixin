Indonesian coal deal fuels doubts over production quota approvals

SCMP | FREE | 1173 words | 2026-09-25

A major coal deal in Indonesia is set to secure a powerful new partner for one of the country’s biggest miners, even as the industry worries a delay in the government’s approval of production quotas could have a severe impact on the business outlook.

Low Tuck Kwong and his daughter Elaine Low, the biggest shareholders of Bayan Resources, agreed on September 16 to sell a 30 per cent stake in Indonesia’s third-largest coal producer to fellow tycoon Andi Syamsuddin Arsyad, better known as Haji Isam.

The deal comes at a sensitive moment for Bayan after the government delayed approval of additional coal quotas for three of its subsidiaries. The uncertainty sent Bayan’s shares sharply lower in the days before the deal announcement. It rose by about 20 per cent as soon as news broke about the stake deal, hitting the upper auto-reject limit.

The timing has fuelled speculation that the quota hurdle might be linked to the deal, a suggestion that the government has rejected.

Under the conditional agreement, the Lows would sell 10 billion Bayan shares to Jhonlin Baratama, a subsidiary of Haji Isam’s Jhonlin Group, a commodities and logistics conglomerate.

The deal would give Jhonlin a substantial stake in a major Indonesian coal producer. Bayan produced 68 million tonnes of coal last year, compared with Jhonlin’s output of 24 million tonnes.

“Acquiring a major stake in a top-three coal producer like Bayan fundamentally rewires our energy landscape. The state must ensure this consolidation doesn’t birth a mega-cartel,” said Dipo Satria Ramli, an economist at the Centre of Reform on Economics.

Indonesian media have described Haji Isam as an ally of the president. In 2025, Prabowo awarded him the Bintang Mahaputera Utama, one of Indonesia’s highest state honours.

Haji Isam has set his sights on expanding Jhonlin’s coal output after having applied for a 60-million-tonne production quota, known as Rencana Kerja dan Anggaran Biaya (RKAB) in Indonesia, for the rest of this year.

Ahmad Zuhdi Dwi Kusuma, principal at Energy Shift Institute, an independent energy finance think tank, said Jhonlin’s 30 per cent stake in Bayan would strengthen its market position.

“Haji Isam can connect the existing ecosystem he has under his group to Bayan,” he said.

Assuming Jhonlin was successful in acquiring its new 60-million-tonne quota, which was separate from the Bayan quota, its output combined with Bayan’s could account for about one-eighth of Indonesia’s coal market, Zuhdi said.

Details about the deal remain scant, pending further announcements from Bayan.

The Lows currently hold 62 per cent of Bayan’s shares. If the 30 per cent stake sold to Jhonlin comes entirely from their holdings, they would be left with about 32 per cent, potentially leaving control of the company uncertain.

But Ricky Ho, chief investment officer and portfolio manager at Jakarta-based Four Capital, a family office investment firm, said control of Bayan would likely remain with the Lows after the sale.

“Like most Indonesian conglomerate families, they have a lot of nominees in the rest of the 38 per cent stock.”

Under Indonesia’s RKAB system, mining companies must obtain government approval for their production volume. Without an approved quota, miners cannot legally produce beyond an amount authorised by the government.

On September 11, the three Bayan subsidiaries notified customers and invoked force majeure, citing the Ministry of Energy and Mineral Resources’ failure to issue additional production quota permits for the rest of 2026.

“The approval has not been issued to the company’s subsidiaries, although applications for approval to amend the RKAB have been submitted in accordance with applicable laws and regulations,” Bayan director Low Yi Ngo wrote in a statement.

Without the permits, Bayan’s production in 2026 is expected to total 39 million tonnes, 43 per cent lower than last year.

When Bayan announced the force majeure status to the Indonesian Stock Exchange on September 14, its shares fell by as much as 9.2 per cent that day.

The company eventually received approval for the subsidiaries’ quotas on Wednesday, a week after the deal was announced.

The episode highlighted how coal mining operations could be severely curtailed because of delays in government approval of production volumes, according to analysts.

Without the RKAB, Bayan would face difficulties in planning its production and budget, Zuhdi said. With its quota cut by almost half, it had no choice but to declare force majeure on its existing obligations to clients, he added.

“This serves as a reminder that the regulatory side of the industry in Indonesia is increasingly the be-all and end-all factor for a miner.”

According to a report by The Jakarta Post, the government has faced questions over whether Bayan’s permit delays were linked to Haji Isam’s acquisition. Other Indonesian media outlets highlighted that the quotas were approved shortly after the deal was signed.

This Week in Asia has reached out to Bayan and Jhonlin for comment.

Ho urged caution over drawing a link between the two developments.

When questioned by reporters in Jakarta on September 16 on why Bayan’s permits were not issued on the same day as the deal announcement on September 16, the director general of minerals and coal at the Ministry of Energy and Mineral Resources, Tri Winarno, said: “It’s still being evaluated and examined in terms of technical aspects, so it will take time.”

He also pointed out that not all of Bayan’s permits were held up. “From all Bayan’s coal subsidiaries, only three are having their permits re-evaluated. So there’s no deliberate targeting here.”

Bayan Resources has 16 subsidiaries – all of which have separate permits to mine coal – covering 126,000 hectares in East and South Kalimantan.

Energy Minister Bahlil Lahadalia on Wednesday rejected any connection between Bayan’s quota crisis and its stake sale. “The two were separate processes with no link whatsoever,” he told reporters.

Nonetheless, the developments have left market participants worrying about uncertainty over regulatory decisions and their impact on the value of Indonesian mining assets.

Calling it an “unpalatable aftertaste” for investors, Ho said: “The optics remain remarkable because a regulatory approval becomes critical to production, the company declares force majeure, a major ownership transaction is signed, and the regulatory uncertainty clears almost immediately.”

Dipo said the developments would affect investors’ perceptions of official transparency. “Investors will be even more cautious about potential government intervention in the future,” he added.

Katherine Hasan, a researcher at the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air, said that beyond the deal, Bayan and others in the coal industry would have to balance between staying competitive even as the Indonesian government sought to increase the proportion of energy production from green sources.

The Prabowo administration is seeking to phase out all coal and fossil fuel power plants by 2040 and increase solar energy production to 100 GW by 2029.

While Bayan and its peers were aiming to bolster their cash flows by increasing domestic coal production, they faced the “political reality” of Jakarta seeking to curb its fuel subsidies and enhance the country’s long-term energy security.
