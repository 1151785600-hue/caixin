US midterm shift could squeeze China on AI, but bring clarity to bilateral talks

Beijing has vowed to continue working with Washington towards a “constructive relationship of strategic stability” following the Democrats’ sweeping victory in the US midterm elections on Wednesday.

But analysts said a Democratic-controlled US Congress would ramp up pressure on Beijing over Taiwan, artificial intelligence (AI) and export controls, though it might also eliminate uncertainties in negotiations.

When asked by reporters about the results on Wednesday, Mao Ning, spokeswoman for China’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs, pledged to continue to work with Washington towards a constructive relationship of strategic stability based on respect, fairness and reciprocity.

She described the election as an “internal matter” for the US and said China stood ready to work with Washington to implement the consensus reached by Chinese President Xi Jinping and his US counterpart Donald Trump to “enhance communication, properly manage differences and expand cooperation”.

Trump’s Republican Party lost control of both the House of Representatives and the Senate, handing Democrats the power to block parts of his legislative agenda and intensify congressional scrutiny of his administration’s policies, including its approach to China.

Wu Xinbo, dean of the Institute of International Studies at Fudan University in Shanghai, said the power shift would certainly affect the China-US relationship.

“One possibility is that the Democratic Party would put pressure on Trump regarding the Taiwan issue, criticising him for betraying Taiwan and developing ties with Beijing and pushing Trump to resume arms sales to Taiwan,” Wu said.

The United States paused a US$14 billion arms sale to Taiwan before Trump and Xi met in May. The US is considering postponing the announcement of major arms sales to Taiwan until after November’s Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (Apec) summit in Shenzhen, the South China Morning Post reported in September.

“The US-China Board of Investment may discuss easing restrictions on Chinese investment in the US, but the Democratic Party has the backing of labour unions, so it seeks to protect many industries, particularly the automotive industry. So they would also oppose this,” Wu said.

In September, 27 House Democrats sent a letter to Trump to urge him not to open the US market to Chinese carmakers through imports, local manufacturing or other arrangements ahead of Xi’s state visit to the US.

Beijing and Washington agreed to establish a US-China Board of Investment during Trump’s summit with Xi in Beijing in May. The White House announced that the mechanism had been established during their second summit in Washington in September, providing a government-to-government forum to discuss investment opportunities and barriers.

Wu said the House Select Committee on Strategic Competition Between the United States and the Chinese Communist Party would also play up bilateral flashpoints, which would in turn influence US public opinion, policy and legislation on China.

On US-China trade talks, Wu said Democratic control of Congress might benefit these talks because the Democrats “will limit Trump constantly playing the tariff card”.

“I feel this might actually be a good thing [for China],” he said.

His view was echoed by Feng Chucheng, founder and partner at Hutong Research, a Beijing-based strategic consultancy, who said a Democratic-controlled Congress would eliminate uncertainties in US-China negotiations.

Feng noted that Congress could constrain the White House’s China policy by influencing the implementation of technology and export restrictions, particularly by denying Trump legislative waivers or exemptions that a Republican-controlled Congress might have been willing to grant. Such waivers, or even delays in implementing certain restrictions, would be valuable bargaining chips for Trump in negotiations with Beijing.

“From Beijing’s perspective, this actually removes a significant element of uncertainty from the US-China negotiating equation. Beijing no longer has to factor in the possibility that Trump could secure congressional waivers or exemptions in exchange for Chinese concessions,” Feng said.

He said a Democratic-controlled House would have limited ability to shape the leader-level diplomacy between Trump and Xi. Feng added that the US president retained considerable discretion to negotiate with his Chinese counterpart, but Trump’s ability to deliver on certain promises to Beijing would be more constrained.

“This makes the boundaries of any potential US-China deal clearer, even if it narrows the scope for bargaining,” Feng said.

In addition to their reunion in Shenzhen later this month, Xi and Trump are set to meet in Miami for a Group of 20 (G20) summit in December.

Zhao Minghao, a professor at Fudan University’s Institute of International Studies, called the election results so far a “blue tsunami” and a “big win” for the Democrats that would turn Trump into a “lame duck”.

Zhao said a Democratic-controlled Congress was likely to launch sweeping investigations into Trump, his family and senior officials once it convened in January, pushing back on the president’s agenda across legislation and the budget.

Zhao suggested that the midterm elections would not change the overall direction of Washington’s China policy, since stepping up strategic competition with Beijing was a bipartisan consensus. But the results could affect the tools Washington uses.

“For one, a Democratic-led Congress will certainly further intensify pressure on China on Taiwan, AI and export controls,” he said.

“Democrats will also scrutinise the Trump administration’s China policy more closely, including how it conducts diplomacy with Beijing. All of this will affect how the executive branch handles relations with China in the next phase.”

As artificial intelligence – from data centres to regulation – took centre stage in the midterm elections, Zhao said the debate was likely to become more closely tied to US-China competition once the Democrats take control of Congress.

“The Democrats may instrumentalise China policy as a tool to pressure Trump. This is a very big challenge for Beijing,” he said.

“Going forward, the decline of Trump’s personal political authority will also pose a major challenge to the new positioning of a strategically stable relationship that China and the US have formed … and the progress made on it.”

Chen Fengying, a Beijing-based expert on US-China relations, said election results could have implications for Trump’s foreign policy, particularly in the Middle East and the Pacific.

She said the US economy was relatively strong apart from inflation, describing this election as one “tied to Trump’s Middle East policy”.

With Democrats gaining ground and placing greater constraints on the president, efforts to resolve the Iran issue could accelerate, according to Chen.

“His room [to] manoeuvre in foreign policy will narrow, and US foreign policy could become more restrained. I don’t believe he will stir up trouble in the Pacific again,” Chen said.
