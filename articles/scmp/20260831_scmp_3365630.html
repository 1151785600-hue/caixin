Singapore’s blueprint for economic success: plan, but be prepared to adapt

SCMP | FREE | 1303 words | 2026-08-31

In the discussion on Hong Kong’s first ever five-year plan, much ink has been spilled on the merits of a planned economy. The most recent example of this was Anthony Rowley’s commentary that cited Singapore as an example of a highly successful planned economy.

Unfortunately, he misrepresents what planning in Singapore is about. There are lessons that Hong Kong can draw from how the Singapore government navigated a changing environment – but not the ones Rowley highlights.

It is quite understandable why an observer would view Singapore as a planned economy. After all, signs of order are all around. Nearly 80 per cent of Singaporeans live in public housing; the city is well-designed, green, and manicured. Clearly, this is a city that has been meticulously planned and engineered for decades.

Above all, Singapore’s politics is carefully managed by a ruling party that has been in power since 1959, and political dissent is often constrained by a paternalistic state.

But the idea of the Singapore economy as a top-down, dirigiste and planned one is a caricature at best. While various government agencies in Singapore engage in planning (loosely defined), the city state has never had five or 10-year plans, nor set production targets the way the Eastern bloc countries and China did.

Policies, not plans

Singapore’s first finance minister and deputy prime minister Goh Keng Swee was openly disdainful of socialist planning, saying that “planning as we know it has a limited value. Economic policy is more important”.

In economic policy, perhaps the most important difference between the Singapore economy and Hong Kong’s is the role of the state in industrial development.

Since the establishment of the Economic Development Board (EDB) in 1961, the Singapore state has promoted manufacturing as one of the key engines of the economy. Even today, manufacturing output comprises an astonishing 19 per cent of Singapore’s GDP, compared with less than 1 per cent in Hong Kong.

The EDB – or any other economic agency – has never produced five-year plans, so that cannot be the reason for Singapore’s success in industrialisation and industrial upgrading. But thanks to industrial policy, Singapore is a key node in global value chains in various high value-added industries such as electronics, petrochemicals, precision engineering, and pharmaceuticals and biotech to name a few.

The lesson from Singapore here is that five-year plans are much less important than specific policies that drive industrial diversification and upgrading.

A plan is simply a declaration of intent; whether that intent becomes reality depends on the right policies being formulated and how they are implemented. It is also quite common for governments to come up with bold visions and ambitious intents; what is much rarer is the capacity to execute change effectively.

With industrial policy, this means ensuring sound macroeconomic fundamentals and having the right microeconomic tools – such as tax incentives, foreign labour allowances, vocational training and worker upgrading programmes – to develop the desired industries.

But none of this was static or unchanging: external shocks and changing domestic conditions often necessitated changes to Singapore’s economic policies in ways that no five-year plan can foresee.

Quick to adapt

A second common error is to mistake the precision of the plan for its validity or quality. Even in a free-market economy like Hong Kong’s, there is a fascination bordering on obsession with setting precise targets or key performance indicators (KPIs) for government. The logic is that since much of what government does not lend itself easily to market metrics, targets or KPIs impose the necessary discipline on government.

This obsession is not always healthy. A target may be very precise, but it could also measure the wrong thing. This is not to diminish the value of planning, but to emphasise the importance of adapting quickly to changing conditions.

Being large bureaucracies, governments are often creatures of habit; their behaviours do not change quickly or easily even when circumstances require them to do so.

You just start it and hope for the best

Goh Keng Swee, Singapore’s first finance minister

When asked in 1992 if he had started out with a master plan in mind, Goh replied: “I had no initial vision. You just start it and hope for the best.” In many respects, Singapore’s economic success was the result of constant adaptation and improvisation.

For example, Singapore’s original economic plan was import-substitution industrialisation. This was one of the motivations for Singapore to join the newly established Malaysian federation in 1963 – to create a common market.

When Singapore separated from Malaysia in 1965, the plan was clearly no longer workable. Singapore adapted quickly and embraced export-oriented industrialisation, laying the foundations for the highly globalised economy it became a decade or so later.

Another example of adaptation was the practice of adjusting Central Provident Fund (CPF) contribution rates as a macroeconomic stabilisation tool. Singapore has, on various occasions, adjusted employer contribution rates in response to changes in macroeconomic conditions – lowering them to maintain cost competitiveness when faced with negative demand shocks, and raising them to deal with wage and inflation pressures.

Pay attention to process

Finally, the real value of a plan lies in the process rather than the plan itself.

A five-year plan is limited in at least two ways. First, the relatively short time horizon means that it is almost inevitable that the plan would be a collection of (achievable) goals that the various parts of the government have already set for themselves. It is unlikely to contain much that departs from existing priorities.

Second, because the emphasis of a five-year plan is to come up with specific deliverables, it is unlikely to catalyse a creative (re-)imagination of the future. Five-year plans are usually linear extrapolations of present trends, with targets adjusted marginally upwards to reflect the government’s enthusiasm in new areas – such as artificial intelligence.

Governments often overestimate their ability to change things in the short term but underestimate it over the long term. This bias informs Singapore’s approach to planning. Because much of what a government does on a routine basis is short term, the two main forms of planning that the Singapore government undertakes adopt a much longer time horizon. The emphasis is also on long-term strategy rather than near-term deliverables.

In city planning, for instance, the Urban Redevelopment Authority’s concept plan is reviewed once every decade and covers a time horizon of 50 years. Meanwhile, its master plan guides the city’s physical development over the next 10 to 15 years.

Since the turn of the century, the city state has also embraced scenario planning as a way of thinking (creatively) about the future.

Unlike five-year plans, the main goal of scenario planning is not to come up with a single plan for government with specific goals, targets or KPIs. Neither is scenario planning meant to produce predictions or forecasts. Instead, it is to generate distinct, plausible stories about the future (even if they seem improbable), question long-held assumptions, and surface the challenges and opportunities in the various scenarios.

Unlike five-year plans that are based on a particular view of the future, scenario planning is a much more divergent process that aims to anticipate various possible futures.

More recently, the Singapore government has expanded its capabilities in what it calls “futures thinking”.

It set up a Centre for Strategic Futures about a decade ago to “build a strategically agile public service ready to manage a complex and fast-changing environment”. The centre’s logo, with a black swan embedded, reflects its ambition to develop the government’s foresight and its ability to adapt.

This idea of planning as thinking creatively about the future is about as far as one can get from the world of five-year plans.
