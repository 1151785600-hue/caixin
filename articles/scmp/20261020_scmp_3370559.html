TV showman Robert Chua spells out his vision for his vast collection of Chinese antiquities

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2950 words | 2026-10-20

Robert Chua Wah-peng does not have the air of a stereotypical collector.

He does not demand of you the donning of cotton gloves or scrubbing of fingernails before touching his Chinese antiquities; he excitedly invites you to pick up vases and vessels with your bare hands; he encourages you to engage with his acquisitions up close. Most of them are not sequestered away behind glass or acrylic.

He also does not have an exact number for how large his collection is.

“Could be hundreds of thousands of pieces,” he muses when asked.

Originally from Singapore, Chua began collecting in 1967 – the year he arrived in Hong Kong – but it was not until his first visit to mainland China, in 1979, that he got serious about his hobby. He became a regular presence at the antiques dealers’ shops on Hong Kong’s Hollywood Road, sweeping through new shipments twice a week for decades, like clockwork, buying entire lots – everything from late-Qing ceramics bearing illustrated foo dogs in bright orange and terracotta “stickmen” from the Han period to wooden mooncake moulds and snuff bottles.

In his home in Kowloon, he has a room dedicated to blue-and-white porcelain from the Ming and Qing dynasties to the Republic era, rows of Chinese wedding cabinets heaving with tableware, wardrobes bursting with vibrant stoneware and jars, a corridor with more than 180 horse sculptures, shelves groaning beneath fossilised dinosaur eggs and Neolithic pottery, and drawers holding delicate jade accessories and brass buttons. Neolithic, Warring States, Han, Sui, Tang, Song, Yuan, Ming, Qing – Chua’s collection reads like a chronicle. Inching along narrow pathways cleared between stacks for necessity’s sake, he is talking a mile a minute, propelled by obvious fervour, sharing historical facts, stories about how he obtained an artefact, the technique used to make it.

Chua is an oddity among his peers in the collecting community in that his incentive to collect really isn’t that deep. “Honestly, I don’t know,” he shrugs. “I just love beautiful things. Never do I buy anything and at any moment say, ‘I bought this for HK$1,000. One day, maybe it will fetch me HK$5,000.’ Never. I buy for the beauty. And if I see something that is lovely, I’ll buy 30 of them, to share with people.”

Among Chua’s extensive accumulation, one theme stands out, not only in quantity but also in peculiarity: his collection of ancient Chinese mingqi – “spirit objects”, traditionally placed in tombs as funerary items to comfort the deceased in the afterlife. As far as the eye can see, there are small-scale models of dwellings, animals, furniture and carriages, as well as figures of guardians and attendants.

For visitors familiar with Chinese customs, it might come as a shock to see Chua living among mingqi. Despite their exquisite craftsmanship and three-colour sancai glaze, keeping burial goods intended for the dead in one’s home is thought to invite misfortune and disruptive yin energy into the household.

Chua is aware of the taboo, but it doesn’t faze him. “I am a bit Western in that sense,” he says. “Western collectors don’t mind antiques like these, but Chinese collectors are too superstitious to want them, so that made it easier for me to acquire them; the demand was lower and they were not difficult to source.

I have thousands of happy spirits at home. I’m not worried about anything – there’s so much company. It’s always a party

Robert Chua

“But I’m happy. I have thousands of happy spirits at home. I’m not worried about anything – there’s so much company. It’s always a party,” he laughs.

Indeed, Chua has a penchant for entertaining and hosting. His calendar is ordinarily filled with visits, events, meetings, lunches and dinners, both personal and professional. He likes to receive guests in the sunroom. He has a talent for driving conversations, and his candour and quick wit work in tandem to put people at ease.

To understand and appreciate Chua’s gravitational pull towards people and telling stories, taking a gander at his own history pulls back the curtains.

Chua, 80, made his name in television broadcasting. His first job, as a property assistant at ADS Channel 7 in Adelaide, Australia, in 1964, sparked an enduring fascination with the industry. After rotating through several positions at the Australian network, he returned to Singapore two years later, to produce for Radio Television Singapore, gaining experience in light entertainment.

It wouldn’t be long before another door opened for Chua: he was offered the role of senior production executive at Hong Kong’s TVB in 1967, to help set up its first colour-television station. At just 21 years old, he created Enjoy Yourself Tonight, which became the city’s longest-running live variety programme, airing more than 6,610 episodes between 1967 and 1994 on the TVB Jade channel.

Colin Bednall, the then-general manager of TVB, “trusted me to do the job. Had I failed, he would have lost his job for sure. But luckily, everything went so well. I am grateful to him”, Chua says of the opportunity extended to him despite his age.

As someone who avowedly attributes his life’s trajectory to an improbable series of events and a chain of people betting on him against all odds, Chua has held up his end of the bargain to deliver on those instincts and that faith in his potential.

Enjoy Yourself Tonight garnered him acclaim, promotions and opportunities. Chua rose through the ranks to serve as the special assistant to the managing director and also brought the Miss Hong Kong Pageant to the local network in 1973, before leaving TVB to start his own production house. Back then, freelance television facilities were few and far between, due to the high upfront costs of launching one. Coupled with its founder’s established reputation, Robert Chua Production House (RCP) readily landed work with broadcasters, agencies and corporate clients to do commercials, shows, documentaries and the like.

Chua expanded his business to the mainland in 1979, brokering deals on behalf of foreign advertisers looking to gain a foothold in the market.

Over a decades-long career in television, Chua put his mark on numerous other ventures, but his success and tenacious pursuit of achieving “firsts” did not come without challenges. In March 1995, after more than three months of experimental screenings, his Chinese-language China Entertainment Television Broadcast (CETV) channel began airing around Asia, showing original productions, films, soap operas, children’s series and more through 24 hours of daily programming. CETV reached millions of households across the region over the 21 years it was in operation, but it also proved to be one of the most difficult periods of Chua’s life.

Already burdened by the stress of keeping the station financially afloat, in January 1999, he suffered from a build-up of excess blood fluid in his brain, which required urgent surgery. While recuperating at home – his first time off since his marriage, in 1974, to his former assistant at TVB, Peggy Jen Ping-ping – and spending more time among his collection, by then already formidable, Chua began to view his antiques in a different light. Damaged and incomplete pieces caught his attention in particular, planting a seed for a new artistic vision: to flip the script on the notion that, in their marred condition, these objects were useless or to be discarded, and revive them through novel methods.

The resulting “Antique Arts” is part of Chua’s mission to find new meaning for his pieces. “An antique does not lose its soul because it is broken,” he explains, and neither should they be valued only for what they once were. A shattered vessel or a shard of porcelain is not just “a remnant of the past. It is a survivor. It carries memory, human touch, craftsmanship, cultural identity and the passage of time”.

Rather than restoring them to their original state, Chua believes that transforming them into new artworks allows them to keep existing through a modern narrative. Combining periods, dynasties, shapes, textures, mediums and contexts, his “Antique Arts” tell a fresh story, simultaneously embodying a metaphor for Chua’s own journey of recovery. (He would go on to have a second brain surgery, in 2008, informing his decision to take a step back from his company.)

Where collectors, dealers and auction houses see broken antiquities as defective, Chua thinks of them as a continuation. “Antique Arts” is his attempt to symbolically collaborate with the “many hands” of bygone makers, resulting in a creative dialogue across time. A Song dynasty pot fragment is paired with Neolithic earthenware to depict the pouring of tea, bridging thousands of years; the remaining handle of an ancient jug is juxtaposed with a modern ink painting to “complete the gaps” of the one-time vessel, leading to an abstract installation.

Chua’s personal relationship with the material feeds into his creation process, too. Anonymous objects they are not. He has lived with them, studied them and cared for them across decades, forming an intimate connection with the pieces.

From a slim case, he pulls out slender sticks of ivory, their tips wrapped in thread, ending in thin brushes of fine hair. Qiancao – cricket ticklers – are tools used in cricket combat, to goad the insects into fighting each other for sport.

“When I have an antique, like this one, I wonder who it belonged to. I imagine what their lives were like. Everything I look at, everything I touch, I feel someone in my mind – the people who used these objects, these pieces that lived in their homes. Frankly, that’s better to me than something that’s brand-new.” He continues, “How much is the value of a piece? It’s not so much about the actual monetary value, but how much it really means to you. ‘Expensive’ is in the mind of the person.

“For most collectors, when something breaks, it’s gone forever. But for me, if something breaks somehow, my consolation is that I can give it a new life. What I have, what’s broken, I try to put them together anyway. I never break anything on purpose. Sometimes, it just happens. But I wait for the right moment. I find the right pieces that will come together, and if they don’t, I won’t rush it.”

As he left broadcasting behind, the “right pieces” ended up falling into place, anyway. Chua’s affinity for entertainment, connecting with an audience and shaping narratives continued to find ways to manifest through different avenues.

In 2013, Chua followed his entrepreneurial nose and snapped up the Asia-Pacific franchising rights to Tim Ho Wan, often hailed as the cheapest Michelin-star restaurant in the world, launching it in Singapore with his brother, Harry Chua Wah-eng. He credits “luck and fate” as the reason for their winning bid – at the time, neither sibling had any experience in the food and beverage industry to speak of – but it was, once again, trust that paved the way to the next chapter.

“At TVB, [Bednall] shouldn’t have given me Enjoy Yourself Tonight, and likewise with Tim Ho Wan, they shouldn’t have given it to me with all the food and beverage professionals out there, but they did,” he told the South China Morning Post in 2024.

Tim Ho Wan co-founder Mak Kwai-pui was a fan of Enjoy Yourself Tonight, and Chua’s unconventional thinking – unhampered by orthodox restaurant strategies – allowed him to be bold and brave. When Tim Ho Wan arrived in Singapore, Chua positioned the brand as an elevated dining experience, growing its footprint to more than 40 outposts. Kam’s Roast (known in Hong Kong as Kam’s Roast Goose) followed in 2016.

“Entertainment and food are the same to me,” he explains. “It’s a people’s business. I look at food as my programmes and I look at my chef as my director.”

Four years later, he founded Joy Luck Teahouse, a chain of food kiosks specialising in Hong Kong-style cha chaan teng snacks, and in 2021, he launched Sinstant, a gourmet line of ready-to-eat and -cook Asian meals designed for home consumption.

At 80, most people would slow down. But Chua, being the “ideas guy”, has not stopped thinking about the future. As he considers his collection, and the richness it holds, there is but one thing left to do with it: share it with the world.

He has laid out the blueprint for his vision, combining his passion for food and Chinese antiques: an all-day restaurant-cum-museum, where the cuisine echoes a rotating curation of pieces from his personal Aladdin’s cave. Imagined as a “cultural-dining landmark”, Treasure of China is designed as an immersive destination to close the distance between heritage, gastronomy and storytelling. Guests will indulge in refined dishes, surrounded by centuries-old artefacts, with restaurant staff providing the backgrounds behind each object to engage visitors.

“Many restaurants, hotels and clubs have their own collection, but the art is just part of the furniture. It’s not interactive. It’s not engaging. It doesn’t change,” says Chua. “Here, the artwork is part of the meal. Whatever you see and touch has a story.”

Chinese history and culture shape each pillar of the museum-restaurant, down to the beverage programme, which will exclusively feature Chinese wines, baijiu, huangjiu and teas. Chua’s “Antique Arts” creations are also planned to be part of the display, and select works – both ancient and “new” – will be made available for purchase, with a percentage of the proceeds donated to charities.

Regional Chinese flavours, ranging from Cantonese and Sichuan to Jiangsu, Fujian and more, will make up the concept’s culinary foundations. But something that is, perhaps, unexpected is that Chua intends to place the experience in the “affordable premium” bracket, offering Michelin-level dining at accessible prices, making good on his ambition to embrace and give back to the wider community.

“Bringing art to a greater audience and developing a concept that not only preserves history, but presents it as a new experience that people can engage with, breaks down barriers,” says Chua. “Antiques are precious because they are records of history, but that doesn’t mean you can’t engage with them on an interpersonal level, because that’s what really creates a connection for people to appreciate them.”

With more than 200 seats envisioned in the estimated 7,000 sq ft floor plan, interactive elements – such as hands-on “touchable history” encounters to hold fossilised dinosaur eggs – and aesthetic spatial design engineered for the social-media generation, Chua plans to appeal to residents and tourists alike.

Treasure of China feels like a natural next step for Chua, whose joy in amassing antiques is matched only by the unadulterated delight he expresses when sharing their stories. On our tour of his “home museum”, through rows of gleaming porcelain and stoic figurines, it is his enthusiasm that leaves the biggest impression.

But this project is not Chua’s alone to bring to fruition. Adviser Jacco Klip, a Dutch F&B professional with nearly 40 years of experience in opening and managing restaurants, provides guidance on the charitable arm of the business. Meanwhile, Choi Biu, a veteran of Hong Kong’s dining scene and long-time member of the Shun Tak Fraternal Association and local Shunde community, and Lawrence Chong, a property-development consultant who has held positions at Sino Land and Galaxy Entertainment Group, serve as co-founder and special advisor to Chua, respectively.

Chua’s concept is still in its early stages, and there’s a lot of work ahead. His collection needs to be catalogued, the search for a suitable venue is ongoing and most of the items he owns hold taboo connotations within Chinese cultural norms, raising questions about how guests will perceive the curated works and whether superstitions around their provenance will win out against curiosity. Not to mention, there are like-minded backers to find and convince.

None of these hurdles seem to be stopping the lifelong pioneer from forging ahead to secure interest and investors in Treasure of China. He has already mapped out a multi-stream revenue model, which includes brand collaborations, merchandising and licensing – a “gastro-tainment” approach, so to speak.

“We’re looking for people with passion,” Chua says. “When I do any business, profit is not my No 1 priority. You must make sure you do a good job. If you think profit comes first, you’ll never be successful, because you’re trying to compromise. I want the partners to feel proud of how they are contributing to society.”

Whether the museum-restaurant sets its roots down in Hong Kong or Macau is still to be determined, but Chua believes that the objective of his cultural destination – to entertain, educate and connect – aligns with either city’s broader tourism goals.

“Over the years, I have always believed in creating ‘firsts’ that change how people think and experience things”

Robert Chua

Whatever shape his new project takes, Chua’s mission has not changed. From “Antique Arts” to Treasure of China, giving the pieces in his collection a new voice remains central to his pursuits, as a poignant reminder that history can be palpably felt, reimagined and revived. “Over the years, I have always believed in creating ‘firsts’ that change how people think and experience things,” he says.

“With art, if it’s something you like, you enjoy, you appreciate, it doesn’t have to be that profound, right? I look at these objects, and some of them look so rough. But to me, they are beautiful because they hold so many stories and years of life.”
