Why Chinese media says US historians are giving ‘ammunition’ to separatists

SCMP | FREE | 1067 words | 2026-08-29

China has stepped up its offensive against a US-born school of thought on China’s last imperial dynasty, accusing it of providing “theoretical ammunition” for attempts to divide the country.

The denunciation comes as online debates over the Qing dynasty (1644-1911) continue to give rise to inflammatory rhetoric, and as Beijing’s ethnic policies and historical narratives are increasingly becoming an arena for international political competition.

An article published by the state-run China Ethnic News last Monday warned that New Qing History was “seriously eroding the historical foundations of the community for the Chinese nation in the real world” and should be “forcefully refuted”.

That marked the latest broadside in a two-year campaign by the outlet to attack the loosely defined scholarly movement, which began to take shape in the late 1980s.

Rather than being an organised movement with a unified doctrine, New Qing History refers to a broad revisionist approach to understanding how the Manchu-led Qing managed both China’s Han majority and the many other ethnic groups under their rule.

Scholars taking this approach have drawn on Manchu-language sources to question the long-held assumption that “Sinicisation” – the idea that conquerors of China and even neighbouring peoples were transformed by Chinese culture, often portrayed as a spontaneous and largely one-way process – was the only explanation.

They also draw on anthropological and sociological perspectives to explain the Manchu rulers’ relations with Tibetan religious leaders, Mongolian chieftains and Turkic elites in Xinjiang.

The China Ethnic News article reiterated several criticisms that Chinese historians have long levelled at New Qing History, including accusations that the approach undermined the Qing’s historical status as a truly Chinese dynasty.

It said historians were “fracturing the integrity of the Chinese nation” by treating frontier regions under Qing rule such as Mongolia and Xinjiang as separate units from China’s interior.

The piece argued that this “erroneous view of history” could lead to “multiple real-world harms” amid the complex international political environment.

“By deconstructing the Qing’s territory into ‘pluralistic modules’ and denying the historical affiliation of frontier regions and China, New Qing History is effectively providing ‘theoretical ammunition’ for various separatist forces,” warned the authors Li Defeng and Zhang Yide from Inner Mongolia University.

For at least a decade, Chinese scholars have explicitly or implicitly accused New Qing History of being part of an “academic intrigue” aimed at dividing China.

In the article, Li and Zhang said that New Qing History could be traced back to theories advanced by scholars in imperial Japan who argued that Manchuria and Mongolia were not part of China.

In a since-deleted interview with Chinese digital outlet The Paper, Georgetown University professor James Millward argued that the allegations that New Qing History was part of an anti-China conspiracy were a “misunderstanding”.

He added that the movement’s purpose was to refine the scholarship of earlier historians – such as John K. Fairbank – on topics such as the tribute system, Sinicisation and the idea of China as the civilisational centre.

In a 2017 interview with The Paper, Ding Yizhuang, a researcher at the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences (CASS), praised the school’s focus on Manchu-language sources and acknowledged its value in inspiring research in China.

“As for whether the ‘New Qing History’ has some political agenda or even a conspiracy behind it, I think we should first … at least understand what they are talking about before criticising it. Only then can such criticism be convincing,” she said.

However, at an academic symposium in 2019, Ding said that “New Qing History is an American thing, not our thing” and rejected any affiliation with the movement.

Liu Xiaomeng, another CASS scholar, wrote in a piece published in 2012 that New Qing History had helped to move research from an excessively Han-centric approach and set a higher bar for Chinese scholars working in the field.

However, Liu also warned that denying the Qing dynasty was part of Chinese history would inevitably push the school towards an extreme position.

Over the past few years, the debate has become more politically charged as Beijing has sought to reshape the relationship between ethnic minorities and the state – such as through the recently enacted Law on Promoting Ethnic Unity and Progress, which some observers have described as providing a statutory mandate for assimilation.

In an academic research guideline issued in 2024, the National Ethnic Affairs Commission – a central government agency overseen by the Communist Party’s United Front Work Department – called for resistance to a range of “erroneous historical views”, including New Qing History.

The criticism of New Qing History also comes as Beijing steps up efforts to build a “Chinese intellectual system in philosophy and social sciences” capable of explaining China’s practices to the outside world without what officials describe as the monopoly or distortion of Western concepts and narratives.

Monday’s article said New Qing History applied modern Western theories of the nation-state to China’s long-standing model of a “unity in diversity” Chinese nation, while using concepts such as “empire” and “colonialism” rooted in European historical experiences to describe centuries of two-way interaction among different regions of China.

The Chinese government began compiling an official history of the Qing in 2002, with the project originally expected to take little more than a decade. But it remains unfinished, a potential sign of the political sensitivity surrounding the period.

“New Qing History has produced a lot of books and many arguments. We need to catch up. A complete rebuttal cannot be built on just one or two articles,” read one comment below Monday’s article.

Rebuttals of New Qing History perspectives have not exclusively been aimed at foreign scholars – the movement has found support among some fringe Chinese nationalists.

Some influencers – often labelled “Han chauvinists” – have blamed China’s backwardness compared to Western powers and its descent into a “century of humiliation” in modern times on the Manchu rulers’ takeover of the Han-dominated Ming dynasty.

They portrayed the Manchus as foreign “barbarians” who colonised China and stalled its civilisation.

That echoes New Qing History’s approach of treating the Qing as more than simply another Chinese dynasty and comparing it with the Mughal and Ottoman empires.

The view reached a peak in popularity in the second half of last year before being rebuked by state media, but its supporters remain active online today.
