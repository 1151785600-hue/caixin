Beyond the summit: how US-China relations could still unravel

SCMP | FREE | 1039 words | 2026-10-09

This year’s Chinese National Day reception in Washington felt noticeably jollier than those I have attended in recent years. The mood was more relaxed and Chinese Ambassador Xie Feng’s opening speech was upbeat. He even joked about the panda ambassadors at his own expense.

The cheerful tone followed President Xi Jinping’s short but successful state visit to the US, during which his counterpart, President Donald Trump, gave him an unprecedented high-level reception that would have flattered even the most fastidious of guests.

Yet the mood outside the embassy was decidedly different. The opposition Democrats criticised Trump’s cosying up to “communist” China as another example of his misguided foreign policy, a betrayal of American allies and even a sell-out of national interests.

The mainstream media, locked in a feud with Trump over his ban on media access to the White House, either downplayed the visit or focused on its lack of tangible progress. Even the president’s allies admitted in private that the timing “was not right” to expect a major breakthrough.

With the midterm elections just weeks away, the polarisation of American politics can only deepen. Even among Trump’s supporters and the business community, there is little agreement on what deals to seek with Beijing. Americans certainly want China to lift its rare earth export controls, but there is no consensus on what to offer in return.

Relaxing the US ban on advanced chips to China meets strong opposition. At the risk of oversimplification, there are two camps. One argues that the restrictions will only push Beijing to accelerate its own chip development, and that it is in American interests to allow a controlled flow of advanced chips to China to maintain its “chip dependency”.

The other believes that China will pursue an alternative chip industry regardless, and that selling advanced chips would merely buy Beijing time. This camp holds that the US should double down on the restrictions, particularly as nobody can be sure whether China, or any other country, can really build an alternative system independent of American-controlled components.

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang and AMD CEO Lisa Su sat at the head table at the state dinner Trump hosted for Xi, but the South China Morning Post was told that chip controls were never on the negotiating table. That suggests the hawks on this issue are in the driver’s seat.

There is similarly no consensus among Americans on regulating artificial intelligence, or “superintelligence” as the White House calls it. Some see regulation as a power grab by a handful of tech giants. Advocates of the open-source approach want to know how standards would be set, who would be responsible for verification, and how, even if a common set of standards could be agreed. The Chinese proposal to include chip controls in the bilateral AI dialogue only complicates matters.

The same is true of allowing more Chinese investment, or Chinese-owned electric vehicle and battery factories, in the United States. As the SCMP reported, some Chinese company executives travelled to Washington in the hope of a last-minute agreement that might open the door to US investment, but it proved futile.

It is telling that just a few days after Xi’s visit, Ford CEO Jim Farley warned that the US must carefully consider whether to admit Chinese carmakers to the American market, lest it become another Europe – a reference to the explosive growth of Chinese cars there. Farley wants to work with the Chinese only in areas where America can acquire technologies it lacks, which is hardly something Beijing will be excited about.

Beyond business, matters are even more complicated. It is widely known that Taiwan is Beijing’s absolute red line. Trump showed remarkable restraint on this sensitive issue throughout Xi’s visit, but even for him, the Chinese request that he openly oppose Taiwan independence was a bridge too far.

There is considerable pushback on the issue, even among Trump’s supporters. The president has delayed the latest round of arms sales to Taiwan, partly helped by supply shortages for more urgent needs elsewhere, but how long the administration can keep the lid on is an open question.

In his speech, Ambassador Xie highlighted the good rapport between Xi and Trump, which has become the most critical ballast stabilising the bilateral relationship. Western media wrote about how Trump caged the China hawks. Yet it is always risky to predict the American president’s behaviour.

He has been careful to maintain his relationship with Xi, but his China policy can change course drastically at any time. Those who accuse Trump of going soft on China would do well to remember that he launched an unprecedented trade war against Beijing just 18 months ago.

Trump has still to make a historic trip to Shenzhen, home of Huawei, BYD and DJI, and Xi will visit Miami. But the focus of these meetings will be more on international affairs, compared with their summit in September. The whispers in Washington are that, besides the lack of American consensus, US allies alarmed at the unprecedented reception Xi received are also working hard to lobby Washington to push back.

Japan is the most obvious example, but it is hardly the only country that feels this way. If there is no significant progress between Beijing and Washington by the time of the G20 summit, it will give the US, the European Union, Japan and others an opportunity to press China on issues such as the trade imbalance and rare earth controls.

Added to this is uncertainty over the impact of the midterm elections, which many predict will go badly for Trump. How will the president react? Will he shift his focus to domestic issues, take a more conciliatory approach, or double down? Dabbling in the arcane art of Trumpology is a career hazard.

None of this is to gainsay the positive results of Xi’s state visit, about which Beijing has plenty of reasons to feel pleased. But it would be dangerous to assume smooth sailing for China-US ties from here on. The next three months will determine whether strategic stability can truly emerge, or the relationship could go off the rails again.
