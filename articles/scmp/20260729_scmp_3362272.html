Limited dual citizenship gives Indonesian diaspora hope of return, better future

SCMP | FREE | 1000 words | 2026-07-29

Indonesia’s attempt to lure back its native talent scattered around the world through limited dual citizenship has highlighted a long-held demand of the diaspora for such a scheme before they would consider returning home.

The proposed revision to the citizenship law would mark a major shift for a country that has largely allowed only single citizenship since independence in 1945. Currently, children of mixed marriages can hold dual citizenship until they reach 18 years of age, when they are given three years to choose a single country.

Under the new proposal, dual nationality would not be open to all Indonesians abroad, but only to selected individuals whose expertise is considered important to national development, such as scientists, medical specialists, athletes and others recommended by ministries, state agencies or reputable institutions.

For Indonesians such as Ayu, who has built a life in France in the last 13 years while holding to her Indonesian passport, even a limited reform would be meaningful for her.

“I feel like this is giving us hope that maybe we could also have our birthright of always being an Indonesian, even though we’re not living there any more,” she told This Week in Asia from Paris.

For some Indonesians living abroad, acquiring foreign citizenship has meant giving up their original nationality – a choice that has long frustrated members of the diaspora who still want to maintain legal, political and emotional ties to their country.

In June, the Ministry of Law revealed that more than 8,000 Indonesians had applied to renounce their citizenship over the past five years, mostly because of marriage to foreigners.

Data from the Ministry of Immigration and Corrections showed that nearly 4,000 Indonesians exchanged their citizenship to become Singaporeans between 2019 and 2022.

Among those who have renounced their Indonesian nationality to acquire citizenship from their host countries are those working in advanced technology and medicine.

Justice Minister Supratman Andi Agtas said last week that Jakarta would ask the House of Representatives to include the limited dual citizenship scheme in the draft bill as part of the revisions to the 2006 Citizenship Law.

“For example, a nuclear expert or [athletes] playing for our national team, in all sports, or [top] chemists. So, it can’t be just anyone,” Supratman said on July 23, as cited by Kompas.

“We said we wanted to accommodate our best talents, right? Many of our diaspora are reluctant to give up their foreign citizenship, especially those with expertise in nuclear, chemical and medical fields,” he added.

The single citizenship principle had also been creating “a problem” for children from mixed marriages between Indonesians and foreigners, Supratman said.

As the proposal remains at an early stage, the government has yet to determine which areas of expertise would be eligible for limited dual nationality.

Supratman emphasised that approval from lawmakers was needed for the measure to be adopted into the draft bill.

The dual citizenship proposal follows a series of efforts by Southeast Asia’s biggest economy to reconnect with wealthy investors, highly skilled workers and the Indonesian diaspora, including its Golden Visa and Global Citizenship schemes.

Glimmer of hope

Ayu, who asked that her last name be withheld, said she supported the measure because it would “open more possibilities for super talented people to be able to give back to Indonesia and not just leave because they have other opportunities in another country”.

While she has “adapted well” to her “second home”, Ayu said she still held her Indonesian passport despite the travel-related difficulties that came with it.

“If I had to give up my Indonesian passport, that means I cannot vote any more, that means that I can no longer put my voice into something that I believe in for the country,” the 39-year-old said.

“In a way, people who are still holding on to Indonesian passports mean that they are still hoping for a better future for Indonesia.”

Legal forfeitures, such as the inability to own land or the difficulty of running a business as a foreigner, also weighed on her decision to maintain her Indonesian nationality, she said.

Ayu said she would feel “really sad” when her daughter had to decide, once she turned 18, whether to choose her Indonesian or French citizenship.

“Indonesia as a country will miss out, as they will lose people just because of their [curbs on] dual nationalities,” she said.

“A lot of mixed-nationality children are probably going to choose the other country just because it is more developed.”

Eddy Setiawan, a researcher at the Jakarta-based Indonesian Citizenship Institute, said the proposed measure could help stem Indonesia’s brain drain, as talented diaspora members “might not have to give up their Indonesian citizenship if they need to pursue a career at a certain level that requires [foreign] citizenship”.

“With people like that, technology and knowledge transfer can occur. Investors with a certain amount of investment can also create jobs in Indonesia,” Setiawan said.

Questions over impact

Hikmahanto Juwana, a professor of international law at the University of Indonesia, however, said there was “no need” to expand limited dual citizenship beyond children of mixed marriages.

“Do we really have a shortage of talented people? Is there any data that backs up this claim?” Hikmahanto said.

“As for nuclear scientists, we have plenty of them.”

He also warned of potential social tensions arising from dual citizenship, including concerns over unemployment and limited land ownership among Indonesian citizens.

But even among those who welcome the proposal, dual citizenship is only one part of a larger question about whether Indonesia can persuade its diaspora to return.

For Ayu, even if she were to obtain dual citizenship, it would not be a decisive factor in her deliberation whether to return to Indonesia or stay in France.

“I think we would like to have a country where there’s just less corruption,” she said.

“I feel like that’s a basic [request], but it’s really hard to [achieve] because that would just ask for the restructuring of the whole country.”
