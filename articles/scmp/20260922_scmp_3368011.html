As the US weighs restrictions on cloud computing, how will China’s AI sector adapt?

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1986 words | 2026-09-22

When Chinese artificial intelligence developers want to train their next-generation frontier models, they all face the same daunting wall: they cannot legally buy the world’s most powerful AI chips.

However, the country’s tech giants and start-ups have still managed to quietly keep pace with global rivals. Their secret is an elusive workaround: moving heavy training workloads across borders through the cloud.

By setting up proxy entities in foreign jurisdictions, domestic firms have for years leased server space in data centres across Southeast Asia, Japan and the Middle East, according to a broker in Shenzhen who asked not to be named due to the sensitivity of the matter.

Under contracts signed by non-Chinese entities, models have been trained on cutting-edge Nvidia processors housed thousands of miles, away outside the reach of US authorities, he said.

That critical lifeline could be on the verge of collapsing.

Washington is reportedly moving to expand its tech blockade from physical hardware to cloud networks. As the US Congress weighs legislation to restrict foreign remote access and executive agencies review cloud-leasing loopholes, China’s AI sector faces its most severe choke point since the chip bans began.

The impending US crackdown threatens to reshape the global AI ecosystem, challenging Southeast Asia’s data centre boom, squeezing Nvidia’s international revenues and forcing Beijing into an acceleration of its domestic chip push.

The cloud escape hatch

Developing AI infrastructure across borders has long been common practice among Chinese developers looking to bypass hardware bans while avoiding US regulatory hurdles, according to observers.

“For a long time, Chinese AI companies have circumvented chip export bans by leasing overseas data centres,” said Arisa Liu, chief director and research fellow at Taiwan Industry Economics Services, a unit of the Taiwan Institute of Economic Research. “[This] has indeed contributed a significant amount of overseas market demand to Nvidia.”

To make good use of these remote servers, some Chinese teams have taken extraordinary measures to overcome technical bottlenecks. Chinese engineers personally carried hard drives storing 80 terabytes of data from Beijing to Malaysia to train an AI model at a local data centre, saving months of digital transfer time, The Wall Street Journal reported last year.

Driven by the global AI boom, demand for high-performance computing power has sent global cloud rental rates soaring.

Leading AI infrastructure provider Nebius Group raised rental prices on certain Nvidia chips by roughly 30 per cent in June, with a further 21 per cent increase to come into effect on October 1. Hyperscaler rates for Nvidia’s A100 processors rose to US$3.75 per hour in late August, up from US$3.71 a month earlier, according to tracking firm Silicon Data.

To maintain secrecy and navigate cross-border payments, some Chinese teams have even executed cloud-leasing transactions via blockchain-based smart contracts and paid in cryptocurrency to keep both parties hidden behind anonymous digital wallets, the Journal reported in 2024.

However, the regulatory net in Washington is rapidly closing.

The administration of US President Donald Trump was reviewing remote access arrangements and was working on policies to curb it, according to reports by Bloomberg and The Information. In January, the US House passed the Remote Access Security Act, which, if signed into law, would expand American export controls to remote cloud computing access.

“If the US passes and implements relevant legislation that extends restrictions from physical chips to cloud leasing, Nvidia’s overall sales growth momentum will inevitably be curtailed to some extent,” Liu said.

Legal experts noted that current compliance remained a grey area. Currently, there are “no regulations or similar provisions in place to restrict or prohibit” such remote access, said Hong Jiayang, managing partner of US law firm Joint-Win Partners.

The only measure that came close to banning the practice was the “AI Diffusion Rule”, said Hong, who focuses on trade compliance. The regulation, which requires US cloud service providers to exercise caution when providing cloud services for Chinese AI models, was introduced by the Biden administration in January 2025 but rescinded by the US Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) after Trump came to power.

The Remote Access Security Act “was introduced for a similar purpose”, Hong said. However, once it goes into effect, “the businesses of cloud service giants such as Oracle and Amazon.com will be significantly impacted, which is bound to provoke a strong backlash”.

Even if the US did not enact legislation to regulate cloud services, the BIS could “theoretically impose penalties on data centres and even model developers that use illegally smuggled chips” on the grounds of “General Prohibition 10”, which penalises transactions conducted with knowledge that a violation has occurred, Hong explained.

“Hi-tech companies should avoid becoming entangled in such legal liabilities at all times,” he added.

Southeast Asia’s data centre boom

If Washington closes the cloud computing loophole, the immediate shock waves will hit Southeast Asia, one of the world’s fastest-growing data centre hubs.

Local data centre capacity in the region was projected to expand at 35 per cent each year from 2023 through 2028, outpacing the global average of 15 per cent, according to market intelligence platform Speeda.

Chinese tech giants, including Alibaba Group Holding – owner of the South China Morning Post – Tencent Holdings and TikTok owner ByteDance, have spent years building out regional infrastructure to serve the Chinese market.

Asian hyperscalers preferred the region for several reasons, including “competitive development costs across construction, equipment and human capital; and lower operating costs”, according to Vivian Wong, lead analyst for APAC at DC Byte, a market consultancy in the data centre industry.

If you want to win customers in a market, you need availability zones in that market

Alex Cordovil, Dell’Oro Group

“If you want to win customers in a market, you need availability zones in that market … Southeast Asia is the natural first step for that expansion, given proximity, existing business ties, land and power availability,” said Alex Cordovil, research director at Dell’Oro Group, a market research firm in the telecommunications, security and data centre industries.

Beyond local demands, the region has attracted international workloads, “including from Chinese internet and cloud companies”, Cordovil said. “Export controls are part of that picture: certain accelerator architectures are only available outside mainland China, so workloads that depend on them naturally gravitate to locations where that supply is accessible.”

The region also offers competitive energy economics.

In Southeast Asia, electricity prices average US$118 per megawatt hour (MWh), with the lowest in Indonesia at US$60 per MWh, according to Wood Mackenzie, a consultancy focusing on the energy, chemicals, renewables, metals and mining.

While higher than China’s average industrial rates - between 600 yuan (US$89) and 700 yuan per MWh, and half that in energy-rich regions like Inner Mongolia and Guizhou – Southeast Asia remains cheaper than US hubs like northern Virginia and Silicon Valley, where prices range between US$70 and US$120 per MWh. In comparison, European centres like Germany and Ireland see prices ranging between €150 (US$174) and €250 per MWh, according to state-backed media China Energy News.

Should remote access become a constraint for certain customers in Southeast Asia, it would “likely create a transient imbalance between supply and demand in the region”, he said, adding that the imbalance was expected to be “short lived” as demand was deep.

Wong held a similar view, saying that “any released capacity is likely to be taken up relatively quickly by other customers”.

The domestic bottleneck

For Chinese AI firms, losing overseas cloud access will lay bare a troubling reality: despite heavy state investment, domestic hardware is not yet fully equipped to handle frontier model training at scale.

Despite China’s push for self-sufficiency in chips, the country’s most advanced AI models are still being trained on Nvidia chips, the SCMP previously reported.

The AI development life cycle involves training and inference – the use of a trained model to generate new content. Systems such as DeepSeek-V4 and Moonshot’s Kimi K3 have been successfully adapted to Chinese hardware for inference work only.

For now, the most advanced Nvidia chips remain unavailable in China, including the B200, B300, H100 and H800. Chinese firms either use Nvidia chips they have in inventory or gain access through cloud computing.

The H200, the latest of Nvidia’s Hopper-series chips but a generation behind the Blackwell family, was approved for import by both Washington and Beijing but is only available in small quantities.

ByteDance and Tencent Holdings each received about 10,000 H200s and a few other Chinese customers obtained shipments of a similar size, the Financial Times reported in August.

“There is a massive gap between this quantity and the actual demand of such top-tier tech giants, which often consume tens of thousands or even over 100,000 advanced [graphics processing units],” said Liu of the Taiwan institution.

Nvidia in late August reported that shipments of H200s to China accounted for less than 1 per cent of its US$89 billion data-centre revenue in the quarter that ended July 26. However, it said it did not expect any data-centre computing revenue from China in the following quarter.

Liu added that even though Nvidia’s income might be temporarily curtailed if the US imposed the ban on remote access, “this shortfall may be partially offset by massive orders from other international tech giants” given strong AI demand globally.

Accelerating self-reliance

If the US bans Chinese companies from renting Nvidia-powered data centres overseas, it may lead to higher domestic rental rates, the Shenzhen-based data centre broker noted. For now, rates for B300 chips outside China are two to three times higher than local prices, not including differences in electricity costs.

Even if there were no restriction on renting overseas data centres, replacing Nvidia with domestic chips was “the main trend”, said a semiconductor designer at one of China’s biggest chipmakers, who requested anonymity.

“Who knows if Nvidia processors have back doors,” the person said. Nvidia has repeatedly said that there are no back doors, kill switches or spyware in its products.

Some Chinese teams have started using local chips for AI training. In June, Meituan released LongCat-2.0, a 1.6-trillion-parameter model that it said was fully trained and run on a 50,000-card domestic computing power cluster, without naming the supplier.

Huawei Technologies’ rotating chairman Eric Xu Zhijun expected a major domestic shift towards its AI computing infrastructure for model training next year.

The semiconductor design engineer said the best domestic AI chip currently was the Ascend 950 from Huawei Technologies, not only for its performance but for the whole ecosystem from architecture to networks. Huawei announced last week that the next-generation Ascend 960DT, designed for model training, will be ready in the first quarter of 2027, nine months ahead of schedule.

However, transferring training workloads from Nvidia to domestic chips is a steep engineering bottleneck. The hurdles included the software ecosystem, as Nvidia’s Compute Unified Device Architecture (CUDA) platform has long been the industry standard for AI development, the SCMP reported in August.

Migrating existing workflows to Huawei’s Ascend chips could add at least 50 per cent to training time and cost, according to researcher James Wang.

Still, China is on track to dramatically slash its deficit in advanced chips over the next decade. The supply of advanced-node wafers made using 7-nanometre processes and below was projected to grow at 46 per cent annually between 2025 and 2035, far outpacing the 17 per cent growth expected in domestic demand, Goldman analysts said in a recent report.

That would narrow the gap between domestic supply and demand to 34 per cent by 2035 from 92 per cent in 2025, according to the investment bank.

As Washington moves to extend its tech blockade from hardware shipments to virtual server networks, Chinese AI developers may soon lose their refuge in the cloud. What remains to be seen is whether the squeeze will stall China’s frontier AI ambitions or simply accelerate its push towards technological self-sufficiency.
