‘Doing a North Korea’? Why the Middle East could be entering a new nuclear age

SCMP | FREE | 1048 words | 2026-09-15

The Middle East stands on the precipice of a new nuclear age, with Saudi Arabia set to acquire American uranium enrichment technology and Turkey eager to follow suit, as Iran weighs withdrawal from the treaty that bars it from making atomic bombs.

Momentum is building: last week, the United States and Israel both threatened further air strikes against Iran’s nuclear facilities, accusing Tehran of attempting to resurrect its nuclear programme. The country’s three main complexes were buried deep underground after the US and Israel bombed them during their first, brief air war against the Islamic Republic in June 2025.

The warnings from US President Donald Trump and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu followed the publication on September 9 of a study by the Centre for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) claiming that a surge in construction activity at the so-called Pickaxe Mountain site “likely” meant it was being developed into an underground fortress for Iran’s nuclear programme.

The CSIS report said the facility could either be a centrifuge assembly plant, an enrichment site or a safe haven for other “nuclear weapons-relevant” work such as uranium metal conversion.

That same day, Trump said Western intelligence agencies had flagged the Pickaxe facility as a likely storage site for nuclear material.

“We notice there’s a little activity at Pickaxe. I would advise Iran not to get cute because we will have to hit them very hard,” he threatened.

In a video message on September 10, Netanyahu alleged that Iran, “after we destroyed its immediate capability to produce nuclear bombs … is trying again”.

However, the CSIS findings were hotly disputed by eminent American nuclear weapons expert David Albright, founder of the Institute for Science and International Security.

In a social media post on Monday, Albright argued that the construction activity at Pickaxe Mountain spotted by satellites in August had in fact been under way since last year’s air strikes and more likely showed Iran preparing the site for a potential US attack.

Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei dismissed the CSIS report on Tuesday as “much ado about nothing”.

Still, its publication coincided with an International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) decision to refer Iran’s file to the UN Security Council for the first time in 20 years.

The resolution, brought by Britain, France and Germany, accused Iran of non-compliance with the nuclear non-proliferation treaty (NPT) on the grounds it had withheld information on its stockpiles of highly enriched uranium stored in the three facilities rendered inaccessible by American bunker-buster bombs and by denying IAEA inspectors access to the sites.

The referral is not expected to yield further UN Security Council action against Iran, because of opposition from veto-wielding permanent members China and Russia.

But the political fallout is already being felt in Tehran, where hardliners have seized on the move to renew calls for withdrawal from the NPT.

Mohsen Rezaee, secretary of Iran’s Supreme National Security Council, warned that the IAEA’s “politicised and disruptive actions will push countries towards withdrawing from the NPT”.

Analysts who spoke to This Week in Asia said further US-Israeli strikes could prompt Tehran to take that very action.

Barbara Slavin, distinguished Middle East fellow at the Stimson Centre, said such attacks would “increase political support within the regime for such a step”.

Sina Azodi, author of Iran and the Bomb, called withdrawal “quite likely” given the war and Iran’s “strong dissatisfaction with the IAEA and the NPT itself”.

Ali Alfoneh, author of Political Succession in the Islamic Republic of Iran, was more cautious, suggesting Tehran would only leave the NPT “if and when it has completed production of two nuclear weapons: one for testing and another to retain as a deterrent”.

Slavin agreed that the likelihood of Iran “doing a North Korea” was increasing.

Azodi also noted growing support within Iran for “weaponisation” since the previous strategy of enriching uranium to near weapons-grade without actually building a bomb had failed to deter the US and Israeli attacks.

“Whether Tehran can actually sneak out and test a device is a separate issue,” Azodi said.

Alfoneh similarly said he suspected that “technical obstacles, rather than an absence of political resolve, are the principal constraint on Iranian weaponisation”.

A July 22 agreement between the US and Saudi Arabia to develop the kingdom’s nuclear power industry was expected to further strengthen Iran’s resolve to keep its uranium enrichment capabilities, analysts said.

The deal, which Alfoneh calls “a sad joke”, has alarmed nuclear non-proliferation experts because it includes scope for future uranium enrichment activities using American reactors and Saudi uranium reserves.

At the IAEA’s annual general conference in Vienna on Tuesday, Saudi Energy Minister Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman announced the discovery of 110 million tonnes of uranium-bearing ore near the holy city of Medina.

The agreement with the US makes proliferation safeguards a bilateral matter, sparing Saudi Arabia the intrusive nationwide inspection regime required by the IAEA.

The Arms Control Association said in a statement published on September 1 that the Trump administration had “rejected key non-proliferation conditions for US nuclear cooperation that have long-standing bipartisan support and are internationally recognised best practices” in negotiating the agreement with Riyadh.

While Saudi Arabia had insisted its interest in enrichment is purely for civilian purposes, the association observed that the same technology could create fissile material for a bomb – a risk it called “particularly troubling given that Saudi officials have openly threatened to pursue nuclear weapons to match any Iranian capability”.

Analysts said Nato member Turkey was closely following all these developments.

According to Gonul Tol, senior Turkey fellow at the Middle East Institute, Ankara sees the US-Saudi deal as “evidence that regional states are becoming more serious about preserving a nuclear option”.

“Just as importantly, Turkish officials see Washington itself as becoming more flexible about non-proliferation,” Tol noted in a September 10 research paper.

The Iran war, Turkey’s expulsion from the F-35 programme, Trump’s threats towards Nato and broader questions about US security guarantees had also “strengthened doubts in Ankara about whether Turkey can rely indefinitely on the American nuclear umbrella”, she said.

Turkey would likely hedge by seeking “greater control over its nuclear supply chain and more indigenous nuclear capabilities”, Tol added.
