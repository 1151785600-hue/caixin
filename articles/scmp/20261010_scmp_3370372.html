The hidden heartbreak of losing a pet in Singapore and Malaysia

SCMP | FREE | 1137 words | 2026-10-10

Growing up in a Malay family in Singapore, Hafeez Hassan feared dogs. He never expected to mourn one like a sibling.

“Maybe that is what I have been grieving all along … The experience of loving something that I was taught I was not supposed to love,” he said.

Even as an adult, walking his former partner’s husky Pieces in public made Hafeez anxious that a relative might see him, or that he might be judged for being close to an animal whose saliva and waste are regarded as ritually impure by many Muslims in the region.

Touching or holding a dog is not sinful, according to the Islamic Religious Council of Singapore. Though it did note in a 2014 advisory that other schools of Islamic jurisprudence had different views on dogs.

Hafeez, who is a dance choreographer and movement coach, did not immediately seek counselling when Pieces died.

Years passed before he started to talk openly about what the loss meant to him with George Chan, a loss and grief counsellor in the city state whom Hafeez knew from their work together on dance productions and musicals.

Disenfranchised grief

Hafeez’s experience is one that counsellors in Singapore and Malaysia say they recognise all too well.

Those in the profession call “disenfranchised grief”, a term for a loss that is not openly acknowledged or socially supported, and that others may not readily understand or accept.

A dead pet can bring on such a feeling when owners sense the bond they had with an animal is being minimised or dismissed.

“The moment they feel like they have to defend their grief, it becomes a very lonely thing,” Chan said.

What is lost is often more than just an animal, according to grief counsellors. It can be the routines that were built around the pet, or the absence of a steady companion that helped an owner through upheavals and challenging times.

Some clients come to Chan specifically because their pets have died. For others, the grief only surfaces while they are working through other issues such as a divorce or the death of a parent.

Chan’s sessions often begin with the pet’s name and what kind of companion it was.

“I’m not trying to understand death,” he said. “I’m trying to understand the relationship.”

For owners who had to decide whether to euthanise their pet, guilt over acting too soon or waiting too long was often part of the grieving process, Chan said.

“Sometimes it is easier to hold on to a guilt story than to accept that you were completely helpless … with guilt, you can still blame yourself, the vet or someone else,” he said, adding that the alternative was to admit there was nothing more the owner could have done.

He sets no fixed number of sessions for his clients and does not regard grief as a problem to be solved.

“My job is not to fix a five-week-old grief, you grieve because you loved,” Chan said.

Lifelines after loss

In Malaysia, PiePie Pet Memorial has made follow-up care part of its services, rather than treating the farewell ceremony as the end of its role.

Manager Tan Chien Yung said the company began formally tracking counsellor follow-ups in 2022, with more than 4,000 bereaved pet owners receiving such support in the years since.

Last year, the pet funeral and cremation services provider recorded around 1,250 follow-ups with grieving owners, up from 1,030 the year before. So far in 2026, some 850 owners have been contacted.

Not all follow-ups result in a dedicated grief support session. In fact, only 21 owners had booked a complimentary session since 2022, Tan said.

“The purpose of the service is not to encourage more people to attend counselling,” he said. “It is to make sure that no pet owner feels they have to navigate their grief alone.”

Tan Su Wen, a licensed counsellor who works part-time with PiePie on its follow-ups, usually calls about a week after the final farewell, or sooner if funeral staff report that an owner appeared particularly distressed during the ceremony.

“Some owners feel a degree of closure after a formal send-off,” she said, adding that guilt was a common concern among those owners who sought help after euthanising a pet or losing one to an unforeseen accident.

Like Chan, she finds that many owners struggle with the shock of a sudden death or the loss of an animal companion that had become part of the family.

For Adam Ishak, her help really mattered. Samuel, his orange Persian-Maine Coon mix, died in late February at the age of 12, after a year-long battle with feline chronic kidney disease.

As the cat’s condition worsened, Ishak and his wife opted for euthanasia to spare it further suffering. Their advice to other owners is to look out for possible signs of the disease and seek veterinary advice early.

Ishak, who has lived with cats all his life, said Samuel “left a mark on my life like no other”.

The PiePie team’s support had been invaluable, he said, especially the “certificate of life … which is hanging above his ashes in our home”.

Every day, the couple light a candle and say Samuel’s name, based on a traditional Central American belief “that a life dies three times: when the body dies, when it is interred and when its name is spoken for the last time”.

“Because of the last ‘death’, I believe Samuel is still alive for as long as we remember him,” Ishak said.

Enduring taboos

While losing a dog or cat is the most common reason people see a pet bereavement counsellor, grief is not necessarily dictated by the pet’s species or size.

Chan noted a tendency for society to create a hierarchy even within pet loss, assuming the death of a dog carried more emotional weight than that of a bird, fish or reptile.

Most of his clients were dog owners, but he said he had also counselled someone grieving a bird.

At PiePie, grief-support appointments have mainly involved dogs and cats, but also rabbits and hamsters. The company has even held a farewell for a pet fish.

Hafeez got another dog after Pieces’ passing. Ali, a shih-tzu, has been with him for six years. But there are still family gatherings at which Ali is not welcome.

“There is something painful about loving an animal deeply while knowing that a relationship is considered forbidden or unacceptable by parts of your own community,” Hafeez said.

“And the more I think about it, the more that hurts, not because I need everyone to relate to dogs the way I do, but because I would genuinely love for my family to meet someone who has become part of my family too.”
