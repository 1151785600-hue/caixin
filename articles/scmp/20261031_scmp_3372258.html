Let them play: the Hong Kong playgrounds teaching children to take risks

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2296 words | 2026-10-31

A climbing frame, a two-metre slide and a handful of seats made to resemble animals that bounce around on a spring: Hong Kong playgrounds often leave children – and their guardians – underwhelmed, but those championing a new era of play hope to fix that.

Spanning 15,000 sq ft, the OC Commons Playground, situated next to the Olympian City shopping complex in Tai Kok Tsui, is part of a recent wave of playgrounds in Hong Kong – both public and private – that hark back to the free, unstructured play that was briefly encouraged during the late 1960s and 70s. Its bamboo-shaped poles with footholds, trampoline-helmed hills and metal plates that chime when stepped on have been designed to develop mobility, cognition, sensory awareness, problem-solving skills and tolerance for risk. But this example remains an outlier, and unless greater diversity in design is encouraged across the city, play areas may remain shackled to a predictable homogeneity that fails to ignite the imagination of children or test their appetite for risk.

Introduced in 1929, Hong Kong’s first urban playgrounds were havens for rowdy, unsupervised children amid economic hardship. After the Pacific War (1941-45) during World War II, as the colonial government began building housing estates for a mushrooming population, playgrounds opened at the bases of residential blocks, many offering little more than the four “S”s: slides, see-saws, swings and sandpits. Although they were prominent fixtures across the city’s public-housing developments, playgrounds were often treated as an afterthought by architects and engineers.

A major advancement in playground design came in the 60s, when features such as cement bridges, mazes and tunnels were fitted into the play areas of residential developments in Kennedy Town and Tsuen Wan, and on the Kai Tak Estate, writes arts practitioner Helen Fan Lok-yi, in her book The Abstract Playscapes of Hong Kong (2023). But the most representative sculptural play space arrived in 1969, when American artist Paul Selinger’s Shek Lei Playground introduced Kwai Chung to monumental forms that resembled a perforated belt, toppled chess pieces and cylinders stacked like waves: installations that were intended to stimulate free play and exploration. Although Selinger “failed in replicating his Shek Lei Playground model in Hong Kong”, writes Fan, “he influenced subsequent Hong Kong designers to experiment with mixing art, landscaping and play”.

In the late 70s and early 80s, however, mounting lawsuits over playground injuries in the United States cast doubt on the safety of sculptural playgrounds, a cautiousness that spread to Hong Kong. As the city tended to import its play equipment from the West, standardised, certified, mass-produced equipment prized for durability and ease of maintenance became the norm. Shek Lei Playground met its unheralded end in the 90s.

According to Fan, the shift suited Hong Kong families, who were increasingly hiring foreign domestic helpers to care for their children. In a rapidly developing city where parents were rarely supervising playground time, creativity came second to safety.

Nonetheless, in 1987, the non-governmental, non-profit Playright Children’s Play Association was established to promote and safeguard children’s right to play. Soon after, in 1989, Playright opened Hong Kong’s first “accessible” play area, King’s Park Children’s Playground, in Yau Ma Tei. Designed to include children with physical, sensory or developmental disabilities, it brought a rack of tubular audio instruments, a “touch wall”, strong sculptural qualities and diverse textures to engage young visitors.

In 2018, Playright introduced the city’s first “inclusive” playground – one that fosters shared social integration as well as multi-ability play – in Tuen Mun. Here, the recreational facilities include a water-play zone, an undulating area, trampolines and musical playthings, such as percussion instruments. Between those two openings, the term “free play” had become popular among parents and educators, who believed benefits such as enhanced concentration and interest in learning would follow when children could choose their own tools, ways to play, playmates and activity areas.

One year later, then-chief executive Carrie Lam Cheng Yuet-ngor announced the revitalisation of more than 170 public play areas managed by the Leisure and Cultural Services Department (LCSD) by 2024, “to make [Hong Kong’s] public play spaces more innovative and fun as well as to meet the aspirations of local residents”. Only 18 have been revamped so far, though, officials having cited the Covid-19 pandemic and the time needed to consult residents and process their requests as reasons for the delays.

Jacky Chan Wai-hin, head of play-environment development at Playright, which has been a consultant on at least one of the government’s 170 projects, says playgrounds support social well-being and resilience, and they also make sense for property developers eager to extend the duration of customer visits to shopping centres.

Since its Playright-designed OC Commons Playground opened earlier this year, Olympian City has seen a 10 per cent rise in footfall, attracting families from as far away as Tung Chung, says Bella Chhoa Peck-lim, chief commercial officer at Sino Group.

“OC Commons is in a great location because it’s right next to a mall that, in this case, acts more like a community living room, where people do more than just shop, but hang out,” says Chan. “Olympian City also has indoor bathrooms and resting spots that are paramount to a good playground.”

Other shopping plazas banking on the appeal of playgrounds include the Tsz Wan Shan Shopping Centre, where, in 2020, a water-play zone and climbing nets were added to the fifth floor. A year later, the 18,000 sq ft Moko Park reopened in Mong Kok’s Moko mall, boasting Hong Kong’s longest climbing wall and slides of varying heights.

Alan Cheung Kwok-lun, co-founder and managing director of One Bite Design Studio and the creative mind behind T Town’s The Wonderful Town of Play, in Tin Shui Wai, says the growing involvement of private developers with strict marketing goals adds pizzazz: “Private sectors’ No 1 priority is the ‘wow factor’; they want Hong Kong’s tallest, longest something whereas government playgrounds don’t care for marketing gimmicks.”

T Town’s 32,000 sq ft, Hong Kong Wetland Park-themed playground opened in December last year on the roof of the mall. Since its debut, footfall on the building’s second floor, connecting to the playground, has doubled, according to Katherine Lo Yan-kay, director of asset management (Hong Kong) at Link Asset Management, the developer behind T Town, drawing unprecedented numbers of visitors from nearby Yuen Long and Tuen Mun.

Like OC Commons, The Wonderful Town of Play has an adventure tower, a rope pyramid, a trampoline and a tyre swing. Cheung’s design also incorporates facts and graphics about the nearby conservation park and its wildlife: a large model of a black-faced spoonbill is perched above the vertical climber, for instance, and a three-dimensional hopscotch game, Mudskipper Jump, is paired with an information plaque about the amphibious fish.

While Cheung understands children are unlikely to visit a playground to learn about wetland species, the theme gives the space a distinct identity and offers guardians something to read while they are supervising their charges. Such intergenerational touches – even those as simple as increased shade and seating areas – are crucial to sustaining the relevance of a playground, he says. “Why can’t teenagers and adults play as well? We have this misconception because our society hasn’t catered to this demographic in our playground designs,” he says. “For the elderly, this could mean including simple workout equipment placed in spots where they can see their grandchildren. Meanwhile, teenagers may want nooks where they can hang out.”

A sense of ownership also encourages repeat visits. Another of Cheung’s projects – Siu Hei Court Play Space, a Tuen Mun playground refurbished in 2020 – is decorated with tiles adorned with artwork by nursery-school and kindergarten students. Although the play area may not be suitable for them just yet, Cheung hopes that by “making the space feel relevant”, those children will return as regular visitors when they are older.

Ownership also informs the 2024 Well-being Design Guide, a framework launched by the Housing Bureau and the Hong Kong Housing Authority. It intends, with the aid of feedback from residents, to transform public housing from blocks of basic shelter into vibrant communities.

In some cases, the reference suggests switching out drab playground sets for what Housing Department chief architect Tim Li Man-wai calls “communal fitness playscapes”, which welcome cross-generational activities rather than serving only as a children’s playground. Li sees this shift not as a loss of play space, but as a way to maximise use. He points to Yip Wong Estate, in Tuen Mun, where, instead of a conventional basketball court, netless rims were installed at various heights against a colourful, numbered wall. “Older children come here for basketball competitions, but the lower rims also invite young kids who may otherwise be deterred by the high hoops. Here, they can see who can dunk a ball into a hoop with a higher number, or a specific colour; they can really stretch their imagination,” says Li.

Before the new initiative, Li explains, residents’ feedback was collected through district councils, and projects tended to conform to a top-down approach. Now, discussions, interviews and workshops are conducted from the ground up, to identify pain points and possible remedies. “Society is changing, and so should the way we engage with our people when designing their living spaces. Before, residents didn’t have time to engage with us – they were likely too busy making a living and were happy with just a roof over their heads. However, with more public spaces and a widespread use of social media, people are more willing to voice their concerns.

“When residents are a part of the design process, they have ownership about what appears in that space and are therefore more accepting of the final product,” he says.

Ownership implies users’ trust in the designers to improve their lives. But designers, too, must have faith that their spaces will be treated with respect. Before its relaunch in 2021, Yi Pei Square Playground, in Tsuen Wan, shared space with a four-metre-wide emergency-vehicle access. A meaningful redesign meant removing the gate that separated it from shops.

“Park-goers and nearby residents were worried that, if we removed the gate, shop owners would spill their belongings into the park area,” says Kay Chan Wan-ki, a freelance product designer and community-interaction planner who worked on the redesign. “But we believed that people’s sense of discipline and respect for the common good would improve if they had well-designed public spaces that met their needs.”

Following the redesign, frequent policing by officials kept the shopkeepers in line.

“You can’t please everyone, but ultimately, it was a change for the better,” says Kay Chan.

Despite the innovation evident in parts of the city, she laments a continuing lack of creativity, adventure and nature in play. Playright’s Jacky Chan shares her concerns, adding that the city’s acceptance of, and education around, unconventional playgrounds must keep pace with the LCSD’s refurbishments, or risk a return to boring uniformity.

“A playground is not a stand-alone place. First, the public must get used to these new designs. Asians have a concept of ‘as safe as possible’, but those in the West believe in ‘as safe as necessary’. A safe playground isn’t meant to eliminate all risks – only the lethal, seriously dangerous ones. Little bumps and bruises are inevitable when children explore.”

Chan’s optimism is fuelled by past victories: “When sandpits first appeared in Hong Kong’s playgrounds, they were littered with cigarette butts and – in some cases – needles. This rarely happens any more, which is a great example of public education making enormous strides.”

Acceptance is one challenge, but construction and administration are quite another. “We face barriers from developers, the government, management and operational teams, because when these non-conventional playground facilities malfunction, it’s not just replacing a loose nail,” says Chan. “We must also look at workmanship, construction companies and suppliers. Do they have the know-how to make these new-age playgrounds? Only a few fields are related to playground design: architecture, landscape design and engineering. We can instil these concepts of risk, adventure and free play in students [of those disciplines] early on.”

Last December, Playright opened Hung Hom’s Dream Together Playground, which is anything but ordinary. As one of a rotating theme, until October 14, the park hosted a “pirate ship”, whose wooden bow was scribbled with children’s paintings, its flag and mast a sparkly sheet of tarp and an upside-down table. Scattered throughout were arm hooks fashioned out of tinfoil and half bottles, swords carved out of wood and “cannons” made from hole-punchers and plastic lids. Its outdoor expanse included sandpits and structures that were meant to be deconstructed and reassembled for all sorts of play. High steps and slim balancing beams pushed children beyond their comfort zone.

Chan calls this style of playground “half-and-half”, wherein children are tasked with co-creating their version of fun. Colouring on the floor is as encouraged as crafting a pulley system to deliver make-believe takeaway boxes. Since every visitor is a co-creator, the settings keep refreshing. (Another thing that’s unusual about the Dream Together Playground is that there is a charge to enter parts of the 23,680 sq ft site; HK$240 for one child-and-adult pair covers on-site supervision staff, materials and the costs of periodically changing the theme of the space.)

“Playright has been pushing this project for the past 20 years,” says Chan. “A playground like this is very rare in our city, but it’s a start.”
