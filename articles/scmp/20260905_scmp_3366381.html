5 years after sweeping ban, China’s tutoring industry still bleeding parents dry

SCMP | FREE | 1006 words | 2026-09-05

Yin Yue, a mother with a 15-year-old daughter, feels like after-school academic tutoring is everywhere again in Guangzhou, the city where she lives in southern China.

Five years ago, China launched a sweeping crackdown that drove much of the industry out of sight. But prep centres had quietly begun popping up again in popular commercial areas across the city, said Yin, a pharmaceutical sales manager in her 40s.

In Guangzhou’s shopping malls, these centres are now among the busiest tenants, pulling in crowds of parents and schoolchildren on weekends and weekday evenings, according to Yin. During the summer and winter holidays, they sometimes get so busy that nearby shops and restaurants see an uptick in business.

That would have been difficult to imagine a few years ago. In 2021, Beijing launched its “double reduction” campaign to outlaw for-profit tutoring in core school subjects such as maths and English, aiming to stamp out an academic rat race that was leading parents to spend eye-watering sums on extra classes for their children.

Thousands of providers closed or were forced to restructure during the intense national enforcement campaign that followed, as the authorities introduced tough punishments for tutors found operating without a licence.

Yet the crackdown did not dampen the intense competition in the country’s education system – or the demand for extra tutoring that it feeds. And that has allowed the industry to gradually make a comeback, aided by a pause in intensive, high-profile national clampdown campaigns.

Everyone is still locked in the same relentless race, spending just as much as before

Yin Yue, mother based in Guangdong province

Recent surveys and interviews with parents in major Chinese cities suggest that families across the country continue to spend heavily on after-school academic tutoring, even at a time when China’s uncertain job market and property downturn are dampening overall household consumption growth.

Yin’s daughter starts high school next month. To help her get a head-start on next semester’s curriculum, Yin spent more than 11,500 yuan (US$1,700) to send her on an intensive 12-day course covering maths, physics, chemistry and English.

In total, Yin said her family had spent close to 70,000 yuan on tutoring over the past year. She felt the extra classes were vital to help her daughter keep up with her peers – after all, they all had private tutors too.

“The stronger the students, the more their parents spend,” Yin said. “Everyone is still locked in the same relentless race, spending just as much as before.”

A survey of more than 2,100 parents in Zhongshan, another city in southern China, conducted by the Southern Metropolis Daily and released in late August, also found that after-school classes were still the norm.

Nearly 70 per cent of the parents surveyed said their children were currently attending subject-based after-school tutoring, while about another 20 per cent said they planned to arrange extra classes for them. Only 8.75 per cent said they had no plans for after-school tutoring.

More than 60 per cent of respondents said they spent at least 1,000 yuan per month on after-school tutoring, with 6.45 per cent spending over 5,000 yuan. This represents a big chunk of a typical household budget, given that Zhongshan’s per capita disposable income was about 35,000 yuan in the first half of 2026, according to official data.

The top three reasons parents gave for paying for tutoring were that their children were struggling to keep up with school coursework, the intense competition for admission to high schools and universities, and the fact that other students were also taking extra classes, according to the survey.

Among affluent Chinese families, the spending on tutoring can be far larger.

“In my son’s class, parents spend an average of 10,000 yuan a month on tutoring,” said Guan Jing, a parent based in Beijing.

Louis Tan, who runs a manufacturing business in Shenzhen, said he had budgeted 300,000 to 500,000 yuan for academic tutoring over the next three years, as his daughter prepared to take China’s national university entrance exams.

He had also set aside another 500,000 yuan for art classes, because his daughter hoped to attend China’s prestigious Central Academy of Fine Arts, Tan added.

“When I was young, a kid from a poor background could stand out on natural talent alone,” said Tan, who grew up in a poor rural community in southwestern China’s Guangxi Zhuang autonomous region. “Today, they have to compete against peers getting tonnes of extra coaching.”

If everyone else is taking after-school tutoring classes and your child doesn’t, she falls behind

Zhang Liang, father based in Hubei province

Families in smaller cities are also feeling the pressure. In Jingshan, a county-level city in central China’s Hubei province, truck driver Zhang Liang said he had spent more than 10,000 yuan a semester on tutoring for his 9-year-old daughter, plus 2,800 yuan for 14 days of summer classes.

The costs are a burden for the family, which earns about 15,000 yuan a month, but Zhang feels the classes are important for his daughter’s future.

“If everyone else is taking after-school tutoring classes and yours doesn’t, she falls behind,” Zhang said. He worries that his kidney stones could one day stop him from driving long-haul trucks, leaving the family unable to afford the tutoring fees.

Education appears to be one of the few non-negotiable spending categories for Chinese households at a time of heightened economic uncertainty. China’s per capita consumption rose 3.7 per cent in the first half of the year, while spending on education, culture and recreation increased 4.9 per cent, according to official data.

Between 2020 and 2025, education, culture and recreation spending in China grew 71.7 per cent, National Bureau of Statistics data showed.

Xiong Bingqi, director of the 21st Century Education Research Institute, said the core problem was the intense competition for places at China’s top schools, which extended from senior high school through to university. The fight for school places helps explain the resilience of demand for private tutoring, according to Xiong.
