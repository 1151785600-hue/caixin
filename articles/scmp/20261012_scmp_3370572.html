From laser shields to iPhone detectors: inside China’s booming secrecy industry

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1778 words | 2026-10-13

A group of Chinese officials are in the middle of a highly sensitive meeting involving critical decisions yet to be made behind closed doors, with documents stamped “classified” spread across the table.

Suddenly, an invisible laser beams into the room, sweeping across a glass window and allowing the contents of the meeting to be transmitted elsewhere.

China’s Ministry of State Security laid out this seemingly futuristic scenario six months ago, warning government departments and other entities against a new form of laser-based eavesdropping potentially conducted by foreign spies.

Sophisticated contactless surveillance technology like this relies on detecting vibrations in glass windows caused by sound waves inside a room. By directing an invisible laser beam at a window, receiving the reflected signal and analysing it, an operator can reconstruct speech from within the room, according to the ministry.

Dramatic and distant as that scenario may seem, solutions to these risks exist. Amid heightened fears of foreign espionage and data leaks, Chinese manufacturers are paying keen attention to client needs and racing to capture the skyrocketing market.

To fulfil the need to protect official secrets, they offer devices designed specifically to detect such laser eavesdropping tactics, as well as a growing list of devices and training to meet the mounting national security requirements in one of the world’s largest bureaucratic systems.

The boom coincides with technological advances, rising espionage fears and Beijing’s tightening national security requirements, creating new opportunities for manufacturers of secrecy-protection equipment.

As demand spreads from national authorities to local governments, companies are diversifying their products and services while seeking growth overseas, particularly in countries with close security ties to Beijing.

Lasers, curtains and Apple products

Any outsider would be surprised at the immense range of devices and technologies to maintain information secrecy available in the Chinese market.

Product catalogues from several secrecy-protection equipment companies obtained by the South China Morning Post showcase diverse products meant to address a host of espionage threats.

In the laser intrusion example, a monitoring system is capable of detecting and issuing alerts over incoming laser beams while identifying the direction from which the laser originates.

Another product uses laser-protection film that absorbs lasers of particular wavelengths while maintaining window transparency.

Then there are specialised curtains made from electromagnetic-shielding fabric that can block electronic signals and electromagnetic interference while providing protection against infrared thermal-imaging surveillance.

An insider said the products currently seeing the strongest demand were systems capable of detecting and blocking unidentified signals, including Wi-fi and Bluetooth signals from devices that are not on China’s approved whitelist for official use.

“Many advanced eavesdropping or covert surveillance systems are capable of wireless control via Wi-fi or Bluetooth from distances exceeding 100 metres (328 feet) – a range that may well extend beyond the defensive perimeter of physical security measures like on-site security scanners,” the insider said.

“As a result, systems designed to counter unidentified Bluetooth, Wi-fi and other signals have become a popular requirement.”

Among these unidentified-signal detection systems is another notable category: equipment that searches for signals emitted by foreign-made devices, particularly Apple devices, according to the catalogues.

Citing national security concerns, some Chinese government ministries and agencies have since 2023 required employees to stop using iPhones in the workplace.

Beijing has long restricted the use of foreign-made electronic devices and vehicles in certain areas of government compounds on national security grounds.

In 2021, Beijing barred vehicles made by US electric car manufacturer Tesla from entering government facilities and military compounds because of concerns about espionage.

The insider said covert anti-recording equipment was another growth area as companies updated and developed their products, with such devices mainly used when setting up secure meeting rooms and during overseas activities involving Chinese personnel.

While most devices sold by these Chinese companies are made domestically – product catalogues show that at least one also imports Russian-made anti-recording jammers to sell to Chinese customers.

Skyrocketing demand

According to an employee at a Chinese counter-espionage equipment manufacturer, the industry has existed for years but demand for its products surged around 2023.

“The company has been making secrecy-protection equipment for 20 years, and business had been neither particularly strong nor weak,” the employee said. “But since [2023], the value of orders has increased substantially year after year, and customers have become much easier to find.”

The company now has an annual output value of about 100 million yuan (US$14.9 million), according to the employee, who requested anonymity citing the sensitivity of the matter.

With brighter prospects for the sector, the company had also become more willing to invest in the development of more advanced secrecy-protection equipment designed to respond to emerging technologies, the employee added.

It is not entirely clear what drove the jump in demand in 2023, but it coincided with a series of major counter-espionage and data security developments in China.

In August that year, the Ministry of State Security set up an official account on WeChat, the country’s largest social media platform, churning out daily posts about espionage risks to the public.

Set up just one month after a new counter-espionage law came into effect, the account publishes weekly updates on typical cases of espionage and information theft to warn the public and officials of threats.

The ministry has also called for society as a whole to “jointly build a line of defence for secrecy protection and engage those in counter-espionage”.

Also in 2023, China passed major amendments to its 2014 anti-espionage law, expanding the definition of espionage activities and the investigative powers of national security law enforcement agencies.

The ministry has repeatedly warned Chinese officials, students and tourists against recruitment traps and foreign intelligence operatives. The message comes against a backdrop of intense competition between Beijing and Washington, alongside its Western partners.

In July 2023, in a rare public comment, then-CIA director William Burns said the American agency was making “progress” in rebuilding its spy network in China after severe setbacks a decade earlier.

Beijing responded by declaring it would take “all necessary” countermeasures.

A year later, in 2024, China updated its Law on Guarding State Secrets, the first major change to the law since 2010.

Explaining the amendments, state news agency Xinhua said the revised law “makes clear that the state encourages and supports research and application of science and technology for secrecy protection … and fosters the secrecy-protection technology industry”.

The law also explicitly places secrecy protection within President Xi Jinping’s “holistic approach to national security”.

As a retired official from a local government secrecy-protection department noted: “Over the past decade, national security has gradually evolved from a relatively narrow and specialised field into a key principle within China’s economic, administrative and social governance.”

“China has made a series of adjustments to its policies, regulations and the way government departments operate to promote this shift,” said the retired official, who also requested anonymity citing the sensitivity of the matter.

Since 2024, the US Central Intelligence Agency has released Chinese-language online videos that explicitly seek to recruit informants from within China’s government and military.

Dennis Wilder, a former senior US intelligence official and policymaker, said the rapid growth of China’s counter-espionage industry stemmed “in part [from the fact] that both offensive and defensive intelligence collected remotely is growing in sophistication as AI comes into play”.

“China’s security system has always been massive,” Wilder said. “The technology of espionage and counter-espionage is advancing. It’s a predictable reaction to have new tools.”

Who buys the equipment and what they want

The industry insider said China had about 10 sizeable companies specialising in secrecy-protection equipment, many of which had some degree of connection to the defence industry.

The main clients of Chinese secrecy-protection equipment are military units, government departments at the provincial level or above, and state-owned enterprises dealing with state secrets, according to the insider, who noted particularly strong demand from companies in the defence sector.

The insider told the SCMP that the business was expanding into some local government departments where “construction of their secrecy-protection systems is still only at an early stage”.

Such ventures could already be accelerating.

The 2024 revision to the state secrets law stipulated that “governments at or above the county level shall strengthen secrecy-protection infrastructure and support the development and application of key secrecy-protection technical products”.

The law further required governments at or above the county level to include secrecy-protection work in their budgets.

According to the former official, “As secrecy protection develops into a mature industry backed by government regulations and procurement budgets, this is expected to create a self-reinforcing cycle in which security requirements and the market for protective technologies continue to expand.”

Anti-espionage training support

Besides the sales of devices, some secrecy-protection equipment companies help train officials travelling overseas in counter-surveillance and anti-eavesdropping techniques, according to another source familiar with the matter.

The source said companies were required to set up a room containing concealed surveillance devices, allowing officials to practise counter-surveillance skills and ultimately locate the hidden equipment.

“We teach the trainees how to use different types of portable counter-surveillance equipment, as well as the possible types and locations of eavesdropping devices. The training normally lasts one or two weeks and involves several rounds of assessment.”

“Sometimes the surveillance device they need to find is a stone inside a flowerpot. Sometimes it’s a piece of decoration attached to a dome light. They can be very well concealed,” the source added.

In January, China’s National Administration of State Secrets Protection published an article calling for stronger supervision, management and security-awareness training for personnel sent overseas on official assignments.

Eyeing growth overseas

Counting successes within China’s own borders, these secrecy-protection equipment companies are looking to expand their business overseas.

“Overseas sales of secrecy-protection equipment mainly involve government departments in Central Asian and African countries, particularly countries in those regions participating in the Belt and Road Initiative, including Tajikistan and Kazakhstan,” the source said.

Secrecy-protection equipment was generally not expensive, the source added, with a complete system typically priced at between 100,000 and 200,000 yuan (about US$15,000 to US$30,000), making it easily affordable for government agencies in developing countries.

A secrecy-protection equipment firm, which asked not to be identified, told the SCMP that its systems could be provided with support for local languages, including Russian, and that the company also offered comprehensive training on how to use the equipment.

Beijing has meanwhile continued to expand security cooperation with Central Asian and African countries, including exchanges and training programmes.
