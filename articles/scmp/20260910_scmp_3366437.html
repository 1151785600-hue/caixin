Social media must join the fight against teen suicides

SCMP | FREE | 1050 words | 2026-09-10

As we observe World Suicide Prevention Day on September 10, the global community reflects on a sobering reality: suicide remains a leading cause of death among young people. Every year, more than 720,000 people die by suicide – each life a story cut short, a family shattered, a community left to grapple with unbearable loss.

In Hong Kong, the Jockey Club Centre for Suicide Research and Prevention (CSRP) at the University of Hong Kong estimates last year’s suicide rate at 13.0 per 100,000 in the population – a slight improvement from 13.8 in 2024. But let me be clear: this is no cause for complacency. The rate among our young people, particularly boys, remains stubbornly high, and that is where my deepest concern lies.

We can no longer afford to ignore the role of social media in the mental health crisis facing our teenagers. Social media has become the landscape of modern adolescence. In the United States, reports indicate that up to 95 per cent of adolescents use social media, with over one-third reporting near-constant use. For young people, these digital spaces are not merely entertainment – they are where identities are formed, relationships are navigated and self-worth is measured.

Their impact on mental health deserves attention. For decades, my team at the CSRP has tracked the data, studied the patterns and advocated for evidence-based solutions. The evidence is overwhelming. Excessive use of social media is correlated with poor mental health and deteriorating family relationships. The causes of youth suicide are complex – home and school problems are major, intertwined contributors – but the digital environment has become a significant amplifier of distress.

We have long known that irresponsible media reporting can trigger the Werther effect – where sensational coverage of suicide leads to imitative behaviour. Social media has made this problem exponentially more complicated. A single post, whether a careless comment or misguided “awareness” message, can reach thousands of vulnerable young people instantly. Specificity in describing methods or locations – even when framed as a warning – drives contagion risk.

Yet the responsibility does not rest solely with individuals. The platforms design their algorithms to maximise engagement, often at the expense of well-being. The recent US$18 billion settlement by Meta Platforms – agreeing to examine its algorithm design to improve mental health among young users – should be a wake-up call for the industry.

The tragic case of American Sophie Rottenberg, who ended her life after months of confiding in a ChatGPT therapist, illustrates the dangers of unregulated digital environments. In our work on chatbot development, we have observed that overly empathetic responses can inadvertently reinforce a user’s negative thoughts, create a dangerous feedback loop and validate harmful ideation.

For years, we have partnered with local media to promote safe reporting practices. But the landscape has shifted dramatically. Passive audiences have become content creators. The old guidelines were not designed for this reality. That is why, with the support of The Better Hong Kong Foundation, we worked with the Hong Kong Press Council to produce an updated 2026 edition. For the first time, we have incorporated targeted guidance for social media alongside traditional recommendations.

So, what does responsible social media use look like? First, pause before posting. If your post describes methods, means or locations in specific detail, do not share it. Do not speculate on causes or spread rumours. Always obtain consent before sharing someone else’s experience with suicide.

Second, promote hope, not despair. Whenever suicide is mentioned, attach help-seeking resources. These can make a tangible difference. Share recovery stories that highlight coping strategies and resilience.

Third, use mindful language. Avoid phrases that frame suicide as noble, peaceful or an act of empowerment. Such framing normalises it for at-risk individuals. Describe the pain, not the act.

But individual action is not enough. We must hold platform operators accountable. With their sophisticated algorithms, these companies have the ability to identify at-risk users – the same algorithms that deliver personalised advertisements can be repurposed for early detection and intervention. Platforms worry that intervening might affect traffic volume. But we cannot accept profit motives as a justification for inaction.

Platforms should detect and remove harmful content using automated tools, but they must route flagged items to human moderators before removal to avoid overreach. They should deprioritise borderline high-risk content in recommendation feeds while reserving outright restriction for clear violations like content that promotes suicide methods, pacts or graphic acts. They must act swiftly against cyberbullying, which remains a major class of harmful content. And they should pin help resources prominently alongside sensitive posts.

I am pleased Meta has suggested a two-hour daily limit for young users and content filtering for children under 15. These are potentially effective measures. But we need systemic change.

Our government has committed to continuing the three-tier system to support mental health in secondary and upper-primary schools. That is welcome. However, I must highlight a critical point often overlooked: by our estimates, about 75 per cent of suicides occur among those who do not have depressive symptoms. By focusing solely on depression, we are missing most of those at risk. This is a central reason our suicide rate remains stubbornly high despite increased mental health awareness.

To truly move the needle, we must think beyond clinical interventions. We must promote a healthy lifestyle: a balanced diet, regular exercise and adequate sleep are all essential. We must make schools and homes safe places where young people feel valued and supported. And we must give them something to look forward to – a meaningful future, hope and a strong support network.

There is no silver bullet to solving the suicide problem among teenagers. The path forward is difficult and requires coordinated effort across families, schools, governments and technology companies. But it is not impossible. We have seen progress: the rate has come down from 13.8 to 13.0. That shows our efforts are working. We just cannot afford to ease up.
