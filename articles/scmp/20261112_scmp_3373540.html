How Hong Kong’s humble plastic stool became a design-world darling

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1617 words | 2026-11-12

Whether you are eating at the Queen Street Cooked Food Market in Sheung Wan, weaving through a back alley in Tsim Sha Tsui to avoid the crowds on Nathan Road, or waiting for the bus at a covered stop in a rural village in the New Territories, there is one object that has come to symbolise a moment of respite in non-stop Hong Kong. It invites anyone, regardless of age or background, to sit and pause: the plastic stool.

Few objects have transcended time and space quite like this modest piece of plastic. First locally manufactured in the 1960s by Star Industrial under its Red A plasticware brand, the plastic stool was designed as an economical choice to make the most of limited spending power and limited space in the small flats of post-war Hong Kong.

Lightweight and inexpensive, the stools in the Red A collection came in many shapes and sizes, but the most iconic of all might be the taller, rounded, four-legged iteration – usually red, with a small hole in the centre of its fluted seat to drain water or chain a stack together.

By the 70s, plastic stools had become fixtures in restaurants, wet markets, shops and homes, their popularity driven by sheer practicality and resilience, whether made by Star Industrial or not.

In the decades since, however, the unassuming stool has found its way into art galleries, exhibitions and collections, transforming from a merely utilitarian item of furniture into a piece of Hong Kong heritage.

Plastic stools join a long line of everyday objects that have transcended their humble origins to become cultural symbols, alongside the Coca-Cola bottles and Campbell’s canned soups of Andy Warhol fame, and Marcel Duchamp’s bicycle wheel, one of his “readymades”.

As architect Roger Wu Tsan-sum, who also serves as the director of business development and projects at the Hong Kong Design Centre and the president of the Hong Kong Institute of Architectural Conservationists, puts it, “the plastic stool is just part of our DNA”.

“Every Hongkonger has a personal relationship with the red stool,” he says. “When I see that stool, I have memories of myself sitting on it doing my homework, with my grandmother by me.”

Post-World War II, a drive for reconstruction and innovation shaped a generation of creatives who went on to challenge conventions in the arts, in music, painting, film and design. In Hong Kong, Wu explains, that generation found a blank canvas, as the city’s colonial history had left it without a single dominant aesthetic.

Meanwhile, the local manufacturing industry took off as the city restructured its economy from entrepôt to producer. Hong Kong became the third-largest textile exporter in the world, and the leading fabricator of plastic flowers. Decades of mass production informed the city’s identity.

Among those on the up-and-up in Hong Kong’s plastic industry was Star Industrial, founded in 1949. It quickly found success as the city’s main plasticware manufacturer for household staples, producing more than 1,700 items under its Red A brand, from buckets and water canteens to lampshades, trays, children’s briefcases and, of course, plastic stools.

Wu says the latter is “built on our culture of efficiency. It survives typhoons, heavy rain and heat. It stays unchanged even in the dustiest closet”. Its sturdiness and versatility – as a chair, table or step, stored indoors or exposed to the elements – ensured its place in our everyday lives.

Over the years, as Hong Kong evolved and old neighbourhoods were redeveloped, authorities tightened regulations on street-level commerce to ease pedestrian congestion and improve public hygiene. In the early 70s, the government stopped issuing new hawker licences; as a result, the number of casual, street-side eateries – such as dai pai dongs – started to decline. And as the economy and living conditions improved, demand for the plastic stool diminished. But as it faded into the background, it began to accumulate a different kind of currency: character.

According to Wu, advancements in technology, especially artificial intelligence, are contributing to rapid global shifts, “and the concept of reality, of originality, is being challenged. So, art is coming back to something concrete, something we can touch, something thought by a human”.

From a revival of traditional crafts to a resurgence of point-and-shoot cameras and a renaissance in printmaking and handmade zines, nostalgia for the old, tangible and tactile is taking many forms, and the plastic stool that has served Hongkongers for more than half a century is finding a new home in the world of art and design, as creatives introduce the household item to a new generation.

Contemporary ink painter Angel Hui Hoi-kiu used them as makeshift plinths and props in her Angel Hui Pop Up Store (2024) multimedia installation, to support the display of her ceramic handbags, inspired by the craftsmanship of the Yuan and Ming dynasties.

In 2025, the international interior-design showcase Maison&Objet Intérieurs, held at the Hong Kong Convention and Exhibition Centre, welcomed an installation by local practice Lim + Lu and CL3 Architects’ William Lim Ooi-lee, featuring thousands of stools. Rethinking its form for a modern environment, their goal was to transform the plastic pieces into striking ceiling and floor lamps, shelves and other structures.

“We wanted to reinterpret a humble yet iconic object found throughout the city,” says Elaine Manzi Lu, co-founder and managing director of Lim + Lu. By removing the stools from their original context and setting them against “luxurious marbles, rich fabrics and silk carpets”, the installation sought to evoke comfort and familiarity while presenting the soul of Hong Kong to a global audience. Staged in “Le Club”, a gathering space at Maison&Objet Intérieurs, it reflected Lu’s belief that the plastic stool is universal.

“Everywhere around the world, different cultures have their [own] different plastic seats,” says industrial designer and artist Law Chi-hin, who creates under the moniker OrangeTerry. “In Europe, it’s the white Monobloc chair. In Hong Kong, we have the Red A plastic stool.”

“In Hong Kong, the Red A plastic stool never belongs to you – it belongs to every Hongkonger”

Law Chi-hin, aka OrangeTerry

Law argues that these plastic seats have become a common language understood by all, even if they are made from different plastics and to different designs. What connects them all is their affordability and utility. Leveraging this universality, he uses them in his art “to send a message” about cultural kinship. As he explains, the stool embodies a shared Hong Kong identity: “In Hong Kong, the Red A plastic stool never belongs to you – it belongs to every Hongkonger.”

His series, Singular Stack (2025), fuses seven Red A plastic stools into one pedestal-like piece, with the deliberately profligate act serving as a critique of overconsumption: “It’s ridiculous, if you think about it. I’m using a very wasteful method to make one stool for one person. But that’s exactly the point: for people to look at it and think it wasteful.”

He also likes playing around with the stackable properties of the plastic stool. “We don’t have much space in Hong Kong – that’s why we like the stools so much and why they became such an important element in our daily lives, in our home as furniture.”

Painter Joannah “Jobey” Hon Pui-yan agrees. Growing up between Hong Kong and Australia, with parents from contrasting socio-economic backgrounds and tensions that could arise around the dinner table, she feels the Hong Kong plastic stool was an equaliser, one that required everyone to eventually sit down at the same level, on an even playing field.

Once sat on them, around the same table, “we were all [just] Hongkongers eating delicious beef brisket for HK$40 [US$5]”, she says, the parity afforded by the stool settling the differences between her family members.

It is one of the reasons she includes the piece of furniture in her art. Another, she says, is to archive local culture.

Hon made a name for herself with large-scale paintings of everyday scenes of Hong Kong. “I’m interested in iconic Hong Kong characters who represent the heart of the city, and the plastic stools are inherently part of them,” she says.

Plastic stools are prominent in Slipper Hitters (2026), her portrait of the villain-hitting practitioners who set up their stalls underneath the Canal Road Flyover, between Causeway Bay and Wan Chai.

“As artists, we ought to take our time and paint, with our own hands, what we believe is worth remembering,” says Hon. “Our world is changing so fast, and I’m worried the next generation won’t have these villain-hitters under the bridge any more, or these red plastic stools.”

As the plastic stool migrates from wet markets to white cubes, Wu wonders what tomorrow holds for the object if it is no longer in daily use. If the piece of furniture still exists in the years to come, he asks, “will future generations have the same emotional connection to it”.

At the end of the day, the plastic stool may not reclaim its place in every Hong Kong home, but it is unlikely to vanish entirely from the city and our collective consciousness. As Wu sees it, its future is less about style and more about persistence. “Fifty years later, or 1,000 years later, there’s still gravity, and we’ll still [need somewhere to] sit,” he says.

Whether the plastic stool survives on Hong Kong’s pavements or inside artworks and in galleries, it will keep doing the same job: holding the weight of people’s bodies, and the weight of their memories.
