How China’s anti-corruption fight became Xi Jinping’s most important policy

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2005 words | 2026-08-28

The South China Morning Post has launched a major data project diving into President Xi Jinping’s signature anti-graft campaign. In the first part of a series accompanying the data project, Phoebe Zhang looks at how the campaign became one of the Communist Party’s most important policies.

In the early years of President Xi Jinping’s first term, when graft fighters were taking down senior officials in ways that raised eyebrows, Chinese authorities constantly had to remind the public that the anti-corruption campaign was not “just a gust of wind”.

At that point, Beijing had already locked up dozens of senior officials, including Zhou Yongkang, a former member of the Communist Party’s Politburo Standing Committee, the highest echelon in China’s political hierarchy. Before his downfall in 2013, Zhou had been one of China’s most influential politicians.

Concerns were so widespread that the anti-corruption push would be a flash in the pan, especially at the local level, that Wang Qishan, then the party’s top anti-corruption chief, warned that local governments might fall back on temporary “movement-style” crackdowns.

A decade later, few need convincing as the anti-corruption fight expands, netting record numbers of senior officials and unearthing new types of graft involving cryptocurrency schemes and clandestine proxies.

A new analysis by the South China Morning Post found that the campaign has widened since its inception. Last year, anti-corruption agencies handed down punishments to 983,000 people, with the fallout ranging from mild warnings to investigations and criminal prosecution. The number of senior officials taken down last year was three times that of 2013.

The SCMP’s data bank project also found that from 2013 to 2025, 60 per cent of senior officials felled by China’s anti-corruption drive were serving in regional governments. The campaign has hit provincial administrations particularly hard, with resource-rich areas – notably Jiangxi, Liaoning, Shanxi and Hebei provinces, as well as Inner Mongolia, autonomous region – recording the highest number of dismissals.

What began as a crackdown on economic graft has evolved into a systemic overhaul of bureaucratic conduct. While bribery remains the main legal charge, the definition of corruption has expanded to include party discipline and adherence to policy directives.

By now, most experts agree that the anti-corruption campaign is among Xi’s most important policies, and one that is key to his vision of China’s future and the party’s ability to rule for generations to come.

And the party has shown no interest in pulling any punches.

During a January meeting of the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection, the party’s top anti-graft body, Xi declared that “the situation in the fight against corruption remains grave and complex”, urging officials to come up with new methods to discover, identify and tackle all types of wrongdoing.

Just last month, China’s top leadership announced that party governance would be the focus of the fifth plenary session of the Communist Party’s 20th Central Committee, expected to be held in October. This meeting is likely to be the party’s most important gathering ahead of its 21st national congress in autumn of next year. The five-yearly party congress is likely to feature a leadership reshuffle and the endorsement of a fourth term for Xi.

In a Xinhua report on the coming plenary session, the Politburo said party self-governance “faces new circumstances and challenges” and called for “consistently exercising full and rigorous governance over the party” – a phrase that typically means fighting corruption and tightening party discipline.

No end point

While generations of Communist Party leaders have pledged to uproot corruption, Xi has tried to convince the country from the outset that his fight is different.

In 2013, at the second plenary session of the party’s 18th Central Commission for Discipline Inspection (CCDI), Xi pledged to fight “tigers and flies at the same time” – targeting not only high-ranking officials but also misconduct at the grass-roots level – to underscore a policy of “no forbidden zones, full coverage and zero tolerance”.

Soon, Beijing introduced a set of “eight-point austerity rules” to restrict party cadre behaviour. These include banning the use of luxury cars and gifts, banning red carpets, floral arrangements and grand receptions during inspections, simplifying meetings, reducing unnecessary documents and limiting overseas visits.

The rules are still in effect today.

The anti-corruption campaign has deepened and broadened to cover all policy areas and sectors, including security, finance, education and the graft fighters themselves. In its first three years, it took down top leaders in all 31 provincial-level jurisdictions. The campaign has also hit the military’s top brass, especially in the past two years.

The push has become more institutionalised as well. In 2018, the National Supervisory Commission (NSC) was created and merged with the CCDI, giving the party’s anti-corruption campaign a legal basis.

Beijing has sought to supplement laws with party regulations. More than 70 per cent of the Communist Party’s regulations have been enacted since 2012.

China has also overhauled its corruption-fighting agencies to reflect a “vertical management” style, giving more oversight to higher authorities.

It has reinvented the inspection regime, inherited from Imperial China but largely inactive in the years before Xi, so inspectors dispatched from Beijing look for signs of corruption at the local level.

Over the past 14 years, Xi has repeatedly emphasised that his anti-corruption campaign has no end point, warning that no official should harbour any illusions of impunity.

At the 20th party congress in 2022, Xi said the anti-corruption fight should “never rest, not even for a minute”.

Staying disciplined

Corruption is the “greatest threat” facing the party, Xi said in a speech at the 19th party congress in 2017.

At times, he has argued that purging corruption is crucial for the Communist Party to escape China’s “historical cycles” of dynastic rise and collapse, and ensure its longevity.

Xi has said that corruption hinders governance and economic development. “If corruption is allowed to spread, it will eventually lead to the destruction of a party and the fall of a government,” he has warned.

Li Jian, a former researcher with the Publicity Department of the Central Committee, wrote in January that the reason that the party could not afford to lose the fight against corruption was because graft harmed the party’s vitality and ability to fight.

“[Corruption] seriously undermines fair and just market rules, distorts the efficiency of resource allocation, erodes the fruits of reform and development, and damages the business environment as well as social fairness and justice,” Li wrote. He concluded that there was a need to create a “sound political environment and a level playing field for high-quality development”.

“Combating corruption is like sailing against the current – if you do not advance, you will fall back,” said Zhiqun Zhu, a professor of political science and international relations at Bucknell University.

“Long-term goals such as rejuvenation of the Chinese nation or unification with Taiwan cannot be achieved any time soon, but the anti-corruption campaign has yielded concrete results, with a cleaner government, a more professional and efficient bureaucracy, and a more loyal and dedicated leadership team,” said Zhu, who is also director of Bucknell’s China Institute.

Alfred Wu, associate professor at the Lee Kuan Yew School of Public Policy at the National University of Singapore, said Xi’s views on corruption were shared by his predecessors, though they never pushed the fight to the same level.

“Jiang Zemin had repeatedly said that ‘anti-corruption is a serious political struggle that matters to the life and death of the party and the nation’, while Hu Jintao said at the 18th party congress that failure to tackle the issue could prove fatal to the party,” he said, referring to China’s previous two presidents.

Beijing has also made the enforcement of its strategies part of the anti-corruption campaign.

The latest revision of party disciplinary regulations explicitly prohibits failing to implement the “new concept for development and high-quality development” – Beijing’s term for its new growth model, coined after Xi came to power.

This violation has been mentioned in the investigation findings of a handful of disgraced senior officials, including Lan Tianli, the former party secretary of Guangxi Zhuang autonomous region, who was investigated last year.

Devendra Kumar, an associate fellow at the Centre of Excellence for Himalayan Studies at Shiv Nadar University, said the corruption-fighting efforts under Xi were ultimately about discipline.

“In this context, anti-corruption in China cannot be understood in the narrow sense of deviations from institutional rules and laws,” he said.

“Rather, it is fundamentally about ‘discipline’, which encompasses not only compliance with regulations but also officials’ political disposition and, increasingly, aspects of their family life, including whom they associate with, where family members work, and where they travel.”

Kumar added that the campaign was aimed at enforcing the party’s agenda and preferred modes of thinking through measures such as the campaign on the correct view of political performance, which began in March.

With local governments across China preparing for a leadership shuffle, Xi said a “correct view” of performance was particularly vital to ensure new appointments prioritised long-term public benefit over short-term vanity projects.

According to Xinhua, local party leaders were tasked with the “top priority” of implementing top leadership’s decisions with “unwavering focus”.

Party discipline also figures heavily in “Xi Jinping Thought on Party Building”, a new branch of the Chinese leader’s political thought unveiled in June. Its key tenets include insistence on “a centralised, unified leadership of the party Central Committee” as well as “full and rigorous party self-governance” and ensuring that “officials do not have the audacity, opportunity or desire to commit corruption”.

Kumar added that there were similarities between Xi and his predecessors’ signature policies in that they all aimed to extend party rule and impose discipline.

For example, he said, Mao launched the “continuous revolution” – an ideological extension of the civil war the party won in 1949 – to prevent the party from becoming a stagnant bureaucracy. Xi, by contrast, sees bureaucratisation as a key remedy, with anti-corruption as a tool to fix the problems arising from the party’s organisational structure, according to Kumar.

Communist Party institutions lack mechanisms such as public oversight and transparency to foster bottom-up accountability. As a result, he said, top-down methods of enforcing discipline and accountability were necessary for maintaining order and preventing deviations from established regulations and norms, even during periods of weak leadership.

Xi laid bare some of those concerns in the report he delivered at the 20th party congress in 2022.

He said that China had made great achievements as a result of reform, opening up and socialist modernisation, but “a number of prominent issues and problems – some of which had been building for years and others which were just emerging – demanded urgent action”.

Among them, there were a “lack of clear understanding and effective action as well as a slide towards weak, hollow and watered-down party leadership in practice”, according to the Chinese leader. He added that despite repeated warnings, ills including pointless formalities, bureaucratism, hedonism and extravagance persisted.

Another problem Xi saw as serious was “privilege-seeking mindsets and practices”, with some “deeply shocking cases of corruption”.

“The intensity with which Xi has pursued disciplinary campaigns must be understood in context,” Kumar said.

“When Xi came to power, party institutions were not sufficiently effective in ‘caging’ cadres in their day-to-day functioning, and cadres’ ideological commitment was also considered weak.”

He noted there were structural reasons for the campaign to continue, as it had become part of Xi’s broader agenda of revitalising the party, both ideologically and institutionally, and the anti-corruption campaign and institutional changes of the past decade had embedded a “disciplinary regime” into the party’s day-to-day governance.

“Consequently, we should expect an intensification of anti-corruption efforts in the run-up to the 21st party congress,” Kumar said.
