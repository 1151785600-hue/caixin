China's first national standard for paid medical-escort services took effect this month, developed under the State Administration for Market Regulation and the National Standardization Administration. It defines providers as registered organizations, addressing unclear service boundaries and uneven quality as an aging population drives demand.

[para.

3

]

Providers must be legally registered and establish systems for personnel management, privacy protection, safety-risk assessments, emergency response, contracts and fees. They must publicly disclose charges and sign contracts spelling out both parties' rights and obligations before providing services, rather than relying on verbal promises or undisclosed fees.

Escorts may collect appointment tickets, guide patients, record doctors' instructions, handle payments and provide emotional support. They are barred from interpreting test results, participating in medical procedures, offering medical advice, changing medication regimens or choosing treatment. They also may not conceal, alter or selectively relay critical medical information, or sign legally binding medical documents.

While the standard doesn't explicitly prohibit individuals from working independently, its focus on institutional providers could gradually push freelancers who don't meet its requirements out of the market.

Demand outpaces supply. The sector began in 2015, entered rapid growth in 2022 and peaked at 501 companies in 2023, per data from the 2026 Shanghai assistance-services expo. A 2025 China Association of Social Welfare and Senior Service report estimated a nationwide shortfall of 830,000 to 2.2 million escorts; Beijing alone lacked about 150,000.

Against a weak job market, the work has become an option for middle-aged people seeking new employment, and local authorities hope it can absorb flexible-employment workers. But the industry lacks clear occupational-entry requirements: escorts aren't in the national occupational classification, competence varies widely, fees aren't standardized and disputes are hard to resolve.

Low entry barriers and flexible hours drew many independent workers, but few persist—one Guangzhou escort said only a dozen of 200-300 training-course members ultimately pursued the work, while some switched to reselling expensive medicines or scalping hospital appointments.

Institutional providers typically train workers in infection control, first aid and operating procedures. Su Mingwei, founder of Beijing Hulaokang Technology, described a recruitment, training, certification and placement process with about 10 days of in-person training. She said the standard's biggest contribution is defining service boundaries and specifying organizational provision—a shift from a fragmented workforce toward a standardized industry.

Human-resources and social-security authorities are organizing specialized competency assessments; Su's company administered the first such exam in Beijing's Xicheng district in October under the "Beijing Housekeeping" program. Some independent escorts could respond by establishing their own businesses, including one-person companies, as registration and qualification requirements become more demanding.

Well-funded competitors are entering—Didi, JD.com, Alibaba and Meituan have expanded into medical-escort services targeting older people's healthcare spending. Hospitals are forming their own teams: Peking University First Hospital created one with three dedicated nurses and 84 flexible nurses, charging 190 yuan ($28) for two hours and 299 yuan for four hours, plus 100 yuan per additional hour, under a performance-based compensation system.
