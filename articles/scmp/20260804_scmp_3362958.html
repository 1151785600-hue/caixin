VIP, APP, PPT: Are English abbreviations harming China’s cultural confidence?

SCMP | FREE | 1007 words | 2026-08-04

A Chinese speaker today might describe their day at work as first making a “PPT” (a PowerPoint presentation) and then having to “PS tu” (Photoshop an image). After clocking off, they might “chang K” (sing karaoke) with friends before taking the “C wei” (centre spot) in a group photo.

These novel phrases spring from the widespread habit of adopting English abbreviations directly into daily speech, usually pronounced letter by letter.

While this is the organic result of cultural exchange, the phenomenon has sparked concern in some quarters that the Chinese language is losing its “cultural confidence”, with one expert stressing the importance of preserving the language’s “capacity for self-renewal”.

In an interview published in the overseas edition of People’s Daily last Monday, Fang Jing, a member of the Shanxi Science Writers Association, said China was dealing with thousands of letter-based terms entering daily usage.

A key concern raised by the People’s Daily interviewer was whether the growing use of English abbreviations could limit the Chinese language’s own expressive capacity.

As new technologies entered the public’s everyday lexicon, they tended to circulate in two parallel forms – an English word and a formal Chinese name – without ever settling into common, easily understood Chinese equivalents, Fang said.

In March, People’s Daily launched a campaign inviting suggestions for a new Chinese name for artificial intelligence (AI).

In the article announcing the campaign, the Communist Party mouthpiece acknowledged that while AI already has a formal Chinese name – the literal translation of “artificial intelligence” – the English abbreviation had become more widely used because it was more concise.

It argued that AI deserved a catchier Chinese moniker to sit alongside its formal name rather than just a two-letter word with “not a trace of Chinese”.

The article also questioned whether the widespread use of “AI” aligned with the country’s law which designates Chinese and standardised Chinese characters as the “national common language”.

In the interview, Fang argued that efforts to promote Chinese terms were closely linked to the “standardisation and continuity” of China’s “national common language”.

“Speeding up the creation of standard Chinese equivalents for letter-based terms and improving mechanisms for translating foreign expressions are matters that influence not only the expressive power of Chinese and convenience of communication, but also cultural confidence,” Fang said.

The concept of “cultural confidence” has official backing from the highest levels. In 2016, President Xi Jinping described the essential “Four Confidences” – confidence in the path, theory, system and culture of socialism with Chinese characteristics. The formulation was later added to the Communist Party’s constitution.

“With unwavering cultural confidence, a profound sense of mission and a spirit of relentless endeavour, we must unite our efforts to create a new culture for our times,” Xi said in 2023.

The debate over English influence long predates these buzzwords, however – it is not even exclusively Chinese. For instance, the Academie Francaise has been battling against English loanwords in French for decades, coining its own versions of new words such as “esports”.

Concerns over the impact of Western languages on Chinese were already being discussed in literary circles in the 1970s and the 1980s.

Fang told the People’s Daily that at that time, prominent poet and translator Yu Kwang-chung criticised the indiscriminate imitation of foreign sentence patterns that could weaken Chinese modes of expression. Fang added that Yu also said that carefully adopted foreign influences could enrich the language.

Such debates have continued. State media and commentators have repeatedly criticised what they see as excessive use of English borrowings, calling for efforts to protect the “purity” of Chinese and reduce reliance on loanwords.

Meanwhile, they have also taken pride in the growing global circulation of Chinese-origin words, from internet slang to terms reflecting contemporary Chinese society, as a sign of the country’s expanding cultural influence.

“Shaolin”, “yin-yang”, “ni hao” (hello), and “dama”, a slang term for a middle-aged woman, were among the Chinese words most recognised in the English-speaking world, according to a report released by the China International Publishing Group in 2018.

In recent years, newer expressions have also appeared in international media. These include “996”, which refers to working from 9am to 9pm, six days a week; “tangping”, or “lying flat”, meaning dropping out of the rat race; and “neijuan”, or “involution”, describing excessive competition.

Fang said the growing use of letter-based terms was not a sign that Chinese had become less capable of absorbing foreign words. Rather, the way foreign terms entered Chinese had undergone a “structural change”.

Earlier borrowings were often stand-alone words that would be transliterated into Chinese and then written with Chinese characters, such as “kafei” (coffee), “shafa” (sofa) and “heike” (hacker).

Many newer terms, however, arrive as abbreviations – such as AI, GDP and CPU – which are made up of letters that carry little meaning on their own and are therefore harder to translate into concise Chinese equivalents.

A 2006 report by China’s Ministry of Education and State Language Commission found that 10 English abbreviations – including NBA, GDP, IT, MP3, DVD and CEO – had already entered the 5,000 most frequently used terms in the Chinese media.

She added that convenience and speed had also helped such words spread. Short forms such as “CT” and “APP” are easier to use in speech and writing than their longer Chinese equivalents – which are seven and four characters long, respectively – thus “squeezing out space” for Chinese versions.

Meanwhile, technological concepts now spread globally within days or weeks, allowing English words to become familiar before translated terms gain wider acceptance.

The priority should be to speed up the creation of Chinese names for emerging concepts and establish widely accepted equivalents quickly, Fang said.

“Protecting the Chinese language’s capacity for self-renewal is a shared responsibility of language professionals and all users of the language,” Fang said.

“Language carries the ways of thinking, values and historical memories accumulated by a nation over generations, serving as a vital foundation for the continuation and development of its culture.”
