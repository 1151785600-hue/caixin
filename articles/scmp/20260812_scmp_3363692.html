Showdown in Indonesia’s Solo pits Widodo’s dynasty against Megawati’s machine

SCMP | FREE | 1412 words | 2026-08-12

The rivalry between two of Indonesia’s former presidents is moving onto the political ground where their alliance was first forged, with Joko Widodo’s youngest son preparing to contest the constituency held by Megawati Sukarnoputri’s daughter.

Kaesang Pangarep plans to seek a parliamentary seat in Central Java V in 2029. The constituency has been represented since 2009 by House Speaker Puan Maharani – one of the Indonesian Democratic Party of Struggle’s (PDI-P) most prominent figures and the offspring of the woman whose party carried Widodo from Solo’s city hall to the presidency.

Kaesang, who chairs the upstart Indonesian Solidarity Party (PSI), announced the plan at a party meeting in Solo on July 25. Central Java V covers Solo, Boyolali, Klaten and Sukoharjo, combining Widodo’s home base with one of PDI-P’s long-standing strongholds.

That makes the race a prospective proxy contest between two political families at very different stages of dynastic power. Analysts see it as part of Widodo’s broader challenge to Megawati and PDI-P – an effort to turn his family’s recent rise and PSI into a durable political machine capable of competing with a party rooted in the legacy of Megawati’s father, Indonesia’s first president Sukarno.

Marcus Mietzner, a professor of political and social change at the Australian National University and author of Ruling Indonesia, a recent study of Widodo’s decade in office, described Kaesang’s candidacy as “the boldest expression of this challenge so far”.

It is not necessarily a winner-takes-all duel – Indonesia’s proportional electoral system could allow both Kaesang and Puan to secure seats.

However, a strong showing by Kaesang and PSI would suggest Widodo can convert his personal popularity into lasting family influence, but a poor result could underline PDI-P’s political resilience at a time when PSI is also navigating an uncertain future outside President Prabowo Subianto’s governing coalition.

PDI-P had propelled Widodo from Solo mayor to a two-term presidency, but the partnership unravelled during the 2024 election after he backed Prabowo – who chose Widodo’s eldest son, Gibran Rakabuming Raka, as his running mate – rather than PDI-P candidate Ganjar Pranowo.

Kaesang’s challenge follows two major electoral clashes between the former allies. After Widodo helped Prabowo and Gibran secure a decisive presidential victory in 2024, including in Central Java, he openly endorsed Ahmad Luthfi, who defeated PDI-P’s candidate in the province’s gubernatorial race later that year.

PDI-P made the rupture formal that December, expelling Widodo, Gibran and Widodo’s son-in-law Bobby Nasution for defying the party line.

Widodo responded from Solo that he respected the decision, adding: “Time will tell.”

Home turf

Kristian Widya Wicaksono, an associate professor at Parahyangan Catholic University, said the overlap between Widodo’s political home base and PDI-P territory gave Kaesang’s choice both electoral logic and political symbolism.

“Choosing a constituency is not merely an administrative decision but part of an effort to send a political message to both opponents and supporters,” Kristian said.

A strong showing for Kaesang there, he added, would strengthen the argument that the Widodo family no longer needed PDI-P as its political vehicle.

Arya Fernandes, head of the politics and social change department at the Centre for Strategic and International Studies in Jakarta, said Kaesang had “two main motivations” for choosing Central Java V.

The first was that it was already one of PSI’s stronger constituencies. The second, he said, was “to challenge PDI-P politically”.

Puan topped the constituency in 2024 with 297,366 votes, while PDI-P won 774,282 votes overall, compared with PSI’s 134,249.

But PSI was gaining ground. Its vote share rose from 1.3 per cent in 2019 to 5.8 per cent in 2024, while PDI-P’s fell from 36.8 per cent to 33.2 per cent and its seat tally dropped from four to three.

Arya said PSI’s 2024 support there was already strong enough to compete for a seat. But the young party, established in 2014, has never entered the national parliament and won only about 2.8 per cent of votes nationwide in the last election, short of the 4 per cent threshold then required.

The Constitutional Court has ordered lawmakers to revise both the threshold rules and the percentage before the 2029 election, although no new figure has yet been set.

To become one of PSI’s first national lawmakers, Kaesang would not need to defeat Puan personally. PSI would need to clear whatever national parliamentary threshold applies in 2029 and win a seat in Central Java V, while Kaesang would have to rank among the party’s highest-polling candidates there.

“All of this is doable, but it doesn’t substitute for PDI-P’s long-entrenched network and ideological rootedness,” Mietzner said.

Yoes Kenawas, a political analyst at the Indonesian Survey Institute who specialises in dynastic politics, said the drama surrounding the two families could exaggerate the immediate threat to Puan.

PDI-P’s deep cultural and organisational roots in Central Java made its dominance difficult to dislodge, he said, but the rivalry still served PSI by helping it “grab national attention, pump up PSI’s popularity” and showing that Widodo’s influence was far from over.

The real objective, Yoes said, was likely to use Kaesang’s strongest natural base to secure PSI a foothold in parliament rather than defeat Puan personally.

Arya was less confident that PDI-P would escape unscathed, saying its representation in Central Java V could fall from three seats to two unless it strengthened its local organisation.

Both parties have downplayed the idea of a personal showdown between the political scions. PDI-P’s Ganjar last month described Central Java V as a natural choice for Kaesang, who is from Solo, while PSI political affairs chief Bestari Barus similarly denied that Widodo’s son was specifically challenging PDI-P.

This Week in Asia has reached out to both PSI and PDI-P for comment.

Family capital

Widodo, whose approval rating stood at about 75 per cent in a poll during his final weeks as president, has remained politically active since leaving office, meeting allies and throwing his weight behind PSI as it tries to enter parliament for the first time.

But the question remains whether his appeal can be transferred first to his children and then to a party capable of surviving without him.

Gibran’s vice-presidential victory offers an early test case. In an analysis of the 2024 election, Mietzner argued that the electoral significance of Gibran’s nomination lay less in his own popularity than in the signal it sent to Widodo’s supporters that Prabowo was the outgoing president’s preferred successor.

Mietzner said that distinction posed a risk for Kaesang.

“The case of Gibran shows that while voters still like Jokowi, they are much cooler on his sons,” he said, referring to Widodo by his popular nickname.

Mietzner said PSI was also important to Widodo because it could give him “other options” if Gibran was not chosen again as Prabowo’s running mate in 2029.

Kristian cautioned that even a Kaesang victory would not necessarily mean PSI had become a durable political force.

“The victory of a popular figure does not always lead to the strengthening of the party organisation,” he said.

For PSI to become more than a vehicle for the Widodo family, Kristian said it would need strong local networks, its own cadre base and support that could be sustained between elections and beyond candidates with famous family names.

PDI-P had vulnerabilities of its own, Kristian said, including highly centralised leadership under Megawati, which he said risked slowing leadership renewal and limiting opportunities for new leaders.

But it also has advantages that would be difficult for PSI to reproduce quickly: structures reaching down to the village level, decades of electoral experience and a relatively loyal voter base.

Arya said 2029 could be a crucial window for PSI, predicting that Widodo would campaign seriously for both the party and Kaesang.

“The 2029 election is the window PSI has left to qualify, especially while Jokowi remains a strong figure,” he said. “By 2034, Jokowi’s stature will have diminished.”

For Kristian, the longer-term measure will be whether PSI can attract support independent of the Widodo family.

“Long-term success will not be determined by the extent of the Widodo family’s influence,” he said, “but by PSI’s ability to build a strong organisation, produce quality cadres, maintain ideological consistency and gain political legitimacy through performance and public trust.”
