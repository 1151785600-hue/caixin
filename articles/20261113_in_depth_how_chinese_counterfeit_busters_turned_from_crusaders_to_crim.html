The article opens with Li Yu, a professional counterfeit crusader in China, recalling that "many places are scrambling to arrest me." On Aug. 31, police from Ma'anshan, Anhui province arrived at her door and took her and her husband away on suspicion of extortion. Authorities in multiple locations, including Ma'anshan and Xiong'an in Hebei province, had opened cases following complaints from various merchants. Li's main targets are the illegal addition of restricted substances in aphrodisiacs and weight-loss drugs, for which she seeks compensation.

Li's cases illustrate her methods: she purchased about 4,000 yuan ($599) of goods from a Ma'anshan merchant, who paid 11,000 yuan in compensation days before the court hearing, after which Li withdrew her complaint — yet police ultimately investigated the merchant for obstructing drug management. In Xiong'an, she bought 5,200 yuan worth of tirzepatide weight-loss injections (a prescription drug banned from online sales), received 13,000 yuan in compensation, and the merchant was similarly investigated. In August, the two merchants reported Li to police for extortion; she was criminally detained for 30 days, though prosecutors did not approve her formal arrest.

Over the past three decades, people like Li have deterred the manufacture and sale of counterfeit goods through specialized complaints and compensation claims, forcing merchants to improve quality and educating consumers about their rights. However, fighting fakes became lucrative, and some so-called "fake-fighters" strayed into switching products, demanding consulting fees, or extorting merchants. Regulators are now ramping up crackdowns on profit-driven fake-fighting.

The article cites wider criminal cases. In Shaoxing, Zhejiang province, police broke up a malicious compensation-seeking syndicate — led by a suspect named Guo Hao, active since October 2025 — whose members placed hair and stones into chain supermarket meals to extort compensation, arresting four suspects. In Changchun, Jilin province, police dismantled an organization headed by media figure Shen Song, arresting more than 20 suspects; Shen allegedly extorted over 100 merchants nationwide under the pretext of exposing product flaws.

Researcher Zhang Xiaohong analyzed more than 70 legal documents from extortion cases involving professional fake-fighters since the 2026 anti-organized crime campaign, spanning over 20 provinces, with detentions concentrated in August. Lawyer Liu Wenchao has represented more than 20 similar cases in three months, with clues mostly coming from market regulation departments. Liu noted that long-term profit-driven compensation claims make it difficult to claim normal consumer status, and demanding money through complaints, litigation, and exposure could constitute "soft violence."

The article traces the movement's history. The 1993 Law on the Protection of Consumer Rights and Interests established punitive damages, requiring double compensation for consumer fraud. In 1995, Wang Hai bought multiple pairs of counterfeit Sony earphones from Beijing's Longfu department store and won double compensation, becoming a nationally known figure and publicizing the law. Lawyer Du Peng called 1993-2013 the "golden age" of professional fake-fighting, targeting goods such as phones, appliances, cars, and clothing.

After 2013, the profession polarized, with top practitioners moving toward corporate, team-based operations. Food fake-fighting, yielding 10 times compensation, attracted floods of newcomers and strained regulatory resources. In May 2017, the Supreme People's Court noted that knowingly buying counterfeits was becoming commercialized, with some leveraging punitive damages for personal gain and extortion. Liu Junhai, director of Renmin University's Commercial Law Research Institute, emphasized that cracking down on malicious claims does not mean lawful professional fake-fighting is a target; the two should not be conflated.
