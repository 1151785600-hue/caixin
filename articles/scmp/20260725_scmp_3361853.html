Judge’s actions could harm public confidence in Hong Kong’s system of justice

SCMP | FREE | 1068 words | 2026-07-26

The prime duty of a judge is to independently assess court cases, resolving the issues and reaching a decision. It requires exercising judgment. The clue is in the name. But this fundamental requirement was not met by Hong Kong judge, Mr Justice Wilson Chan Ka-shun, in a case involving a dispute over a company’s shares.

Last week, the Court of Appeal took the drastic step of ordering a new trial before a different judge after finding Chan to have extensively and improperly copied legal arguments from the defence into his 2024 ruling.

Almost 95 per cent of the judgment “was incorporated from the submissions of the defendant,” said the court. Chan’s reasoning and findings were copied “almost verbatim” from the defendant’s closing arguments. Not surprisingly, he ruled in favour of the defence.

The extent and “quality” of the copying was so extreme the court took the view the judge did not appear to have applied his mind to the legal issues or fairly considered the plaintiff’s case. The appeal judges did not use the term plagiarism, but that is what it amounts to.

Chan will now be “seriously reprimanded” by the city’s two top judges, the chief justice of the Court of Final Appeal and chief judge of the High Court.

This is bad enough. But it is not the first time Chan has been censured for such conduct. He is a serial offender.

The judge, who had already been accused of copying in 2019 and 2020, was chastised by Chief Justice Andrew Cheung Kui-nung in June 2023 for replicating more than 98 per cent of the winning side’s arguments in a judgment in 2021.

The Court of Appeal, considering that case, said the judge had “taken the easy way out and abdicated his judicial responsibility”. The chief justice described his conduct as “totally unacceptable”.

But in August 2023, the Court of Appeal again found him to have plagiarised, with 221 paragraphs of 239 in another 2021 judgment copied almost verbatim.

The court said “the copying has occurred to such a degree and in such a manner that gives rise to justifiable grievance that justice has not been seen to be done.”

A third 2021 ruling by Chan, although not appealed, was the subject of a complaint to the judiciary on the same grounds. The complaint, considered by a panel of judges, was not upheld last year because the judge was found to have exercised independent analysis.

But the panel said a substantial amount of copying had taken place and that the judge “should avoid similar practice in the future”.

Chief Justice Cheung, responding to the complaint, said Chan should be “firmly reminded to desist from the practice of substantial copying”.

A second Court of Appeal ruling this month found Chan’s copying, in another of his decisions, delivered in May 2023, to have been to a “highly undesirable” extent, although he exercised his own judgment, authoring four important paragraphs.

It is common practice for judges

to include the submissions of lawyers in their judgments. This is a natural step when setting out the reasons for a decision. But there are limits. They

are expected to demonstrate they

have used their own ideas and words

in explaining how they decided the case.

Anyone reading a ruling should be able to see that the judge has resolved the case independently and impartially. Public confidence in Hong Kong’s system of justice depends on it.

The judiciary said last week that Chan will be requested to undertake “targeted training”. Last year, the panel of judges considering the complaint case recommended “suitable support and guidance on judgment writing should be given to the judge”. A promise to step up such training for judges was also made when Chan was reprimanded in 2023.

But Chan has been a judge for 13 years. He was even approved by the chief executive to sit on national security cases. The writing of judgments is a fundamental part of a judge’s work. It should not be necessary for an experienced judge to be taught how to do it.

The judiciary appears to have almost given up on Chan’s ability to write up his rulings. It told the South China Morning Post that the judge’s duties were adjusted last year.

He is now primarily assigned to criminal cases that do not require written rulings. This step was taken “so as to avoid occurrences of excessive judicial copying in his judgments”. This is an extraordinary state of affairs.

In most other fields, a professional found to have repeatedly plagiarised would be sacked. But judges enjoy security of tenure. This is an important safeguard to ensure they can decide cases freely without worrying that a controversial decision will cost them their job.

Judges can, under the Basic Law, only be removed for “misbehaviour” or for inability to discharge their duties. The decision would need to be made by the chief executive on the recommendation of a special tribunal comprising three judges appointed by the chief justice. It is envisaged that the misbehaviour concerned must be very serious. No judge has been dismissed or a panel established since Hong Kong’s return to China in 1997.

We have not heard from the judge. No explanation or mitigation, such as ill health or pressure of work, has been provided. Chan has received repeated warnings. He should reflect on his conduct.

The ordering of a retrial, under any circumstances, is best avoided. As the Court of Appeal said last week, it is a last resort. Time, money and precious judicial resources are wasted. It is unacceptable that a new trial has to be ordered because of improper copying by a judge.

Other parts of the world have also faced the issue of judicial copying, including Britain, Australia and Singapore. The leading legal authority is from Canada in 2013. Hong Kong’s Court of Final Appeal expressed concern about the practice as far back as 2005.

The question of whether judges have formed their own independent judgments when deciding cases is likely to be further complicated by the emergence of artificial intelligence (AI). The Hong Kong judiciary issued guidelines on the use of AI in 2024.

Fighting a case in court is stressful, expensive and time-consuming. Litigants are entitled to expect that judges will use their judgment rather than, in the words of the Court of Appeal, deliver rulings that appear to lack an “active, independent and impartial judicial mind”.
