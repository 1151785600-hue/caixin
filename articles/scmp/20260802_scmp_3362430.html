From Mao to now: why China’s Communist Party is intent on control over the military

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2187 words | 2026-08-02

Having marked its 99th anniversary on August 1, the People’s Liberation Army is now just one year away from its centenary goals, with its evolution highlighting the past, present and future of China’s military strategy. In the third part of a series, Yuanyue Dang and William Zheng examine how Beijing leans on Mao-era doctrines and anti-corruption drives to guarantee absolute party control over the armed forces.

In December 1929, then Communist revolutionary Mao Zedong led a pivotal meeting in Gutian, Fujian province.

The Communist Party’s armed wing at the time was a coalition of rebel forces fighting the ruling Nationalists or Kuomintang (KMT). The “Gutian conference” defined the Chinese Workers’ and Peasants’ Red Army as “an armed body for carrying out the political tasks of the revolution” and set up party organisations throughout the military.

The next two decades witnessed some of the defining moments leading up to the Communist victory of 1949, including the Long March, the consolidation of Mao’s leadership and the renaming of the armed forces as the People’s Liberation Army (PLA).

In 1938, as the party faced a turning point following the departure of co-founder and Mao’s revolutionary rival Zhang Guotao, Mao wrote: “Our principle is that the party commands the gun, and the gun must never be allowed to command the party.”

Nearly a century on, those principles still serve as the political bedrock of the party’s control over the military.

In 2014, President Xi Jinping convened a military political work conference in Gutian, in a clear tribute to Mao’s doctrines from more than 80 years before.

Urging the PLA to reflect on discipline and uphold its revolutionary tradition, Xi said: “Upholding the party’s absolute leadership over the military is the soul of building a strong army.”

These ideological doctrines drove the massive PLA overhaul launched the following year. Paired with the military’s most aggressive anti-corruption campaign in decades, the reforms swept through every level – from service branches and theatre commands to equipment procurement and top-level appointments.

They were also central to Beijing’s messaging during the latest anti-corruption drive targeting the Central Military Commission, the PLA’s top command body led by Xi. Five of the seven-member CMC, including former vice-chairmen Zhang Youxia and He Weidong, have been removed from office.

Only Xi as chairman and Zhang Shengmin, a vice-chairman who previously oversaw anti-corruption work in the military, now remain on the body whose current term began in 2022.

Zhang Youxia was the CMC’s first-ranked vice-chairman, China’s most senior military officer after Xi.

An editorial in the official PLA Daily shortly after Zhang’s downfall said he had “seriously trampled on and undermined the CMC chairman responsibility system” as well as “seriously fuelled political and corruption problems that affect the party’s absolute leadership over the military and endanger the foundations of the party’s rule”.

As the PLA gears up to mark its centenary next August, analysts say Beijing’s relentless focus on “the party’s absolute leadership over the military” highlights concerns that extend well beyond corruption.

Control of the military is foundational to the party’s power, they note. This mindset – shaped by the historical lessons from the collapse of the KMT in mainland China and later that of the Soviet Union, as well as the political turmoil of the Mao era – helps to explain Beijing’s resolute rejection of what it calls the “nationalisation of the military”.

Zhang Youxia was placed under investigation in January, alongside Liu Zhenli, fellow CMC member and chief of staff of its Joint Staff Department.

This came three months after CMC vice-chairman He Weidong and Miao Hua, former head of the commission’s Political Work Department, were expelled from the party and the military over allegations of corruption and serious discipline violations.

In October 2023, former defence minister Li Shangfu was removed from office and expelled from the party eight months later, also over corruption.

His predecessor, Wei Fenghe, was similarly investigated, expelled from the party and stripped of his rank in 2024. Both men received suspended death sentences in May.

Joseph Torigian, a professor at American University’s school of international service and a senior fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations, said Xi’s view was that corruption could affect the military’s political choices at critical moments.

“He believes that the military will only serve the party during moments of political crisis at home and in wars abroad if it has the right world view,” Torigian said.

“When a military leader is corrupt, that means materialism and consumerism have eroded the conviction and idealism Xi believes are necessary for the PLA to make the right decision.”

Lessons from the KMT

The Communist Party has long viewed discipline and organisational capacity as central to its 1949 victory over the KMT in the civil war. Peking University historian Wang Qisheng has similarly traced the KMT’s defeat in part to weak organisational structure.

In his 2003 book, Party Members, Party Power and Party Struggles: The Organisational Form of the Kuomintang, 1924–1949, Wang wrote that the KMT borrowed organisational techniques from the Soviet model in the 1920s, but never replicated its substance.

Unlike the Soviet system – where party authority was supreme over the state and military at every level – local KMT branches remained weak and marginal, lacking the authority to oversee or discipline officials. After the civil war broke out in 1927, real power shifted decisively towards the military, leaving KMT leader Chiang Kai-shek increasingly dependent on forces under his personal control, according to Wang’s book.

Elizabeth Perry, Henry Rosovsky Professor of Government at Harvard University, said that the Communist Party “is proud of its revolutionary heritage, in which the Red Army had a reputation for not stealing from the peasants”.

“It sometimes broke those rules, but for the most part the Red Army was a well-disciplined army, whose connection to the peasantry helped the Chinese Communist Party win the revolution,” she added.

“The difference in levels of corruption between the Kuomintang and the CCP, and between their militaries, is often cited as an important reason for the victory of the Chinese Communist Party.”

Unlike the KMT, the Communist Party brought the PLA under direct party control.

Party organisations exist at every level of the PLA. Political commissars and military commanders share leadership responsibilities, while the appointment, education and disciplinary supervision of officers are also handled by party bodies within the military.

A Beijing-based political scientist who requested anonymity because of the sensitivity of the matter, said the arrangement was a result of the military’s somewhat unique status in China.

“After all, the PLA is largely a system on its own,” the academic said.

As an example, the academic noted that the party’s organisation department did not handle military personnel – a responsibility that fell to the CMC’s political work department.

The party’s top anti-corruption body, the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection, does not directly investigate corruption in the military either. Such cases are handled by the CMC’s own discipline inspection commission.

Within this relatively closed system, the flow of information, personnel and resources becomes central to party control over the military.

“So the party’s rule over the military basically hinges on the party’s general secretary being the sole and final decision-maker on the CMC,” the political scientist said.

“That requires detailed and strict workflow design in the CMC’s daily work to ensure the chairman holds the ultimate decision-making power on key things like personnel, budget, major military actions and technology developments.”

The CMC “chairman responsibility system” sits at the top of this institutional structure.

China’s constitution – adopted in 1982 – stipulates that the state CMC operates under a chairman responsibility system. Xi further strengthened the system after taking the helm in 2012.

Following the 2014 Gutian meeting – which came in the wake of a corruption scandal involving a former top general – the CMC set up three mechanisms to ensure that critical issues and information were reported directly to the chairman.

The mechanisms covered requests and reports, supervision and inspection, and information services.

Then came sweeping military reforms. The overhaul launched in 2015 dismantled the four general departments in favour of multiple functional departments directly subordinate to the CMC.

A new command structure was created, under which the CMC retains overall authority, the theatre commands oversee joint operations, and individual services focus on force development.

According to the anonymous analyst, the reforms elevated joint operations while curbing the agency of the former general departments and individual services over personnel, equipment, and resource allocation.

In 2017, the CMC chairman responsibility system was written into the party constitution. Follow-up regulations mandated that the military treat the system’s roll-out as its highest political requirement and core disciplinary standard.

According to Beijing, the CMC chairman oversees its overall work and makes final decisions on major military issues. The vice-chairmen and other members assist him and are responsible for implementation.

But the military’s daily operations still require extensive delegation.

“The most critical part of the system is where you draw the line,” the Beijing-based political scientist said.

“The CMC chairman’s office cannot make all the decisions … The CMC must have clear guidelines on what are classified as ‘major decisions’ to be decided at the top and what reports shall be sent to the chairman’s office.

“That is a very delicate balance. It seems the trend now is to over-report rather than under-report.”

The present arrangement gives CMC vice-chairmen, commissioners and the leaders of the services and other forces significant intermediary authority.

They can influence what information is reported, how the chairman’s orders are interpreted and which officers are placed on promotion lists. Personnel networks can also lead subordinate officers to direct their loyalty first towards the superiors who promoted them, according to the anonymous observer.

At the heart of history

Time and again, the PLA has proven to be a decisive factor at the party’s most critical historical turning points.

A stark instance was in 1971, when Mao’s deputy and designated successor Lin Biao died in a plane crash while fleeing China after an alleged coup attempt – his downfall triggering investigations into a slew of generals seen as close to him.

The arrest of the Gang of Four in 1976 and the martial law imposed to clamp down on the Tiananmen protests in 1989, meanwhile, are regarded by the party as examples of the military obeying the central leadership during political crises.

Torigian at American University said: “Xi believes that the party has only survived for so long because at moments of crisis, like the split with Zhang Guotao, the fall of the Gang of Four, and the Tiananmen crackdown, the PLA made the ‘right’ choice.”

China’s party-army system is sometimes compared with North Korea’s state policy of songun, or “military-first politics”.

After then-leader Kim Jong-il implemented songun in the 1990s, the Korean People’s Army (KPA) gained greater political status and priority in resource allocation, while becoming extensively involved in infrastructure, agriculture, trade and other economic activities.

Jongseok Woo, an associate professor of political science at the University of South Florida, argued in a 2014 paper that Kim adopted songun as a strategy for regime survival, shifting “the locus of political power” from the party to the military.

But Woo also wrote that Kim developed mechanisms to prevent the armed forces from dominating politics.

In a 2018 paper, Woo drew a distinction between the military’s elevated institutional status and its independent political influence. “The military has not been a politically influential organ from the state-building to the current Kim Jong-un era,” he wrote.

China’s official description of its own party-army system is more direct.

A document issued by the CMC in 2017 said: “The system of ultimate responsibility resting with the chairman of the Central Military Commission is a major system stipulated in the constitution and the party constitution, and is the fundamental system and fundamental form of realising the party’s absolute leadership over the military.”

Yet both systems reject a professional military that remains neutral during a political crisis.

In China, this rejected model is usually described as the “nationalisation of the military”. Beijing believes that such a model could undermine party rule.

The dissolution of the Soviet Union further reinforced this wariness.

In Beijing’s view, the Soviet Communist Party’s loosening grip on its armed forces led directly to the military’s failure to defend the party during the 1991 political crisis.

“Xi believes that the Soviet Union collapsed because the party lost control of the military,” Torigian said.

In September 2025, a PLA Daily commentary said the Soviet military had at the time “become estranged from the party and disobeyed its commands, and even colluded with the opposition”.

For Beijing, the collapse of the Soviet Union offers a clear lesson: the PLA must always uphold the principles established by strong leaders Mao and Xi at Gutian – the party must always lead the military.
