JW Marriott Hotel Hong Kong redefines luxury dining with zero-waste culinary innovation

SCMP | FREE | 1393 words | 2026-10-29

For Tony Wong Chi-chuen, the executive chef of JW Marriott Hotel Hong Kong, waste is never the conclusion of an ingredient’s story. Lemon ends can be used to perfume seafood, shrimp shells to deepen the nuance of a broth and fishbones to build a base for pasta dough. In Wong’s kitchen, food waste is simply ingredients waiting for a second life.

“Discarding something is easy,” says Wong. “[Reusing] is not about saving cost; saving is about reinvesting in more quality ingredients.”

In person, he doesn’t mince his words. As the conversation turns to his craft, Wong – who has 38 years of experience in the industry – becomes animated, revealing a passion honed over decades, rooted in a lesson learned long before he entered a professional kitchen.

His parents used to run a dai pai dong-style stall behind the Yau Ma Tei Police Station, peddling steaming bowls of fish-ball noodles, before it closed more than 40 years ago. From the age of 12, Wong would help out after school. He watched his parents work hard, stretch ingredients and make full use of what they had.

He recalls that “they didn’t throw anything away”: fishbones were simmered into broth for the noodles – an ingrained reflex that later inspired his fishbone fettuccine at JW Marriott Hotel Hong Kong, showing how every ingredient could be made to work harder. Wong came to understand that saving on food waste only mattered if it was turned into something better eventually.

“When I was 18, I realised being thrifty is not enough,” he says. “You have to use what you save and make it into something good for customers.” His philosophy – waste not, but elevate – became the bedrock of his kitchen. It is an exercise not in austerity, but in wringing maximum quality and flavour out of scraps.

Wong joined JW Marriott Hotel Hong Kong in 1990, a year after its opening. He started as a senior cook at the former Marriott Cafe, now JW Cafe, and steadily moved up the ranks: sous chef in 2003, executive sous chef in 2009 and, finally, executive chef in 2018.

As a young cook, he noticed how quickly bins filled up. Celery ends, carrot peels, onion trimmings and other offcuts were tossed aside because only the neatest parts were used. When he became sous chef, he began making changes: a bucket near a chopping board was added to hold bones, end cuts and trimmings to be reused.

Now, the system has become part of kitchen culture. Watermelon rind is blended into a light breakfast drink, valued in traditional Chinese medicine for its refreshing sweetness and cooling effects; leftover pieces from Peking ducks are shredded into a braised rice-vermicelli dish.

What it has done for food-waste numbers is significant – the hotel repurposes around 8.2 tonnes of watermelon rind a year, along with 900kg of lemon peel, more than 3.6 tonnes of fishbones and 3.65 tonnes of shrimp heads and shells, says Wong. At its core, however, his philosophy remains simple: an ingredient should have as many “lives” as possible before it leaves the kitchen.

Across JW Marriott Hotel Hong Kong’s eight dining venues and multiple event spaces, Wong’s strategy goes beyond creating isolated “green” dishes – it is about building a circular kitchen system. His approach aligns with Marriott’s Serve 360 sustainability and social-impact platform, which is guided by the belief of “doing good in every direction”.

Furthermore, the culinary initiatives sit within the property’s broader push towards responsible luxury – a commitment that has won prestigious recognition, recently from the US Green Building Council for sustainable building operations and maintenance, and the International WELL Building Institute for health and wellness, ensuring that guest well-being and environmental stewardship are both fully considered.

In a five-star hotel, sustainability has to meet the expectations of luxury dining. Wong does not see a contradiction. He believes that responsible cooking should make food better, not lesser, and the clearest expression of this thinking is Fish Bar’s lobster fishbone fettuccine, one of the restaurant’s signature dishes. Instead of treating grouper bones as waste, Wong’s team dries and pulverises them into a fine powder, then blends the meal with gluten-free flour to make pasta. No mere sustainability gimmick, the bones add calcium and deepen the pasta’s umami, turning scrap into part of the dish’s flavour structure.

If anything, Wong says, zero-waste practices allow him to be more generous. Financial savings from careful portioning and cross-kitchen reuse are channelled back into premium buffet offerings, such as abalone, fish maw, sea cucumber, lobster and crab.

High-quality offcuts receive similar care: Australian Wagyu trimmings are crafted into burger patties, while at the hotel’s Michelin-star Man Ho Chinese Restaurant, prime cuts of Iberico pork are reserved for char siu and flavourful remnants are minced for xiaolongbao fillings at The Lounge.

For banquets and functions, Wong’s team makes enough food for no more than 10 per cent above the expected number of guests. At the buffet, dishes are prepared in smaller, more frequently replenished batches, ensuring that food does not sit for long periods. It is a more labour-intensive practice, but one that benefits the environment and creates a better experience for diners. “Other places refill food maybe twice; we do it 20 times,” says Wong.

Luxury diners used to fixate on origin – lobsters from France, tomatoes from Italy, sea urchin from Japan – but these priorities have shifted. “I think guests mainly care about the taste, quality and freshness of the ingredients – these are the three things they focus on now and they care less about where they come from,” says Wong.

For him, this change has widened the meaning of luxury. Premium dining is no longer defined only by provenance or price, but by how thoughtfully an ingredient is sourced, handled and cooked. If a dish tastes good and aligns with sustainable values, guests will often see it as something better.

JW Marriott Hotel Hong Kong prioritises local sourcing where possible, which helps reduce transport time, spoilage and carbon emissions. Suppliers are screened for certifications – including from organisations such as the Marine Stewardship Council (MSC) and Aquaculture Stewardship Council (ASC) – and traceability. Purchasing decisions made by the procurement team are guided in part by monthly WWF recommendations of vetted seafood suppliers and sources, says Wong. Endangered species, such as bluefin tuna and shark, are off the menu, replaced by alternatives like yellowtail and vegetarian shark’s fin – a plant-based ingredient that replicates the texture of the original.

At Fish Bar, renovated in 2024, sustainability is built into the space as well as the dining programme. An open design allows for natural ventilation, reducing reliance on air conditioning. Uniforms were designed in partnership with the Caritas Bianchi College of Careers and incorporate coffee-ground materials. Bottled water has been replaced with a filtered system.

Meanwhile, JW Garden grows herbs such as rosemary, basil and perilla, which are used in drinks, dishes, amenities and even canned beverages that extend the farm-to-table idea into a “farm-to-home” experience. Edible surplus, such as croissants and muffins, is donated to organisations serving people in need, while food waste that cannot be reused is sent to recycling partners for renewable energy or composting. JW Marriott Hotel Hong Kong also works with The Nature Conservancy, turning discarded oyster shells into reef substrate for local marine restoration.

From Wong’s point of view, these hotel-wide initiatives connect back to the same principle: resources should circulate, not dissipate. His message for the next generation is clear: cooking well is no longer enough, because a chef must also think about impact.

His 180-strong team, many of whom have worked with him for decades, now closely follow his doctrine.

It has created a virtuous cycle: the kitchen grows increasingly efficient as reuse practices are streamlined; diners enjoy fresher, higher-quality food; staff develop discipline and pride – all while protecting the environment. Wong shares of his team that “they feel this is a good habit; they feel like they can be good people”.

Some might label the rethinking of food waste as accumulating good karma, but in hospitality terms, it’s simply good business.
