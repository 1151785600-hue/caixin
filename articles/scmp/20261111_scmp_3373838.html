Lithuanian president confirms talks on renaming Taiwan office in diplomatic U-turn

SCMP | FREE | 1019 words | 2026-11-11

Lithuania is in talks with Taiwan over changing the name of a controversial representative office in Vilnius, with its president saying the government is “not obligated” to follow policies that predate it.

Gitanas Nauseda, speaking to the state broadcaster LRT on Monday night, confirmed that the Baltic state’s foreign ministry had sent a letter to Taiwan’s representatives asking to recalibrate the relationship.

“Without a doubt, we are coordinating and, without a doubt, the new ruling majority is not obligated to somehow continue the policy of the former ruling majority,” Nauseda said, adding that “changing the name of the Taiwan mission is … only part of the whole picture and far from the most significant”.

The leader was talking following local media reports that the foreign ministry had asked Taipei to change the name before Beijing’s National Day last month.

The twist comes as Vilnius seeks to restore diplomatic ties with Beijing, which have been frozen since the opening of the office in 2021.

Reports in Delfi, a Lithuanian news portal, on Monday said a deputy foreign minister delivered a letter to Taipei’s representatives – including the head of the “Taiwanese Representative Office” – on September 24, asking them to change the office’s name by October 1.

According to the report, Lithuania offered to send a vice-ministerial delegation to Taipei in exchange and sign agreements on investment and taxation.

Nauseda downplayed the significance of the saga but insisted that it should have been done publicly rather than through backchannels.

“I honestly don’t understand why it needs to be made into a special secret, because all of this could have been publicly known from the very beginning,” the president said.

Beijing delegation heading to Lithuania in sign of thaw

Meanwhile, a delegation of diplomats from Beijing is due to land in Vilnius on Wednesday for two days of meetings, in a further sign of a thaw.

Meetings are expected with the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, but not at ministerial level, the South China Morning Post understands.

Nauseda – who said nobody from his office would meet the visiting party – said the visit will help determine the trajectory of the normalisation process.

“We will probably have fairly clear conversations about what the expectations of both sides are, and I very much hope that we will be able to gradually restore normal diplomatic cooperation.”

Earlier on Monday, Foreign Minister Kestutis Budrys confirmed talks with Taipei were ongoing even if he said the information had been “inaccurately presented”.

“I confirm that we have spoken with the Taiwanese side about various aspects of our relations, maintaining the foundation – which is constructive cooperation and utilising the full potential of our capabilities,” Budrys was quoted as saying in LNK Zinių, another Lithuanian media outlet.

“We talked about various aspects,” he added, when asked they had requested Taipei to change the name of the mission.

The Lithuanian foreign ministry did not immediately respond to a request for comment, nor did China’s.

Representatives of Taipei did not respond to questions about whether the office name had been or would be changed, but told local news portal 15min that it did not intend to change the name.

Beijing sees Taiwan as part of China and has not ruled out the use of force to reunify it. Most countries, including Lithuania and the rest of the European Union, do not recognise the self-ruled island as an independent state.

However, the EU states that it wishes to expand business and cultural ties with Taipei, short of diplomatic recognition.

China-EU relations were hit by opening of Vilnius’ Taiwan office

While Taiwan retains a diplomatic presence in other European countries, Lithuania is the only place to feature “Taiwanese” in the name. In other capitals, it bears the name Taipei.

After the office was opened in 2021, China removed Lithuania from its customs system, meaning Lithuanian exporters could not ship goods to the country.

Beijing claimed Vilnius had violated the EU’s one-China policy and expelled its ambassador in 2021, while also withdrawing its own envoy from Lithuania.

It also deepened tensions between the EU and China, which worsened further because of Beijing’s close relationship with Russia.

The EU has said that Lithuania had not breached its one-China policy and launched a World Trade Organization case against the trade embargo, although that case was subsequently dropped.

Since the Social Democrat-led Lithuanian government took office in 2024, it has tried to smooth relations with China.

Successive centre-left prime ministers have also condemned the previous centre-right leadership’s decision to open the office, claiming that Vilnius got little in return.

EU sources said, however, that whenever delegations from Lithuania had gone to Beijing in an effort to normalise ties, they had been told they would have to accept all of China’s terms, including changing the name of the office.

In a post on its Facebook page last Friday, the “Taiwanese Representative Office” in Vilnius wrote: “We believe that public discussions concerning Taiwan-Lithuania relations should be grounded in verifiable facts rather than unsubstantiated assumptions or speculation.”

Speaking earlier on Monday, Nauseda again tried to play down the significance of the stories, even if he did not deny their substance.

“I repeat once again, we want to restore normal relations [with Beijing] and maintain normal relations with Taiwan,” Nauseda said, according to Lithuanian National Radio and Television (LRT).

“We’ll see, we’ll wait, we’ll see,” he replied when asked whether Vilnius wanted to see the name changed.

“Now we have a meeting with Chinese diplomats and then we will see, we will move forward,” he said, adding that he wished to restore relations with Beijing to the level of “diplomatic hygiene”.

“The issue of the Taiwanese mission, yes, is a linguistic issue; it was expected that it would provide large political dividends for Lithuania. There was talk of big things that would start in 2021,” LRT quoted Nauseda as saying.

“This did not happen, so one of our goals is not only to talk about the names of the mission, but also about the reasons why this did not happen”
