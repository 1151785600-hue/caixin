Chinese luxury brands rise from challengers to market shapers as global houses lose lustre

SCMP | FREE | 1419 words | 2026-08-09

After months of declining gold prices, Li Yue recently spent nearly 28,000 yuan (US$4,148) on a 10-gram (0.35-ounce) pure gold necklace – more than double the prevailing market rate for the metal.

The piece in question: a gourd-shaped pendant from Laopu Gold, a Chinese heritage jewellery brand that has captured significant attention in recent years.

But for Li, a software engineer in Shanghai, the purchase had little to do with bullion value. She cared more about the necklace’s design – the gourd is a traditional symbol of fortune and prosperity – and the care with which it was crafted.

“No one asks how much the fabric of their Louis Vuitton bag costs, right?” she said.

Li is precisely the kind of customer Laopu Gold is courting. Having cultivated a sophisticated customer base through its choice of retail locations and emphasis on craftsmanship and aesthetic expression, the brand has earned a place in the luxury echelon, becoming one of the bestselling labels in China’s high-end malls – even as overall luxury spending continued to decline, analysts said.

Like Laopu, a growing number of local brands are now appealing to premium consumers. As international luxury brands such as Cartier, Tiffany & Co, and Gucci close their doors on Huaihai Middle Road – one of Shanghai’s premier shopping thoroughfares – a wave of home-grown names has taken their place, from handbag label Songmont and shoe brand Pane to perfume house To Summer.

If top-tier Western luxury labels were status symbols that newly affluent Chinese consumers clamoured for in past decades, today’s shoppers were more discerning and rational – and they were increasingly turning to domestic brands that offered a “high-value sense of luxury”, according to Zhou Ting, a veteran researcher with decades of experience in the luxury goods industry.

The next decade will be [Chinese luxury brands’] golden era, with the entire segment growing into a market worth tens of trillions of yuan

Zhou Ting, Yaok Institute

Compared with global luxury houses, domestic brands had a superior grasp of Chinese consumers’ aesthetic sensibilities and contextual needs while offering a pricing framework that provided more compelling value, said Zhou, founder of the Shanghai-based Yaok Institute, which provides research and consulting services related to high-end goods.

“After the first wave of global expansion by European and American luxury houses, China is now emerging as the world’s second core incubator for luxury brands,” she said.

By 2035, Chinese luxury brands would match the China market share of their international counterparts in terms of sales, Zhou said.

The success of brands such as Laopu Gold and collectible toys creator Pop Mart extends beyond their financial performance. They had shown the viability of premium branding for Chinese labels, and provided a replicable framework for emerging domestic brands, she said.

Though no longer drawing the massive queues it saw during the recent surge in gold prices, Laopu Gold, with dozens of stores across China and one store in Singapore, “outperforms every other luxury brand in terms of sales revenue”, a source close to the firm told the South China Morning Post.

The company had 610,000 registered members as of the end of last year – more than 80 per cent of whom overlapped with those of major luxury brands such as Louis Vuitton and Cartier – and was now in talks with shopping malls in Japan and South Korea to open new stores, said the source, who declined to be named as they were not authorised to talk to the media.

Citing Frost & Sullivan data, the Hong Kong-listed company said in its 2025 financial report that it ranked second by revenue among global luxury brands in mainland China, and first in sales per store and per square metre within a single mall. Laopu issued a positive profit forecast for the first half of this year last week, estimating total revenue of about 20 billion yuan (US$2.96 billion), up about 60 to 65 per cent year on year.

“One major reason behind the brand’s rise is that it has created entirely new demand; before this, there was no gold-focused brand among top-tier luxury jewellery houses,” the source said.

“For established luxury players, gold is not a test of their technical mastery, but rather of cultural resonance and aesthetic sensibility.”

Global luxury leaders remain in the depths of a protracted downturn. Last week, Louis Vuitton parent LVMH reported a 3 per cent year-on-year decline in total revenue and a 4 per cent fall in profits for the first half of the year. The first-half results for Kering Group – manager of luxury brands Gucci, Balenciaga and Bottega Veneta, among others – showed revenue down 3 per cent and net income plunging 60 per cent from a year earlier.

China’s total retail sales of consumer goods grew just 1.3 per cent year on year in the first half of 2026, with the gold, silver and jewellery category contracting by 3.4 per cent in June alone, data from the National Bureau of Statistics showed.

Meanwhile, China’s high-end consumption market stood at 1.56 trillion yuan in 2025 – a 5 per cent decline from the previous year, according to a Hurun Research Institute report released in January.

More than half of the 470 high-net-worth Chinese consumers surveyed by Hurun said they planned to cut expenses on daily luxury goods this year, underscoring a widespread move towards more value-conscious consumption.

While the slowdown of the overall economy has played a role, this came after a gradual shift in consumer mentality – from obsession with logos and brand heritage to greater emphasis on product quality, craftsmanship, innovation and the overall brand experience, said Zhou, the Shanghai researcher.

It is within this evolving environment that Icicle, a premier Chinese fashion brand using natural materials with a focus on comfort and durability, has stood out.

When it first emerged in the late 1990s, luxury consumption in China was closely tied to status and external recognition. But today, consumers were increasingly looking for products that reflected their personal taste, lifestyle and values, which had contributed to the brand’s double-digit compound annual growth in China in recent years, it said in a statement to the SCMP.

“There has been a broader shift away from conspicuous consumption towards what might be called ‘quiet luxury’ – quality that speaks for itself, rather than through logos or status signalling,” it said, adding that this played directly to the brand’s strengths.

With more than 200 stores globally, including five in Europe, the brand is pushing for further international expansion through a partnership with Kering, which in April acquired a minority stake in ICCF, the brand’s owner.

As international luxury houses become demystified for mainstream consumers and stripped of social distinction for high-net-worth shoppers, local high-end brands are now capturing market share and redefining luxury.

Despite a strong recovery in overseas tourism, 65 per cent of Chinese luxury spending occurred within mainland China last year, while domestic brands captured more share by appealing to cultural connections and local preferences, according to a Bain & Co report in January.

“Chinese luxury brands have evolved from challengers to market shapers,” it said. “They are transforming the China luxury landscape with their distinctive fusion of global appeal and authentic Chinese identity and redefining what luxury means for modern Chinese consumers.”

Buying local luxury is not a compromise – it’s a statement of cultural identity and pride in Chinese heritage

Li Yue, Shanghai

And this was by no means a short-term phenomenon as consumers tightened their belts amid the current economic downturn, but a “long-term, irreversible shift” supported by multiple factors, Zhou said.

Those factors included a growing cultural affinity for home-grown brands, the maturation of China’s domestic supply chain, and the democratisation of information, she added.

“Chinese luxury brands are at the starting point of rapid growth. The next decade will be their golden era, with the entire segment growing into a market worth tens of trillions of yuan,” Zhou said.

On quality and service, Chinese brands were on par with – or even ahead of – top-tier luxury houses, thanks to China’s unrivalled supply chain and manufacturing expertise, she said. The real gap lay in brand legacy and global reputation, which required years of history and international exposure to cultivate.

Li, the Laopu Gold customer, said she would continue chasing traditional luxury brands while at the same time embracing new ones from local companies.

“To me, buying local luxury is not a compromise – it’s a statement of cultural identity and pride in Chinese heritage,” she said.
