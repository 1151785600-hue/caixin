EU-China truce under strain as both sides brace for potential trade war

SCMP | FREE | 1167 words | 2026-11-06

Weeks after the European Union and China struck a deal aimed at averting a looming trade war, the two sides remain at loggerheads over what is wrong with their relationship, how to fix it and who is to blame.

At an event in Brussels this week, threats were exchanged and differences rose to the surface. With the ink barely dry on their fragile trade peace, it was clear that both were still preparing for a trade war.

“If the EU insists on adopting restrictive and discriminatory measures against China, China will have to take countermeasures to firmly safeguard its interests,” Beijing’s ambassador to the EU, Cai Run, warned on Thursday.

Speaking at the same event – which was sponsored by China’s mission to the EU – Stefano Grassi, a senior official who helps coordinate the European Commission’s China policy, returned criticism of his own.

“Where we are now with China … [is] a unidirectional, unsustainable structural imbalance, and this can be corrected in a managed diplomatic way or, unfortunately, [it] will lead to a big turmoil and a lot of protectionism, a lot of closure of markets that will hurt both buyers and sellers,” Grassi said.

A day earlier, EU trade chief Maros Sefcovic outlined plans to unveil a package of tools on December 9 that would be, in part, aimed at helping businesses “in case of a possible tit-for-tat retaliatory situation”.

He said the EU sought to ensure “we could react much, much faster, even by limiting exports into the EU, especially from countries which are treating us unfairly”.

A widely circulated WeChat post from prominent Chinese trade expert Cui Fan this week said Beijing could launch anti-discrimination and industrial and supply-chain security investigations, export controls and reciprocal import restrictions if the EU made good on promises to develop a “kill switch” to shut off the market to Chinese firms.

“China is closely monitoring and studying the EU’s policy developments and has prepared a range of targeted countermeasures,” Cui wrote.

Work will continue in the hope of adding to the deal in which China will accept an EU quota of 350,000 per year on its hybrid car exports without retaliating.

The EU’s top trade enforcement official, Denis Redonnet, led a group of medical device manufacturers on a “matchmaking” trip to Shanghai on Thursday. The EU has restricted China’s market access in the sector due to a lack of reciprocation.

“China and the EU have stressed that the second meeting was a new starting point rather than an endpoint. The negotiations and consultations ahead remain demanding,” envoy Cai said on Thursday.

This jarred, however, with what a delegation of European Parliament trade lawmakers took away from a trip to China last week.

“We see this as a first step to rebalance the trade imbalances, and here I think China pushed back a bit … that is an important message for us. They, as I read them, didn’t see this as a first step of many,” said centre-right member Jorgen Warborn, who was among those to meet Chinese Commerce Minister Wang Wentao for two hours.

Socialist member Kathleen van Brempt, who was also on the trip, said Wang had indicated that “the deal on the hybrid cars is not to be seen as a model to proceed”.

“They want to start negotiations on a full-fledged investment or free trade agreement, but that is a trap,” she told the parliament’s trade committee on Wednesday.

“We’re open to that, but they are negotiations on a huge deal that will take years … our industries are under siege now.”

While some MEPs praised the positivity of talks, van Brempt suggested there were some clashes.

“It was crystal clear they do not accept our narrative. They don’t have to, but they do not accept our figures on overcapacity, on overproduction. They mocked a little bit when we used the figures,” the Belgian politician said, adding that Beijing had a long list of EU laws about which they complained.

Addressing that committee later in the day, Sefcovic hit out at China’s trade surplus over the EU, saying “we’ve been making it crystal clear that our deficit with China is not sustainable”.

“Because of its growth and pace and dynamics, it went well beyond trade optics, and therefore it becomes political.”

At Thursday’s China-backed event, commission official Grassi said the bloc’s fears over China’s role in global imbalances could be attributed to latent trauma from the euro-zone crisis, which he said was caused by Europe’s internal imbalances.

Grassi said the lesson learned was that “when you are in a situation of macroeconomic imbalances in a highly integrated economy … the risks are for all the participants in the system”. He added that imbalances “lead to traumatic adjustment”.

Several Chinese speakers, however, rejected the diagnosis and downplayed the deficit talk, suggesting that despite the surprise trade deal, they were far from a mutual diagnosis of their relationship problems.

“I think energy prices, high tariffs from Washington to Brussels, or every capital city in Europe, that’s a major reason. So the trade deficit with China is just consequences. It’s not a reason. It’s not the cause,” Cui Hongjian, director of European Union studies at Beijing Foreign Studies University, told the event.

During a Q&A session, a Chinese diplomat said that for hundreds of years China had had a trade surplus over Europe “but this didn’t prevent Europe from becoming a global leader in terms of economic development, social progress, technical innovation and classical music”.

The rhetorical dissonance stretched beyond trade and into technology.

During a panel discussion on technology, several Chinese speakers said the sides should establish a dialogue on AI issues, but Gunnar Wiegand, a former top EU diplomat on Asia, said such a format existed in the EU-China digital dialogue, which Beijing cancelled “just two weeks before it was supposed to happen in June”.

Sun Chenghao, head of the US-Europe programme at the Centre for International Security and Strategy at Tsinghua University, told the event that when proposing China-EU cooperation on AI, a European friend had said it was impossible because of concern “China would transfer data to Russia”. He said China had the same concerns “that Europeans will transfer our data to the US”.

Further technological tensions were telegraphed in Berlin this week, where German newspaper Handelsblatt said the government was preparing to classify 5G base stations as “security relevant”, a move that could lead to a de facto exclusion of Chinese companies Huawei Technologies and ZTE.

Despite the mutual suspicion, officials on both sides insist progress is possible without resolving every disagreement.

Joanna Szychowska, who manages EU trade policy on Asia, said October’s deal had transpired because the two sides had set aside competing interpretations of the relationship.

“We decided to focus on what really is a problem and how we want to tackle it,” Szychowska said.
