Malaysia urged to police its firms in Indonesia as haze shuts schools

SCMP | FREE | 1120 words | 2026-10-10

Malaysia is facing renewed pressure to clamp down on its companies over their alleged links to forest fires abroad, as recurring haze from Indonesia continues to disrupt schools and daily life despite years of regional efforts to tackle the problem.

On Thursday, Malaysia ordered schools to close across several states, including Johor, Malacca, Negeri Sembilan, Kuala Lumpur, Putrajaya and the Rompin district in Pahang, while sporting events scheduled for the weekend were cancelled.

The latest closures follow findings by environmental watchdogs Greenpeace and Pantau Gambut alleging links between Malaysian and Singaporean corporate interests and fire hotspots in Indonesia’s Kalimantan. The findings have intensified calls for Putrajaya to take action on companies linked to fires abroad, rather than rely on regional cooperation that has yet to prevent recurring haze crises.

On Wednesday, Greenpeace Malaysia and civil society groups marched to parliament, demanding stronger legislation to investigate companies linked to fires that have contributed to the ongoing haze crisis.

“Malaysia must have the legal power to investigate and hold companies accountable where credible evidence links them to environmental harm beyond our borders,” Greenpeace Malaysia campaign manager Heng Kiah Chun said in a statement.

“A transboundary haze law cannot remain on the drawing board while communities continue to bear the health consequences of haze year after year.”

Members of parliament received the memorandum from Greenpeace on Wednesday, with Bandar Kuching MP Kelvin Yii saying authorities had struggled to gather evidence, investigate and enforce laws against entities outside the country’s jurisdiction.

Bukit Bendera MP Syerleena Abdul Rashid said the recurring impact on schools, air quality and public health demanded stronger regional action.

“We already know the cause. Now we need action,” she said, according to local news outlets.

Malaysia has sought to draft a transboundary haze law since 2019, with the previous Pakatan Harapan government agreeing in principle in February 2020 to develop a framework that would give authorities powers to pursue companies over activities abroad. The proposal was championed by former environment minister Yeo Bee Yin after the severe haze crisis in 2019.

The proposal was not advanced after a change of government. The new administration argued that its ability to pursue companies for activities in foreign jurisdictions was limited, particularly without cooperation from foreign authorities.

As civil society groups press for the law to be revived, Malaysia faces the challenge of holding its companies accountable for environmental damage beyond its borders. Experts say targeted legislation and continued cooperation with Indonesia would be needed.

Helena Varkkey, an associate professor of environmental politics and governance at the University of Malaya, said the country had a responsibility to ensure its companies operated responsibly abroad but currently lacked the jurisdiction to take action against them for misconduct in Indonesia.

“If the Malaysian law is revived and passed according to this ‘Malaysian entities only’ format, it should be quite effective,” Varkkey said.

She said a revived law should focus on Malaysian entities operating overseas whose activities had caused haze at home, rather than adopting Singapore’s broader approach of pursuing companies regardless of nationality.

Such a focus could deter Malaysian companies while signalling to Indonesia that Malaysia was also overseeing its own entities.

Singapore enacted its Transboundary Haze Pollution Act in 2014, allowing authorities to pursue Singaporean and foreign entities whose conduct abroad causes or contributes to unhealthy haze in Singapore.

Under the law, perpetrators can face criminal or civil liability for activities such as land clearing by fire, with companies facing fines of S$100,000 (US$78,000) per day of haze pollution, capped at S$2 million.

Varkkey said Malaysia’s proposed legislation could also ease diplomatic tensions following Singapore’s adoption of its haze legislation, which Indonesia has viewed as interference.

“Limiting the legislation to Malaysian entities could give Indonesia diplomatic space to deal with other companies while avoiding diplomatic sensitivities of an extraterritorial law,” she said.

However, other experts said giving Malaysian authorities powers to pursue companies abroad would not solve the enforcement problem that derailed the legislation six years ago.

Renard Siew, climate adviser at the Malaysia-based Centre for Governance and Political Studies, said any transboundary haze law would need clear standards of liability, credible evidence, meaningful penalties and due process if it was to become more than a symbolic deterrent.

“A company’s responsibility should not stop at Malaysia’s borders simply because its operations are offshore,” Siew said, but cautioned that detecting fires alone does not establish who started them or prove corporate wrongdoing.

Greenpeace Malaysia, Greenpeace Indonesia and Pantau Gambut identified Malaysian plantation group Kuala Lumpur Kepong (KLK), through its reported ties to Indonesian entity Menteng Jaya Sawit Perdana, among the top 20 entities linked to findings on fires in Indonesia in 2026, according to a report released on October 5. The analysis drew on data tracking haze sources from August to October 1, the groups said.

Greenpeace said Menteng Jaya Sawit Perdana had also been identified as having fires on its land in a separate 2019 report. The findings do not, by themselves, establish that KLK or the Indonesian entity started the fires or was legally responsible for them.

“Forest and peatland fires that continue to persist are not part of a natural cycle but a reflection that economic expansion based on global commodities is valued more highly than the lives of citizens,” Greenpeace said in a statement.

Separately, Greenpeace Indonesia said its analysis identified 5,540 smoke-source points across Indonesia between August 8 and September 20. Of these, 774 were within 210 oil-palm plantation concessions and 859 within 115 licensed forestry concessions. The figures indicate the locations of detected smoke sources, not proof that concession holders caused the fires.

KLK rejected suggestions that Menteng Jaya Sawit Perdana was responsible for the fires, saying most had originated outside its concession area and had since been extinguished.

“As a Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil member, we uphold our zero burning policy aligned with its principles and criteria,” KLK said in a statement on October 6. “We do not condone or permit the use of open burning for land preparation or any other purpose across our operations.”

Experts say the dispute shows why legislation alone may not be enough, as Malaysia’s ability to pursue companies beyond its borders ultimately depends on obtaining evidence, establishing liability, and enforcing it against those found responsible.

While Asean cooperation matters, cooperation without enforceable obligations risks becoming a cycle of statements followed by the same crisis.

“The biggest failure is not a lack of regional commitments; it is the gap between commitments and consequences,” Siew said.

He said the fundamental problem was no longer the lack of regional commitments but the failure to translate those promises into consequences.

“When schools close, the costs are borne by the public, while those responsible may face limited consequences. This is fundamentally an accountability failure.”
