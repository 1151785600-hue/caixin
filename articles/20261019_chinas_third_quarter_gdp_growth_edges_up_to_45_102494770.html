1. China's economy grew 4.5% year-on-year in the third quarter, marking a slight acceleration from the previous quarter.

This performance narrowly surpassed market expectations, primarily due to stepped-up pro-growth policies and a comparatively low base of comparison from the prior year.

The modest pickup signals a degree of stabilization in the world's second-largest economy, although the breadth and durability of this recovery remain subject to close scrutiny by economists and investors. The low base effect, stemming from earlier subdued activity, provides some statistical tailwind, while the policy measures appear to be directly supporting investment and industrial output.

2. For the first nine months of the year, the National Bureau of Statistics reported on Monday that cumulative GDP growth reached 4.6%.

This year-to-date figure sits comfortably within the government's official annual target range of 4.5% to 5%.

The adherence to the target band underscores that the overall policy framework is effectively anchoring economic expectations. It also suggests that, barring unforeseen external shocks or a severe fourth-quarter slowdown, the full-year growth objective remains attainable. The data further illustrates a steady, if not spectacular, expansionary path, consistent with the government's emphasis on maintaining stability rather than pursuing aggressive stimulus.

3. Turning to nominal terms, Caixin calculations reveal that nominal GDP—which measures total output at current market prices—grew by 6% year-on-year in the third quarter.

]

This represents the fastest pace of nominal expansion since the first quarter of 2022.

[para.

The acceleration in nominal growth is largely attributed to higher energy prices and price increases associated with the booming artificial intelligence (AI) sector.

[para.

4

]

These specific price drivers have inflated the monetary value of production, creating a notable gap between real output growth (4.5%) and nominal growth (6%). This divergence indicates that while the physical volume of goods and services produced is increasing moderately, inflationary pressures in certain key sectors are significantly boosting the overall dollar value of economic activity.

4. However, these upstream price gains provided only limited relief further down the supply chain.

In September, factory-gate inflation—the gauge measuring prices received by domestic producers—reached its highest level in more than four years.

This substantial rise in producer prices reflects strengthening cost pressures or improved pricing power at the industrial level. Conversely, consumer price inflation improved only marginally during the same period.

This distinct disparity between the Producer Price Index (PPI) and the Consumer Price Index (CPI) underscores a highly uneven price recovery across the economy. The gap implies that producers are facing higher input costs but are largely unable to pass these on to end consumers, squeezing their profit margins. Simultaneously, the feeble improvement in consumer prices suggests that household demand remains weak and sensitive, pointing to persistent softness in domestic consumption.
