What China’s expanded funding for HIV/Aids in Africa means as US cuts health aid

SCMP | FREE | 1036 words | 2026-08-07

China is expanding its role in the fight against HIV/Aids in Africa by funding new projects and backing local medicine manufacturing as part of its signature health initiative, the Health Silk Road.

Beijing’s larger presence comes as traditional Western aid wanes following the closure of the US Agency for International Development and sweeping cuts to the World Health Organization and the President’s Emergency Plan for Aids Relief (Pepfar).

Pepfar was a major bilateral American initiative providing life-saving antiretroviral treatment and healthcare support for millions of people in developing countries.

Instead, Washington has signed bilateral health agreements with some African countries, though a few such as Zambia and Zimbabwe declined to sign the deals over worries about data sovereignty and sensitive health information sharing.

Following a recent trip to China, UNAids executive director Winnie Byanyima revealed that China’s Global Development and South-South Cooperation Fund was backing HIV prevention projects in Lesotho, South Africa, Uganda and Zimbabwe.

South Africa, which has about eight million people living with HIV, received a US$3.49 million Chinese grant last year to fund prevention programmes targeting more than 50,000 young people and harm-reduction services for people who inject drugs.

In Uganda, Chinese funding supports the Triple Elimination Project to end mother-to-child transmission of HIV, syphilis and hepatitis B.

Byanyima said the China International Development Cooperation Agency (CIDCA) had invited UNAids to submit further project funding proposals for regions facing distinct HIV challenges.

“Grateful for China’s strong commitment to multilateralism and understanding that global epidemics must be fought together,” Byanyima said in July after the trip.

The funding ties into Beijing’s broader health diplomacy under the Health Silk Road initiative, which has previously seen China fund programmes addressing Ebola and malaria across the continent.

According to the Chinese Loans to Africa Database at Boston University’s Global Development Policy Centre, China committed US$1.3 billion in health financing to Africa between 2000 and 2024.

Beijing also funded and built the US$80 million Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) headquarters and reference laboratories in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, as a gift for Africa to help fight diseases.

Those amounts came alongside direct government grants through agencies such as the CIDCA and the deployment of thousands of medical experts over decades.

Shenglan Tang, co-director of the Global Health Research Centre at Duke Kunshan University in China, said that as traditional donors, particularly the US, reassessed their foreign aid priorities, important financing gaps were emerging in global health.

“China appears increasingly willing to provide targeted support in areas where it believes it can add value, especially through South-South cooperation, technical collaboration and capacity building,” Tang said.

He observed that the recent support provided through China’s Global Development and South-South Cooperation Fund, as well as the invitation for additional UNAids concept notes, suggested that “China is becoming more willing to finance selected global health initiatives”.

However, Tang described what was happening as an incremental evolution rather than a major transformation.

“China’s financial contribution to HIV remains modest compared with that of Pepfar and the Global Fund, but its role may become increasingly important when reductions in traditional donor funding may continue,” he said.

Meeting with officials in China in July, Byanyima voiced enthusiasm for partnering with the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology to strengthen local and regional manufacturing of health commodities in Africa.

“I appreciate China’s willingness to share the dividends of the development of its health sector in the interest of the shared future for all humanity,” Byanyima said.

However, David Shinn, a China-Africa expert and former US diplomat in Africa, said China remained a minor player in HIV/Aids treatment compared with the US, which continued to provide billions globally despite local funding shifts.

“Although the US did stop funding in South Africa, it continues to provide about US$5 billion globally each year for the prevention and treatment of HIV/Aids, much of it going to Africa,” Shinn said.

He added that a close look at “China’s contributions demonstrates they are small but strategically located and heavily publicised”.

“China’s strength continues to be in the sending of medical teams to most African countries, a contribution that is rare from Western countries.”

According to Zhou Taidong, vice-president of the China Centre for International Knowledge on Development, affiliated with the Development Research Centre of the State Council, supporting HIV prevention under the Beijing-proposed Global Development Initiative aligns with broader health security.

Rather than treating HIV in isolation, China linked it to wider system resilience, Zhou said.

“China’s model increasingly focuses on building productive capacity, including pharmaceutical manufacturing, vaccine production, [Africa] CDC laboratories, hospital construction and digital health infrastructure.”

This approach, he added, “reflects China’s broader development philosophy that sustainable health outcomes require industrial capability as well as financing”.

“China’s role in HIV and broader health security is likely to become more capacity-oriented than purely aid-oriented.”

Rather than replacing traditional Western donors in financing service delivery, China could be expected to focus on complementary contributions such as health infrastructure, workforce development, technology transfer, pharmaceutical manufacturing and partnerships under South-South cooperation, Zhou said.

Tang, who is also a professor of medicine and global health at Duke University in the US, noted that African countries increasingly viewed local pharmaceutical production as an essential industrial development strategy following coronavirus pandemic-related supply disruptions.

Accordingly, China was regarded as offering affordable technologies and scalable capacity for technology transfer and investment partnerships, he said.

“The Covid-19 pandemic exposed Africa’s heavy dependence on imported medicines and vaccines, strengthening political commitment to regional manufacturing, as we have seen now in many African countries.”

Furthermore, China possessed extensive manufacturing capacity, relatively affordable production technologies and experience in scaling pharmaceutical production, Tang said.

“These strengths make it an attractive partner for technology transfer, investment and industrial cooperation.”

More Chinese pharmaceutical manufacturers had worked with their African counterparts to achieve a win-win outcome in this regard, he said.

Overall, Tang expected China’s role in global health, including HIV, to “continue expanding gradually”, while adding that it was “unlikely to replace the scale of traditional Western financing in the foreseeable future”.
