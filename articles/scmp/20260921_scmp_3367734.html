Pacific island states are trapped in Cold War 2.0

SCMP | FREE | 1067 words | 2026-09-21

In early July, China fired a test missile from a nuclear submarine into the South Pacific for the first time, a weapon capable of carrying nuclear warheads. Washington and Canberra were livid. The Pacific has long been the exclusive testing ground for such long-range missiles from the United States. How dare China!

The reaction from Pacific island states was more measured. While leaders from Tuvalu, Palau and the Solomon Islands strongly criticised the test, the 18-member Pacific Islands Forum (PIF) failed to draft a united statement. Last month, foreign ministers again failed to agree on criticising Beijing, with Kiribati and Nauru reportedly refusing.

Finally, this month, a PIF statement that “registered concern on the recent intercontinental ballistic missile (ICBM) test” was issued. China was not named, and Nauru disassociated itself.

Kiribati previously said singling out China was unfair when the US regularly tested such missiles near the Marshall Islands. In March, the US launched a Minuteman III ICBM from California, landing near the Marshall Islands 6,700km away; it followed a similar test in November last year. The US has test-fired at least 15 ballistic missiles into the Pacific since 2021.

The historical precedent weighs heavily. Between 1946 and 1996, the US, Britain and France conducted over 300 nuclear tests in the Pacific, particularly in French Polynesia and the Marshall Islands, resulting in radioactive contamination, mass displacement, high cancer rates and generational trauma. The double standard is glaring.

Geopolitically, the region resembles a feudal hierarchy: small island nations as vassal states, Australia and New Zealand as local lords, and the US as king.

Since its Aukus alliance with Britain and the US, Australia has increasingly resembled just another vassal. According to a recent Guardian report, US military interests control or have access to more than 100 defence facilities on Australian soil – what some analysts describe as a “colonisation”. Data by California’s Nautilus Institute shows the US military controls 17 facilities on mainland Australia and has access to 75 more, with defence corporations accessing an additional 18.

Meanwhile, under the Compacts of Free Association, the Marshall Islands, Micronesia and Palau operate as US defence outposts, with Washington exercising defence veto power over their foreign conduct.

Australia’s diplomatic recalibration towards China reveals a troubling disconnect. Prime Minister Anthony Albanese initially pursued stabilisation after Beijing lifted punitive sanctions. China ended embargoes on Australian wine, barley and lobster by December 2024 as bilateral trade soared.

Yet stability proved ephemeral. Albanese now speaks openly of China seeking “hegemony” – clearly an exaggeration, paranoia or both. He defended the US missile tests, saying there was no comparison with the Chinese test. He is right – who can compare with America when it comes to weapons tests?

This tougher posture coincides with alarming developments. As US President Donald Trump’s erratic Asia policy erodes Washington’s credibility, Canberra doubles down, gambling its strategic future on a declining Pax Americana. Australia’s flurry of Pacific defence pacts with Fiji, Papua New Guinea and Vanuatu signals renewed great-power competition even as Aukus submarine deliveries remain uncertain.

Beijing has warned of “measures” over any challenge to the Chinese lease for Darwin port even as it distances itself from what it calls “selfish political gains”. Australian public opinion diverges from elite consensus: 61 per cent view China as an economic partner rather than a security threat. Yet Australia’s security establishment reflexively adopts Washington’s hawkish assumptions precisely as their foundation crumbles.

As Laura Tingle, ABC’s global affairs editor, wrote recently: “The world is a particularly confusing place just now. But no one seems more confused about their place in it than Australia.”

Meanwhile, former New Zealand prime minister Helen Clark has issued a pointed warning: Wellington’s rush into Pacific security pacts threatens to erode its cherished tradition of independent foreign policy. Clark – who also led the UN’s development arm for eight years – argued that such moves undermine the Pacific’s future as “a zone of peace”.

For decades, New Zealand balanced independence from superpower blocs with maintaining a nuclear-free Pacific. Both pillars now face strain. “The Australians being in the market for nuclear submarines isn’t helpful,” Clark said, predicting Canberra will host an American base for such vessels.

Her critique targets the premise that Washington can indefinitely guarantee Pacific security while managing global commitments. Invoking Australian strategist Hugh White’s argument that China’s rise makes US primacy unsustainable, Clark cited America’s Iran preoccupation as evidence of overreach. “Apply that to the Asia-Pacific context. It’s not real, is it? So this is all a game of bluff.”

Australian public opinion diverges from elite consensus: 61 per cent view China as an economic partner rather than a security threat.

Clark warned that “New Zealand must never be seen as a proxy”. Her 2003 refusal to send combat troops to Iraq defined her premiership’s principled independence. She accused the current coalition government of having made “unfortunate moves”. On China, Clark counselled pragmatism: “We need to continually work on how we maintain good engagement and dialogue”.

Small nations preserve agency through principle, not alignment. Wellington should remember that. As usual, however, the interests of small island nations are being trampled on amid their larger neighbours’ strategic muddle.

The Pacific islands deserve better than to become pawns in a new cold war. Their residents suffered generations of trauma from nuclear testing conducted by powers that now demand their loyalty in condemning others for far less. They face existential threats from climate change while watching wealthy Western nations prioritise military competition over climate action.

Australia and New Zealand style themselves as Pacific champions, but their actions reveal different priorities. Canberra’s strategic panic drives deeper military integration with Washington, while Wellington’s traditional independence frays. Neither has offered Pacific islanders a vision beyond choosing sides in someone else’s conflict.

Washington, Canberra and Wellington cannot credibly claim to champion Pacific interests while maintaining double standards on missile testing. The US is militarising Australian territory while reducing island states to defence outposts. Australia cannot reconcile economic interdependence with China and security anxieties through military posturing. New Zealand is surrendering the independent foreign policy that gives it moral standing.

The small island states’ months-long hesitation before issuing a mildly worded statement without naming China shows they no longer automatically align with the Anglo-American sphere. They need to assert greater agency if they are to have a fighting chance against climate change and outside dominance.
