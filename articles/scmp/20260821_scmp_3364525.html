Will Putin’s Kuril Islands visit push Japan closer to Ukraine?

SCMP | FREE | 1312 words | 2026-08-21

Japan is weighing how far to push back after Russian President Vladimir Putin visited a disputed island last week, as analysts debate whether tougher measures would have much impact on Moscow.

Some observers urged Japan to recall its ambassador, impose additional sanctions and deepen cooperation with Ukraine to show it would not be “bullied”, while others said Tokyo had little economic leverage left given already weak bilateral ties and its reliance on Russian natural gas.

Putin made his first-ever visit to one of four disputed islands, collectively called the Northern Territories by Japan and the Southern Kurils by Russia, last Thursday.

Japanese media reported that senior foreign ministry officials discussed possible responses on Friday, including tougher sanctions and the temporary recall of Japan’s ambassador.

On Tuesday, Japan’s envoy to Moscow Akira Muto was summoned to the Russian foreign ministry after Tokyo protested against Putin’s visit.

During the meeting, Russian Deputy Foreign Minister Mikhail Galuzin warned that his country reserved the “right to take appropriate countermeasures” against Japan, given Tokyo’s continued policy of sanctioning Moscow and its support for Kyiv, according to the Russian foreign ministry.

The Japanese embassy in Moscow said Muto “appropriately explained” Tokyo’s stance in the 20-minute session with Galuzin.

Putin travelled to Iturup Island – known as Etorofu in Japan – where he visited a fish-processing plant and met local residents.

Japan claims the Soviet Union illegally seized the islands – Etorofu, Kunashiri, Shikotan and the Habomai islet group – soon after Japan’s surrender at the end of the second world war in 1945. Russia maintains that the action was legitimate, however.

Describing Putin’s visit as “grave” and “absolutely unacceptable”, Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi warned last Thursday that the visit would harden anti-Russian sentiment in Japan and make restoring relations more difficult in the medium to long term.

That same day, her Foreign Minister Toshimitsu Motegi summoned Russian ambassador Nikolay Nozdrev to lodge a formal protest, making it clear that Tokyo considers the island an inherent part of Japanese territory both historically and under international law.

On Tuesday, the Russian ministry said in a statement that Galuzin had “conveyed a strong protest” to Muto over Takaichi’s and Motegi’s “anti-Russia” remarks.

Adding that the Southern Kurils were “transferred to our country following World War II”, the statement said that “any attempts by the Japanese side to directly or indirectly call this obvious reality into question are categorically unacceptable and illegitimate”.

Closer to Kyiv?

In addition to recalling its ambassador to Russia and imposing additional sanctions, Japan could respond by deepening cooperation with Ukraine, said James Brown, a professor of international relations specialising in Russian affairs at Temple University’s Tokyo campus.

Takaichi has yet to have a face-to-face meeting with President Volodymyr Zelensky, even though both leaders attended the Group of Seven summit in France in June.

“A face-to-face meeting, especially if it entailed a visit to Kyiv by Takaichi, would send a strong message that Japan will not be bullied by Russia into changing its foreign policy,” Brown said, adding that Putin’s visit came as Japan had been exploring tentative re-engagement with Russia.

This included sending a delegation from the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry to Moscow in May, and restarting foreign ministry-backed student visits in July.

“This tentative re-engagement, which was always controversial, is now likely to be curtailed,” Brown added.

Michito Tsuruoka, a professor of policy management at Keio University, said Tokyo’s priority should be showing Russia that it could not pressure Japan into changing its policies.

“Strengthening support to Ukraine would be a possible response, though whether the prime minister is interested in this remains unclear,” he said.

There does not appear to be a consensus within the Japanese government on how to handle relations with Russia, according to Tsuruoka.

“Different actors have different views on what Russia is and they see different aspects of [the country] and their meaning to Japan,” he added.

While the economy ministry saw Japan’s northern neighbour as an important energy source – more so given recent developments in the Strait of Hormuz – Tsuruoka said the defence ministry was increasingly concerned about deepening Russia-China and Russia-North Korea ties and the Russian military’s increasing activities near Japan.

“A new consensus needs to be built,” Tsuruoka said.

The Strait of Hormuz has been all but closed since the United States and Israel launched their war against Iran in late February. Before the conflict, Japan was importing over 90 per cent of its crude oil, and a portion of its liquefied natural gas (LNG), through the strait.

Last month, Japan’s defence ministry said that a Russian military IL-20 reconnaissance aircraft had intruded into its territorial airspace over disputed islands, with Russia conducting large-scale live-fire naval drills involving 60 warships and 13,000 troops nearby less than two weeks later.

‘No economic leverage’

Artyom Lukin, a professor at the Far Eastern Federal University in Vladivostok, questioned Japan’s ability to impose any significant economic penalties on Moscow over Putin’s visit.

“Russia-Japan economic ties have already been reduced to a bare minimum because of the Ukraine conflict,” he said, noting that bilateral trade was now largely limited to Russia supplying LNG and seafood while importing Japanese vehicles.

“Japan cannot halt LNG imports from Russia’s Sakhalin Island because it is difficult to find viable alternatives, especially amid the continuing crisis in the Middle East,” Lukin said.

There were also no alternatives to some Russian seafood delicacies, he added, saying: “For the Japanese, seafood specialities are far more important than Ukraine and the Kuril Islands combined.”

Should Japan ban car exports to Russia, Russians was likely to respond by buying from China and South Korea instead, according to Lukin.

“In a nutshell, Japan has no economic leverage over Russia,” he said, adding that Tokyo’s main response would be “rhetorical”.

Japan’s bilateral trade with Russia hit roughly US$8 billion last year, dwarfed by its trade with China at about US$300 billion. Over the same period, Japan’s Russian LNG supplies ticked up 2 per cent from 2024 levels to about 5.8 million tonnes, making up about 9 per cent of Japan’s total annual LNG imports.

By comparison, Japan imports roughly 11 per cent of its LNG from the Middle East, according to Reuters.

While Japan could “theoretically” step up military activities near Russia, Lukin said Tokyo was unlikely to do so as it needed those resources to deal with the challenge posed by China.

In its latest Defence White Paper issued earlier this month, Tokyo repeated and reinforced its stance that China’s rapid and opaque military expansion was its “greatest strategic challenge”.

Takaichi took advantage of Putin’s trip to score an easy win “with her hardcore nationalistic audience”, according to Lukin, who said Moscow had always maintained that its sovereignty over Iturup and Kunashir – the larger of the disputed islands – was non-negotiable.

“Russia was only ready to negotiate the two smaller islands, Shikotan and Habomai,” he said. “Takaichi is not naive. I guess she fully realises that Russia is never going to return Iturup to Japan.”

Bilateral relations were already in the doldrums but could worsen, Brown said, citing Japanese concerns over Russia’s possible provision of military technology to North Korea in exchange for Pyongyang’s assistance in the Ukraine war.

“If Russia [strengthens] this support by meaningfully enhancing the North’s ballistic missile and nuclear capabilities, it would lead to even greater tensions between Tokyo and Moscow,” Brown said.

Analysts say Tokyo is concerned that Russian technology transfers will accelerate North Korea’s nuclear, ballistic missile and hypersonic weapons programmes, threatening the regional security balance.
