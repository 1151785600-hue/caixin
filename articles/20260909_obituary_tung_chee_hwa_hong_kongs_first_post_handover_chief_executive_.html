Tung Chee-hwa, Hong Kong's first chief executive after its handover from British to Chinese rule, died peacefully on Sept. 8 at Hong Kong Sanatorium & Hospital, aged 89, surrounded by family. Sworn in on July 1, 1997, his legacy is tied to the "one country, two systems" framework, marked by his defining declaration: "For the first time in history, we, the people of Hong Kong, will be masters of our own destiny."

His office issued an official obituary memorializing Tung as a "righteous patriot" who dedicated his life to the nation, with details of memorial services from central and local governments expected shortly.

His tenure was immediately beset by crises, including the Asian Financial Crisis and the 2003 SARS epidemic, which derailed his ambitious plans. In his inaugural policy address in October 1997, he proposed building 85,000 apartments annually to achieve 70% household ownership within a decade. This policy collided violently with the regional financial crisis: property values plummeted over 60%, leaving many middle-class families with negative equity, and unemployment rose to a historical high of 8.7% by mid-2003. Observers later noted he suffered from extraordinarily bad timing.

During his second term, Hong Kong and mainland China signed the Closer Economic Partnership Arrangement, initiating an individual visit scheme for mainland tourists that fundamentally shaped the city's retail and tourism sectors for two decades. However, political pressure mounted: his 2003 push for national security legislation under Article 23 of the Basic Law sparked widespread protests, and he resigned in March 2005 citing health reasons, succeeded by Donald Tsang. That same month, he was appointed vice chairman of the Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference, a role he held for 18 years until March 2023.

Born in Shanghai in 1937 into a maritime dynasty, Tung moved to Hong Kong at age 12, studied at the leftist Chung Hwa Middle School, and graduated from the University of Liverpool in 1960. After eight years rising through the ranks in the U.S., he returned to assist his father; by 1973, the family company, Orient Overseas Container Line (OOCL), went public.

6.

[para.

Following his father's sudden death in 1982, Tung took the helm amid the global oil crisis, facing tens of billions in Hong Kong dollars of debt and near bankruptcy in the mid-1980s. He secured a crucial $120 million capital injection from the late businessman Henry Fok and spent a decade returning the enterprise to profitability.

His political emergence paralleled his corporate struggles: he joined the Hong Kong Basic Law Consultative Committee in 1985, was named a Hong Kong affairs adviser by Beijing in 1992, and became a CPPCC delegate in 1993. He also served on the Executive Council under last British governor Chris Patten. In December 1996, a 400-member selection committee gave Tung 320 votes, defeating former Chief Justice Ti-liang Yang and businessman Peter Woo for the highest office.

Beyond local politics, Tung wielded extraordinary influence in Sino-U.S. diplomacy, hosting President Bill Clinton in 1998, privately meeting former President George H.W. Bush in 1999, and meeting both Clintons and George W. Bush during U.S. visits in 2000 and 2001. He also chaired the China-U.S. Exchange Foundation for years. Singapore's Lee Kuan Yew characterized his old friend bluntly in 2005 as "too nice a man, not sufficiently young and nimble."

In 2014, Tung founded Our Hong Kong Foundation, a think tank advocating for land reclamation and housing supply—seen by many as an effort to finish his unresolved 1997 housing policy. His final public appearance was on July 4, 2021, at a film premiere celebrating the Chinese Communist Party's centenary. His health declined soon after, and he underwent heart surgery at Queen Mary Hospital in September 2021, spending his remaining years recuperating at home.
