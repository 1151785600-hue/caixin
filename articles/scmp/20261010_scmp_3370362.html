Southeast Asia takes on Big Tech to protect children online

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2410 words | 2026-10-10

Indonesia officially barred under-16s from using “high-risk” social media earlier this year. But that hasn’t stopped the son of one Jakarta-based writer from using it.

Aged 14, he already had accounts on Instagram and TikTok – among the platforms Jakarta said children would be banned from using – as well as popular video game Minecraft and gaming platform Roblox. To register, he simply lied about his age.

“He can access everything,” Inne Abidin said of her son. “His friends use everything too.”

Since March, Indonesia has had some of Southeast Asia’s strictest rules governing children’s social media use.

Yet like elsewhere in the region, such restrictions are only as effective as the systems Big Tech platforms put in place to identify underage users – and keep them out.

From Malaysia to Vietnam, the Philippines and Singapore, governments that once merely urged platforms to protect children are now imposing rules designed to force social media companies to take responsibility.

Patchy compliance

Indonesia’s child protection regulations have already had an effect on how social media companies operate.

In April, Meta Platforms raised the minimum age for a Facebook, Instagram or Threads account from 13 to 16 in Indonesia, with YouTube following suit later that month. Both said they would begin a phased deactivation of accounts belonging to underage users.

TikTok, which was the first platform to comply with the new rules, raised its minimum platform age to 16 on March 28. By October, it had deactivated 9 million underage accounts, Indonesia’s digital affairs minister announced on Wednesday.

And online gaming platform Roblox now scans Indonesian users’ faces to estimate their age, restricts some communication features for children and has added age-based account limitations.

Yet youngsters seemingly still find ways to skirt the rules. To combat this, Indonesia’s government has given tech companies until the end of the year to complete comprehensive self-assessments detailing how their algorithms, age-verification and ad-targeting systems protect children.

If the platforms fail to comply, the Communications and Digital Affairs Ministry has finalised a sanctions mechanism that – once codified into law – would allow Indonesia to levy fines of up to 6 per cent of tech firms’ global revenue. Applied to Meta’s reported revenue for 2025, that would amount to roughly US$12 billion.

Such a fine would not be without precedent. In August, Meta agreed to pay a coalition of US states up to US$18 billion to settle lawsuits alleging Facebook and Instagram were deliberately designed to make children addicted and caused them harm.

The settlement also forced sweeping changes to how teens experience the platforms, including daily usage limits, overnight usage blocks and strengthened age-verification controls.

Meta has reportedly migrated some 22.5 million Indonesian platform users across to these “teen accounts”, but for the country’s government this does not go far enough.

“While we see this as progress, we still do not consider Instagram and Facebook teen accounts to be low-risk,” Communication and Digital Affairs Minister Meutya Hafid told reporters in Jakarta on Wednesday. “They remain categorised as high-risk.”

Unggul Sagena, digital rights advocate

For Southeast Asia, the unavoidable question now being asked is whether governments can turn the threat of penalties into real change.

“In my view, the level of cooperation comes down to a few factors: how valuable the market is, how credible the government’s enforcement is, whether the rule threatens the platform’s global business model, how closely it aligns with obligations they already meet elsewhere and whether it carries legitimacy,” said Unggul Sagena, head of internet access at the Southeast Asia Freedom of Expression Network, an Indonesian digital rights organisation that advocates for internet freedom and user safety.

As the region’s largest economy, the sheer size of Indonesia’s consumer market – as well as its risk-based approach to online child protection – lent its arguments and regulations weight that smaller countries lacked, Unggul said.

“Global platforms are complying,” he said. “Even if not yet fully.”

Part of the reason for the changes is the legal machinery Indonesia already has in place, including a regulation passed in 2020 that obliges platforms to act on government takedown orders within tight deadlines, as short as four hours in urgent cases.

“The government has also taken the issue directly to headquarters,” Unggul said, noting that the communications minister met representatives from Meta’s global public policy and safety leadership team in New York last month to push for faster deactivation of underage accounts.

“Child protection is hard to oppose publicly, so resisting it carries a high reputational cost,” he added.

Still, Indonesia’s proposed fine framework has yet to be legally codified and many question marks remain over how it will ultimately be implemented.

Unresolved issues include how to collect fines from companies with no local assets, how to accurately verify global revenue “and what due process exists when the same ministry writes the rules, judges compliance and imposes the sanction”, Unggul said.

Blocking sites, meanwhile, “hurts Indonesian users more than the companies”, he said. “And the government can order [content] removals, but it still has little visibility into how the platforms are actually designed.”

The threat of high fines could push platforms to invest more in safety-by-design and high-privacy defaults for children, said Indriyatno Banyumurti, executive director of ICT Watch, an Indonesian civil society organisation focused on digital literacy.

But he warned that legal clarity and consistent enforcement were key, stressing that child safety “should not depend solely on voluntary corporate commitments”.

Indriyatno said much would depend on how violations were defined, how compliance was monitored, how evidence was assessed and whether sanctions could be applied fairly and consistently.

Regional rules

Indonesia’s social media ban may have been Southeast Asia’s first, but it is far from alone in trying to make Big Tech take responsibility for children’s safety online.

In Malaysia, rules in force since June now bar under-16s from opening or maintaining social media accounts and require platforms to verify users’ ages. Companies that fail to comply face fines of up to 10 million ringgit (US$2.5 million), with further penalties for ignoring regulatory directives.

Vietnam is considering a draft decree that would limit under-16s to a view-only mode of social media, preventing them from posting content, commenting, sharing or reacting to other posts. Accounts would have to be registered using a parent or guardian’s details under the proposed rules, with platforms compelled to block age-inappropriate material and place caps on children’s online gaming time.

The Philippines’ approach has been more piecemeal. It already legally forces internet providers to block child sexual abuse material or face heavy penalties and has used regulatory pressure to squeeze direct concessions from platforms. Lawmakers are also considering introducing age limits and other measures.

In August, Meta and Roblox agreed to strengthen age checks, parental controls and content takedowns following talks with Philippine authorities over child safety and online radicalisation. A spate of school shootings this year also spurred officials in Manila to order Reddit and Discord to appoint local representatives to coordinate on online threats. Discord was briefly blocked before agreeing to cooperate.

Singapore requires social media giants to shield users, including children, from harmful material under its Online Safety (Miscellaneous Amendments) Act that came into effect in 2023, with non-compliance carrying a financial penalty of up to S$1 million (US$781,500).

App stores have been legally required to verify the ages of users who want to download age-restricted material in the city state since April this year. And an Online Safety Commission, launched in June under legislation passed last year, has the power to order the immediate removal of harmful content and restrict offending accounts.

While these efforts have helped effect some change, analysts say the results have been uneven.

“Platforms do respond, but market size is a factor they consider,” said regional tech policy adviser Paco Pangalangan, a technology and human rights fellow at the Harvard Kennedy School in the US. “A large market might get more attention, while a smaller one might get a slower, more minimal version. Whether there’s a law matters too.”

By his count, most of the big social media companies do not even have in-country representatives in five of Asean’s 11 member states.

“So there’s often no real channel for regulators or civil society to raise problems,” Pangalangan said.

Strength in numbers

Individual governments, especially those that represent sizeable markets with credible enforcement mechanisms, may be able to force social media platforms to comply with their rules.

But global technology companies can also opt to deal with countries one by one, which analysts and advocates say makes it harder for smaller markets to exert sustained pressure. One solution could be to present a more united front.

“A regional coalition can push Big Tech to create child-friendly ecosystems in ways no single country can,” Unggul said. “Individually, each Asean country is a market platforms can afford to deprioritise. Together, the region is not.”

Pangalangan said that, taken as a whole, the Association of Southeast Asian Nations could leverage the region’s more than 400 million social media users to strengthen its hand in negotiations..

“Collectively, we have a market that platforms can’t afford to simply deprioritise,” he said.

“If we can agree on common standards, including requirements around how platforms are designed, there’s less room for platforms to play one country off against another or just wait things out.”

Allan Cabanlong, Asean regional director for cybersecurity and cloud services provider CyberCX’s regional cyber advisers programme and a former assistant secretary at the Philippines’ Department of Information and Communications Technology, agreed that collective action could hand regional governments greater bargaining power.

“A broom with one strand cannot sweep,” he said. “We have hundreds of millions of users in Asean. If the Philippines were to stand alone, it would not be listened to.”

As this year’s Asean chair, the Philippines could push the protection of children online higher up the regional agenda, Cabanlong said, while the bloc could also work to strengthen its existing mechanisms for digital regulation.

Asean would not be starting from scratch. Its declaration on the protection of children from online exploitation and abuse, adopted in 2019, and associated regional plans of action already recognise online harm to children as a cross-bogrder issue.

Other frameworks on personal data protection – and a declaration on safe and responsible social media use, adopted last October – widen that scope to include online radicalisation and AI-related risks.

But few observers expect a single, regional law on children’s social media use to be the end result.

“Asean does not necessarily have to start by creating one identical social media law for all member states,” said Ysrael Diloy, executive director of Stairway Foundation, a non-profit children’s rights organisation in the Philippines.

“Given differences in national legal systems, regulatory structures and capacities, that may not be the most realistic starting point.”

Indriyatno agreed that a more realistic approach would be for Asean to agree on common expectations for factors such as risk assessments, minimum standards of platform accountability, privacy defaults for children and cooperation on cross-border cases.

“Regulators could also develop more compatible requirements for platform transparency and information sharing,” he said.

Alejandro Reyes, chief strategic officer of non-profit organisation AI Safety Asia, said national regulators would also need to share information and cooperate more closely.

“If Singapore identifies a serious failure in a platform’s child-safety systems, its counterparts in Malaysia, the Philippines and elsewhere should have access to those findings,” he said.

“Joint investigations, shared technical expertise and coordinated enforcement would make it harder for companies to exploit differences between national regulatory regimes.”

Such a baseline would not demand identical laws, but it would provide common ground in dealing with companies that straddle borders.

It would also carry risks. “The concern here is that child protection is part of a much broader framework of government control over online content and expression,” Reyes said, urging caution given the differences in countries’ legal systems, political priorities and enforcement capacity.

Other issues, according to Unggul, include the fact that Asean members currently compete for technology investment, have very different track records when it comes to free expression and make decisions as a bloc by consensus.

“Collective leverage only works if it is credible and rights-based, and that is where civil society across the region must make sure the coalition strengthens accountability rather than state control over content,” he said.

“Child safety must not become cover for censorship. Bargaining power comes not only from penalties but from legitimacy.”

Limits of laws

Governments can restrict access, require age verification and impose penalties on platforms, but they cannot regulate the behaviour of children and families, online child safety experts say.

Fandy Zulmi, programme manager at the Centre for Child Study and Protection in Indonesia, pointed to recent Communications Ministry data showing that three out of five Indonesian children had falsified their age to access restricted platforms.

Without stronger digital literacy, parental involvement and robust reporting mechanisms, Indonesia’s social media ban for children might simply move the risks “somewhere else”, he said.

“It is still too early to conclude that the policy has effectively deterred children from going online,” Fandy said.

Indonesia’s implementation challenge begins with the fact that there are between 60 million and 80 million children in the country whose circumstances differ enormously, according to Diena Haryana, founder of the Sejiwa Foundation, a Jakarta-based child welfare organisation.

“Where they live, what education their parents have, what digital parenting awareness the parents have, whether the parents themselves have digital skills – all of that varies massively, especially between somewhere like Jakarta and rural areas,” she said.

Some parents used social media platforms to keep children occupied while they worked, Diena said, while others were more aware of the risks involved with giving their children unfettered access.

“We all need to work much harder to make sure children understand what the regulation is, why it exists and how implementation should actually work,” she said.

But ultimately, it is the platforms themselves that bear the brunt of the responsibility for child safety, according to Pangalangan.

“They already know where many of the harms are,” he said. “They don’t need a law to fix things they control in their own products.”
