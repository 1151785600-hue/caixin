Why Sabah has Malaysia watching South China Sea tensions more closely

SCMP | FREE | 1124 words | 2026-10-02

Malaysia’s long-standing strategy of non-alignment amid US-China rivalry in the South China Sea could soon be tested, with the eastern state of Sabah sitting near a potential flashpoint involving Philippine territory.

Observers pointed to comments by Malaysian Defence Minister Khaled Nordin, who last week voiced concern that a conflict or “unexpected incident” in the maritime region could spill over into Malaysia.

Khaled, who singled out Sabah, added that Malaysia was looking to strengthen its national defence as a precautionary measure.

“We know the challenges in the South China Sea and the rivalry between major powers. If something happens in that geographically small area, it could also affect our country,” local media quoted Khaled as saying on Saturday.

The Philippines and the US have deepened defence cooperation since 2014, including by expanding American military access to Philippine facilities under the Enhanced Defence Cooperation Agreement (EDCA) as tensions with China have mounted.

Two of the nine EDCA sites accessible to US forces are in Palawan, an island province north of Sabah. They include Antonio Bautista Air Base in Puerto Princesa and Naval Station Narciso Del Rosario on Balabac Island. The sites remain under Philippine control and while they do not function as permanent US bases, the agreement allows American forces rotational access.

Balabac, the southernmost EDCA site and closest to Sabah, is being developed to include a 3km (1.86 mile) military runway.

About 50km (31 miles) away lies Banggi Island, the northernmost part of Sabah state that sits at the southern end of the so-called first island chain – a Cold War-era American defence concept describing an arc of islands extending from Japan through Taiwan to the Philippines and Malaysia that act as a strategic bottleneck for China’s navy.

Hoo Chiew-Ping, a senior fellow at the East Asian International Relations Caucus, said this proximity meant Malaysia could no longer ignore the possibility of a conflict spreading southwards even as it sought to remain non-aligned.

Malaysian Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim raised concerns over Balabac in August, pledging to strengthen Malaysia’s defences in response to the growing US-backed Philippine military presence on the nearby island.

“As a neighbouring country closely affected by claims over Sabah, we are somewhat concerned with the tension, even though our bilateral relations with the Philippines remain very good,” Anwar said on August 18.

Malaysia could not match the scale of military facilities being developed on Balabac, he said, noting a need to strengthen basic defensive capabilities as the regional security environment became increasingly shaped by US-China competition.

Hoo said Malaysian defence and security strategists had yet to fully recognise this reality despite long-term defence cooperation with the US, including extensive joint military exercises in these areas.

“The assumption that a US-China military confrontation could occur near Borneo stems from concerns over a Taiwan Strait contingency, where the Philippines would become the immediate frontline,” Hoo said. “If the conflict is about Taiwan, strategic space will become extremely narrow.”

Beijing sees Taiwan as part of China to be reunited by force if necessary. Most countries, including the US, do not recognise Taiwan as an independent state, but Washington is opposed to any attempt to take the self-ruled island by force and is committed to supplying it with weapons.

While a major conflict would inevitably add pressure on countries to choose sides, Hoo said Malaysia would make every effort to stay out of it, prioritising protecting and evacuating its citizens instead.

She argued that Malaysia’s existing defence relationship with Washington gave both countries established channels to discuss concerns over US deployments and regional escalation, rather than addressing them through public political statements that could reflect “limited substantive bilateral dialogue”.

Over the years, Malaysia has maintained close defence ties with both Washington and Beijing.

Malaysia has taken part in joint drills with the US for more than three decades, including maritime exercises such as Cooperation Afloat Readiness and Training.

This July, nearly 2,850 Malaysian, US and Australian personnel took part in Exercise Keris Strike in Kota Belud, Sabah, involving tactical and live-fire drills aimed at strengthening interoperability.

In October 2025, Malaysian and Chinese forces held the Aman Youyi exercise in the Strait of Malacca, including maritime interdictions, search-and-rescue and surveillance drills between the Malaysian and Chinese navies.

Innocent passage?

Distancing itself from any conflict would require Malaysia to enforce its neutrality consistently against both sides, said Mohd Hazmi Mohd Rusli, a maritime law expert at the Islamic Science University of Malaysia.

Hazmi said Malaysia should also make its position clear in advance so both Washington and Beijing understood the limits of what it would allow.

“If a US-China conflict spills over near Sabah, Malaysia … should not allow its territory, territorial sea or airspace to be used by either side to conduct hostile military operations,” he said.

But Malaysia’s exclusive economic zone would present a different challenge, Hazmi said, as other states retained freedom of navigation and overflight under international law.

Foreign warships could still pass through Malaysia’s territorial waters during an armed conflict provided that their passage remained “innocent”, he added, referring to Article 19 of the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea.

Oh Ei Sun, principal adviser at the Pacific Research Centre of Malaysia, said the conundrum for Southeast Asian states would revolve around requests by rival powers for logistical support or access to facilities in the event of a conflict.

“Their acquiescence or refusal will be seen by either big power as alternately friendly or hostile,” Oh said.

Andrea Passeri, a programme director for international relations at Taylor’s University in Malaysia, said there was a “breaking point” where maintaining non-alignment would become difficult if the rivalry escalated into open conflict.

“Non-alignment would not vanish as a principle, but it would become much harder for Malaysia and many Southeast Asian states to sustain in practice,” Passeri said, adding that even states seeking autonomy could find their waters and airspace increasingly important for military access and surveillance.

The pressure could also expose internal fault lines within Asean, he said, with the Philippines pulled directly towards Washington while other members faced pressure to clarify positions they had deliberately kept ambiguous.

“Consensus becomes harder precisely when collective diplomacy is most needed,” Passeri added.

Such pressures could also deepen existing divisions within the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, whose 11 member states maintain vastly different relationships with Washington and Beijing.

“There are already Asean member states which are viewed as either very pro-US or very pro-China,” Oh said. “Their vastly different positions are likely to deepen should a conflict break out between the two big powers.”
