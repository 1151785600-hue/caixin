How China’s youth job crisis is driving graduates to German vocational apprenticeships

SCMP | FREE | 1119 words | 2026-09-07

Two years ago, Yuki Liu was a university student in the northern Chinese province of Hebei, studying traditional Chinese music and the pipa – a pear-shaped Chinese lute – weighing the usual paths considered by her peers: postgraduate degree or civil service exam.

Today, her day begins at 1am in Germany.

While many people sleep, she is already working as an apprentice at an industrial bakery in a small town outside Stuttgart in the country’s southwest, preparing the shop for the morning rush.

The turning point for Liu came as she approached graduation and grew increasingly unsure about the path her studies could offer her in China. “With a music degree, your only option is to teach,” she said. “Otherwise you starve.”

Liu came across Germany’s apprenticeship system almost by happenstance, discovering a path that the musician had never imagined: becoming a blue-collar worker in Europe’s largest economy.

Liu is among a growing number of young Chinese turning to Ausbildung, Germany’s dual vocational training system, as an alternative to an increasingly competitive graduate job market at home and as a potential route to staying in Europe. Many applicants, like her, already hold university degrees or have worked for well-known companies.

On Chinese social media, the pitch is straightforward: little upfront cost and the prospect of a job at the end. Apprentices are employed by a company in Germany and paid a training allowance while attending a state vocational school tuition-free. Most stay on as regular employees once they qualify.

Back in China, there is a popular cultural refrain that students and degree-holders often use to describe how hard it is to find a job: “graduation means unemployment”.

A record 12.7 million students graduated from Chinese universities this year, up 480,000 from 2025. Unemployment among people aged 16 to 24, excluding students, rose to 17.9 per cent in July – an 11-month high.

For some, Ausbildung – literally translated as “education”, “training” or “apprenticeship” – offers more than a pay cheque. It is also one of the cheaper routes to relocating abroad, particularly for those looking to escape a working culture epitomised by a notorious “996” schedule – working from 9am to 9pm, six days a week.

Social media has helped fuel the appeal. Liu recalled a video on Douyin – China’s version of TikTok – featuring a philosophy postgraduate student who had dropped out and moved to Germany to train as a gardener, listing what he found different about working there: no rat race, no overtime and no office politics. The video drew thousands of likes, with viewers asking in the comments how they could follow a similar path.

The financial incentives also blunt some reluctance towards blue-collar work. Workers in Germany with a recognised vocational qualification earned about €4,125 (US$4,800) a month before tax in April 2025, according to Germany’s Federal Statistical Office – roughly five times the average pay of production workers in China last year, data from the National Bureau of Statistics shows.

“You can really feel it: far more people are talking about it than before,” said Emilie Xie, a former media worker in China who is now applying to become a carer in Germany.

However, for some, reality often hits harder than the online pitch.

Lin Chenxi runs a youth hostel in Hamburg and helps place Chinese apprentices with German employers, supporting them after they arrive. “Only about a third of the students we placed made it through,” she said. “The other two-thirds either got weeded out or lost heart and left.”

The language, Lin said, can be the biggest hurdle. She explained how applicants need a B1 proficiency level in German – an intermediate stage in which they can handle everyday situations and communicate familiar topics – but many appear to have drilled their way through the German language exam in China, and they arrive unable to hold a conversation.

I feel like a screw on an assembly line

Yuki Liu

The promise of plentiful jobs can also be misleading. Germany lacked enough qualified workers to fill 369,500 skilled jobs in 2025, according to the German Economic Institute, with nursing among the hardest-hit professions. Some of the roles that are more accessible to Chinese applicants are said to include care work, baking and kitchen jobs.

“I feel like a screw on an assembly line,” Liu said, explaining how she spends about six hours every workday sorting deliveries and two hours making products. In nearly a year, she lamented, she had never taken a single item from raw ingredients to a finished loaf.

Apprentices spend about three years on a training allowance that averages €1,238 a month before tax, according to the Federal Statistical Office, with smaller firms typically paying less. Liu takes home €873, of which €680 goes towards rent. She said she is already considering her next restart, possibly by pursuing another apprenticeship.

Those realities may help explain the gap between the buzz on Chinese social media and the number actually taking the route. Germany counted 573 Chinese nationals in dual vocational training in 2024, up from 282 in 2020, according to the Federal Institute for Vocational Education and Training.

The figures exclude nursing and other school-based programmes that attract many more Chinese applicants. Still, the overall number remains a fraction of China’s outbound workforce.

For Emilie Xie, though, none of this has changed her mind. “A degree isn’t hard currency any more,” she said. “It has become consumption, not an investment.”

Xie graduated from Hengshui High School – famed for its gruelling exam drilling – and went on to study at a university in Beijing. It was a path once seen as a ticket to a comfortable career. She worked in media before deciding to retrain herself.

Xie said she deliberately chose care work: the sector is short-staffed, relatively easy to enter and – she believed – harder for artificial intelligence to replace. She said she knew the job meant changing adult diapers and cleaning up after people, but she said she was ready for it.

For his part, Willie Wei had an advantage many other students lacked: he studied German as an undergraduate before dropping out of a journalism master’s degree programme in Beijing. He was hired by a large care provider for disabled people in Saxony on a regular employee contract, rather than an apprentice wage, with 32 days of paid leave and what he said was one of the better salaries in the sector.

“Things are falling into place, step by step,” he said, revealing his plan to apply for German citizenship once he qualifies.

Even so, looking back on his journey through the Ausbildung system, Wei said he would have preferred to have taken an academic path through a university – a route that he said he still may consider in the future.
