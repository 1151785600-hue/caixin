China’s private firms are fighting for profits. Are regulations adding to the burden?

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1972 words | 2026-10-24

Plenary sessions of the Central Committee, the upper echelon of China’s ruling Communist Party, are major events on the country’s political calendar. This month’s edition, the fifth plenum, is attracting even more attention as the world’s second-largest economy takes on a more prominent role in global affairs. In the second part of this series, Emma Ma speaks to businesspeople as they adapt to stepped up regulatory enforcement and deal with other economic issues the government has pledged to solve.

Six years after China Evergrande Group started to show signs of insolvency – the beginning of a downward spiral that would eventually lead to the property developer’s bankruptcy and its founder sentenced to life in prison – Zhu Yinfen is still waiting to recover money the company owes to her Shanghai firm.

“Evergrande still owes us 500,000 yuan (US$74,885),” Zhu said, adding that the worst problem was not the unpaid debt, but the many other clients who routinely failed to settle their bills on time.

“I was quite anxious at first, but I’ve grown accustomed to it. Payment delays are rampant in the construction sector,” said Zhu, whose company supplies water-purification equipment for construction projects.

While government data shows China’s private sector accounts for about 80 per cent of urban employment and 90 per cent of new jobs, its performance has become increasingly uneven.

Though emerging industries such as technology and advanced manufacturing benefit from government support and attract investment, firms in traditional industries, including construction and manufacturing, have borne the brunt of an economic slowdown.

Delayed payments rank among the biggest concerns of Chinese entrepreneurs. At the same time, compliance costs are rising rapidly as tax authorities step up enforcement of rules and scrutinise the collection of social insurance premiums.

“Dented by weaker profit expectations and faltering investment confidence, private‑sector enterprises have stepped up deleveraging,” wrote Zhang Xiaojing, a researcher at Beijing-based National Institution for Finance and Development, in a July note.

The government has not ignored these issues. President Xi Jinping personally pledged to address arrears owed to private companies in a high-profile address to the country’s leading entrepreneurs in February 2025.

The central government has since rolled out measures designed to help businesses weather the storm. But some businesspeople said that while the policies appeared promising in theory, they remained challenging in practice.

Now, attention is turning to the fifth plenum of the Communist Party’s Central Committee, to be held from Monday to Wednesday. The committee will focus on “strictly governing the party,” including measures to enforce party discipline and combat corruption.

Xi has repeatedly said such efforts are needed to ensure Beijing’s policies, including those intended to benefit the private sector, are properly implemented.

Payment delays evolve

Wang Xianhui runs a medium-sized firm on the outskirts of Shanghai. It mainly works with local governments and major state-owned construction enterprises.

According to him, a 12‑month payment cycle is considered normal for the sector, yet delays stretching beyond that time frame can also occur. Before 2020, he said, receipt of payments typically took only three to six months.

“Some local governments simply cite audits as excuses. We have a project in a small city in [central China’s] Hubei province, and payments for it have been held up for a decade,” Wang added.

With the property market yet to recover from a prolonged slowdown catalysed by Evergrande’s default, the construction sector remains the epicentre of overdue payments. In the years since the slump, the problem has only evolved, industry insiders said.

Over the past two years, Zhu said she has watched the situation shift from payment delays to debt settlement via unsold flats.

“One of my clients is a major Shanghai-based developer. Starting in 2024, they began settling out contract obligations with unsold apartments in the suburban areas,” Zhu said.

She added that the properties were part of the developer’s unsold inventory, and the firm offset her construction payment at the original sales price – far higher than current market rates.

“Housing prices have kept falling over recent years. Local authorities set pricing for new apartments, and it is common for new-home prices to stand well above resale values – a 10 per cent to 20 per cent premium is typical in Shanghai,” Zhu said.

That means if Zhu took ownership of these flats and tried to sell them, she would incur losses. On top of that, her firm would also be required to cover transaction-related taxes.

“And it is not a choice between cash or property – it is either accept the apartment, or keep waiting,” she said. “It’s not limited to just one developer.”

According to China’s National Bureau of Statistics, total accounts receivable of China’s industrial enterprises above a designated size hit 29.48 trillion yuan (US$3.9 trillion) by the end of August, up 9 per cent year on year and outpacing the 6.6 per cent growth in revenue.

To address the issue, Beijing has reiterated that state-owned enterprises (SOEs) must settle payments in cash. At a mid-September press conference, an official with the State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission said cash payments to small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) “must be enforced as a hard rule”.

The government has also set up a website where SMEs can report delayed payments. Still, some private business owners said that while the initiative was well-intentioned, it lacked practical effectiveness.

“I can sense the government’s resolve to fix these issues through the website. But we would only file a complaint there if we no longer wanted to do business with those SOEs,” Wang said.

“Besides, our firm is a lower-tier contractor. That means even if we chose to submit a complaint via the website, we lack many key contracts and supporting documentation. The reality is far too complicated.”

Problems with delayed payments are not unique to China, however. In Europe, business-to-business transactions generally face a 60-day deadline for fulfilment. Late payments carry statutory interest set at least 8 percentage points above the European Central Bank’s reference rate.

Zhang Lin, deputy director at the research centre of Shanghai-based Far East Credit Rating, wrote in a May column for financial media outlet Caixin that Beijing could draw on Europe’s measures to impose tangible financial costs for payment delays.

“What awaits settlement is not merely individual invoices, but better liquidity and stronger confidence across the economy,” he said.

Paying in full

This summer, Shanghai entrepreneur Richard Zhu began ensuring full social security payments are made for his employees, a process he jokingly described as “window dressing”. He heads a medical-device contract manufacturer generating 20 million yuan in annual revenue.

Previously, his company had been remitting most social security contributions at the statutory minimum – more than 7,500 yuan a month for Shanghai as of this year – even though over half of his staff earned higher salaries.

At his firm, he said, workers in their 20s were willing to accept lower insurance premiums to boost their take-home cash. For his older, core employees, he has always made full social security contributions, he added, as they were mature enough to understand the value of social security benefits.

The informal practice is widespread among China’s SMEs as they deal with heavy operational burdens.

Combined employer and employee social insurance contributions in China can reach as high as 38 per cent of an individual’s wage, a higher share than in some developed economies, including the US at 15.3 per cent and Japan at 30 per cent.

Social security premiums have emerged as a significant source of public revenue. According to figures from the Ministry of Finance, national social insurance premiums hit 9.12 trillion yuan in value in 2025, outstripping the 6.89 trillion yuan in proceeds from value-added tax.

“I’ve heard of several larger firms getting calls from the tax authorities recently. Officials flagged mismatches between their social-security contribution bases and actual payroll, ordering them to get their social-security contributions into line with regulations,” Richard Zhu said.

Even though he has not received any notification from the local tax authority, he said he proceeded with the adjustments as a precautionary step – though he remains worried that non-compliance might derail his company’s partnerships with large pharmaceutical firms.

A professor with close ties to the State Taxation Administration, who requested anonymity over the sensitivity of the matter, said that enforcement has ramped up this year, and that provincial governments have established specific performance targets stipulating what proportion of local enterprises should achieve social security compliance.

After the change – made without reducing the pay of younger staff members to compensate – the combined outlay for social security contributions and the mandatory housing fund at Richard Zhu’s firm has surged to nearly 40 per cent of the expanding wage base, eating into the company’s profit margin.

He and others in the private sector are asking a similar question: why step up enforcement of premium collection when the economy is losing momentum?

“As China’s population is ageing at an accelerating pace, rigid expenditure pressures on the pension fund have risen steeply,” said Tony Peng, an independent researcher who has studied the domestic social security system for more than 10 years. “The domestic pension fund is confronted with heightened real-world pressures to balance its current-year revenues and outlays.”

Those pressures compound as local governments’ fiscal positions grow strained with less revenue from land sales, he added. In the past, fiscal resources have served as a backstop for social insurance accounts whenever premium collections came up short.

The fallout

Peng said he believed tightening enforcement was still the correct policy direction. However, he added, “with weak domestic demand and extremely thin profit margins among private firms, a tighter enforcement ramp‑up would exacerbate their difficulties and could trigger wage cuts or lay-offs.”

The repercussions already appear to be manifesting in the job market, insiders said.

Confronted with narrowing profit margins, Richard Zhu opted for the simplest solution – scaling back new hiring. This summer, he slashed his recruitment quota, dropping from eight positions for engineers and office-based staff to two.

Research from 2025 by Ye Jingjing, a professor at Zhongnan University of Economics and Law in Hubei province, found that heavier social security contributions led to a decline in new job postings.

Beginning in 2019, China’s tax authorities took over collection of social insurance premiums, an institutional shift that triggered an earlier round of tighter regulatory enforcement.

Ye’s research showed that average new job listings on one major domestic online recruitment platform dropped by 7.2 per cent in the post-2019 period relative to the 2017-19 baseline. At the same time, total social security contributions from surveyed companies climbed by 16.22 per cent.

“Conditions differ from one firm to another. Labour-intensive enterprises that carry relatively high wage-to-cost ratios are more vulnerable to changes in the social security contribution base,” Ye said.

Peng, the independent researcher, suggested that authorities carefully consider the pace of implementation and design of transitional arrangements.

He proposed that local governments provide rebates or interest subsidies for those firms that remit full social-security contributions and have avoided staff lay-offs. This kind of “targeted compensatory support” would help incentivise some enterprises to abide by the law more willingly, he said.

Richard Zhu, meanwhile, said he was choosing to stay positive.

“Having spent 23 years in this business, I’ve learned it’s all about cycles. There have been ups and downs. Perhaps in another five years, all these problems will be absorbed during an economic upturn.”
