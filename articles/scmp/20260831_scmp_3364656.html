Beyond Amsterdam: Amersfoort’s 15th century charm lies in its canals, cafes and tourist-free calm

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1798 words | 2026-08-31

It would be odd to visit the Netherlands without spending time in Amsterdam, the canal-laced city of slender, leaning houses in patterned brick with their toothy stepped gables, famous for its Rembrandts and Van Goghs, and for action-movie speedboat chases along its waterways and under narrow humpbacked bridges.

Some visitors are attracted less by this culture than by the city’s cheerful open-mindedness; sex workers on display in windows among everyday shopping in a central red-light district and marijuana that was available in cafes for decades before deregulation became fashionable anywhere else. It’s also pleasant just to take a canal-side bench with a lunch of pickled herring, stroopwafels, or chips with peanut-butter sauce, and watch the boats and bicycles go by.

However, faced with up to 20 million tourists a year, the city’s 950,000 residents would increasingly like you to consider alternatives, or at least to spread out a bit. It’s not as if the Netherlands’ culture and history stop dead at Amsterdam’s periphery. Within a short train ride (the only kind here; anything more will take you to Belgium or Germany), there are what seem like little bits of Amsterdam that have broken off and floated away. These also have centuries-old tottering houses, ancient churches, canals and the same generally welcoming atmosphere, but each with its own individual style.

Amersfoort, for example, was once made prosperous by beer and textiles, as well as its location on the navigable Amer river, later renamed the Eem, but now is a backwater half an hour away from Amsterdam by frequent train. It’s a concentrated and walkable pocket of Dutch culture ancient and modern, best viewed not as a checklist of sights but as a souvenir-shop-free town where ordinary life goes on in a historic setting. Wandering is a pleasure in itself.

From Amersfoort Centraal station, it’s a 15-minute walk to the largely pedestrianised Old Town and a landmark that can be seen from some distance away, the 98-metre (321 feet) Gothic Onze Lieve Vrouwetoren, or “Tower of Our Lady”. This solitary finger of reddish brick, built in the 15th century, its accompanying chapel long vanished, rises in steps that change to carved grey stone topped with a shining golden cockerel. From the top, if you can manage the 346-step climb, there are views over the town centre’s labyrinth of narrow streets. But there’s as much pleasure in relaxing with something restorative at a cafe, bar or restaurant in the small square at its base, taken while listening to the sound of the bells reverberating around the ancient facades as the tower’s clock strikes the quarter-hour, the half-hour or the hour.

In one corner of the square, the 16th century Kapelhuis (“Chapel House”) is a reminder of when Amersfoort was a major destination for pilgrims, attracted by a supposedly miracle-working stone statue of the Virgin Mary that once perched here. But the continuing survival of the seemingly flimsy stepped frontage, its brickwork in herringbone and circular decorative patterns, seems miracle enough.

A little further down the narrow Lieve Vrouwestraat is antiquity of a more domestic nature, in a pair of equally diminutive half-timbered houses that also date from the 16th century, framed in stridently red-painted wood, with dark green shutters.

Carry on and the road swings to the left to follow the route of the original city wall along a parade of further ancient housing of particular bricky dignity, walls bulging in some places and studded with the plates of the tie beams holding the buildings together, large ground-floor windows opening onto the thin streets. Dormers with hoists suggest the impossibility of getting furniture up or down steep interior stairs. Many are muurhuizen, or “wall houses”, built in the 15th and 16th centuries on the route of the 13th century city wall using some of its remains. The expanding town had replaced the earlier defences with a larger ring, some of whose impressively fortified water gates still stand.

These narrower streets intersect those of regular shopping – shoes, a pharmacy, a bustling bookshop with plenty in English – and cross short and sturdy bridges over tree-lined canals that reflect the ancient frontages alongside. Wandering visitors may happen upon the small, covered 17th century fish market, an 18th century synagogue or fragments of the original city wall. And if they are in need of a souvenir, there’s the Mondriaanhuis.

Amersfoort may be short on Rembrandts or Van Goghs, but the town was the birthplace of Pieter Cornelis Mondriaan, more famously known as Piet Mondrian (1872-1944), the master of a grid-like abstraction whose style was picked up and absorbed into the fashion of Saint Laurent and Moschino. The house in which the painter was born shows nine earlier figurative pieces that only hint at the abstraction that was to come, although there are many works by contemporaries clearly influenced by the artist’s later work. A shop sells attractively “Mondrian-ised” merchandise.

The finest wall house is the Groot Tinnenburg, which was built before 1414. It was part of the original city wall and connected to a substantial water gate now replaced by a simpler bridge. Still privately owned, and currently up for sale (yours for a rumoured €2.65 million/US$3.08 million), its great mass, towering stepped gables, smart red shutters and location right on a canal make it perhaps Amersfoort’s most photogenic spot, although various water gates compete for that title, too.

The gate once attached to the Groot Tinnenburg was rendered redundant by the construction of the Monnikendam, on the second wall, here close to the route of the first. Both protected the water supply and defended the city from waterborne assault where several streams combine to form the Eem.

A blank-faced bridge, its flatness relieved only by arrow slits, the Monnikendam connects two squat brick towers capped with witches’ hats of grey slate, the canal passing under an arch low enough to make standing boatmen duck.

Walk anticlockwise from the Groot Tinnenburg along the outer bank of a city-circling canal, this section known as the Zuidsingel (“South Canal”), and you’ll discover that several wall houses have long gardens reached rather romantically by private bridges.

A clockwise stroll takes you past a small cobbled bridge leading to the Thieves’ Tower, part of a wall house that functioned as a prison from 1434 to 1889, and on to the Kamperbinnenpoort, the only gate from the first city wall to have been preserved, at least partially. Its tall towers, standing over the high road entrance, seem like a version of the Monnikendam that’s been over-fertilised and has bolted skywards.

The carving of a windmill and assorted greenery over the ornately framed entrance to a stolid 19th century building here, De Snuif Molen (“Tobacco Mill”; now an Asian grocery store), is a reminder of a later source of revenue for the town. And continuing round to the north, the white-painted Prattenburg house, with a tall, dark-red octagonal tower poking from a steep-sided roof, was once the home and warehouse of a Jewish tobacco dealer.

Turning south towards the town centre quickly brings you to the Kanunnikenhuisje, another pocket-sized Gothic brick frontage, from 1520, once home to the priests of the Sint-Joriskerk (“St George’s Church”) opposite. It’s now home to the Tante Cor cafe, with seats outside from which to watch people pass by on their way to market while sipping the Dutch version of coffee with extra milk – the koffie verkeerd, or “wrong coffee”.

The medieval Sint-Joriskerk is on a different scale from anything seen since the Tower of Our Lady, with triple steep roofs running its length over nave and aisles, having expanded over time to absorb its formerly external spire-topped tower, which now shoots up unexpectedly from within one roof. There’s carved stone around the entrances but the whole is otherwise domesticated by being constructed of the same materials as the rest of the town, and even more so by having had businesses glue themselves to the church’s outer wall on all sides.

These include the rectangular Butter Market hall, which opens onto De Hof square and is supplemented at weekends by a cluster of stalls, all family bustle and with nothing made for tourists. It offers the aromas of fresh baking, waffles and other hot snacks wrapped in gingham paper, and vast wheels of orange cheese.

Examining the facades around the square reveals signs of historical trade; the painted head of a helmeted figure with gaping mouth that protrudes from one of them suggests this was long the site of a pharmacy.

Proceeding northwest down Havik, following the canal of the same name along the city’s north side, you’ll pass substantial former merchants’ houses to arrive at the Museum Amersfoort. Reached across the canal by its own little bridge, the museum occupies three former wall houses in striking striped brick, linked to form a labyrinth of small spaces containing displays that tell Amersfoort’s history. Within are early landscapes of the city when its first wall was intact, a table model of its historic layout and local treasures such as the keys to the city that French emperor Napoleon Bonaparte was too busy to accept when he passed through in 1811.

The museum explains the city’s relationship with water, the Eem having been used not only for shipping but also to provide fresh water, vital for both drinking and the development of the brewing and cotton-cloth industries that financed the town’s growth. But the only river that rises within the Netherlands, to drain into the ocean not far to the west, presents something of a threat to Amersfoort’s future, providing a channel for flooding as sea levels rise, and if dykes fail.

The best exit from the Old Town is through the nearby Koppelpoort, the most substantial of the city’s remaining gates, uniquely dominating both land and water approaches to the town. Built around 1425, it’s a string of octagonal towers and connecting buildings frowning forbiddingly over roadway and waterway alike.

From here, the railway station is a 15-minute walk away, but by now, it’s clear that Amersfoort isn’t just a side trip from Amsterdam; it’s an ideal base from which to explore the larger city and the rest of the region. Its central location, also on a major railway junction, makes access to the rest of the Netherlands easy. For now, it’s quiet and relaxed. The accommodation is relatively cheap and likely to become more competitive still as Amsterdam places further limits on short-term holiday rentals.

Perhaps the day-tripping should be done the other way.
