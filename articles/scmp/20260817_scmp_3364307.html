South Korea on edge over Trump’s call to cut military drills, goodwill for Kim

SCMP | FREE | 1269 words | 2026-08-17

US President Donald Trump’s call to “substantially” reduce US-South Korea military drills has revived speculation that he is trying to persuade North Korea’s leader Kim Jong-un to attend another summit between them even though Kim has expressed no such interest.

Trump’s comments in a social media post on Sunday came as South Korea and the US began their annual Ulchi Freedom Shield exercise on Monday, putting Seoul in an awkward position of defending the alliance while not undercutting his latest overture to Kim, according to analysts.

“Based on my very good relationship with Kim Jong-un of North Korea, I am not happy with the fact that the United States has, long ago, agreed to participate in Joint Military Exercises with South Korea,” he wrote on Truth Social, calling the drills “costly” and a “totally inappropriate and hostile” signal to North Korea.

Analysts say Trump appears to desire talks with Kim again and give him a foreign policy win. But they see little prospect of a breakthrough given North Korea’s growing alignment with Russia and China, its nuclear ambitions and its distrust of Washington after the failed Hanoi summit in February 2019.

In the same post, Trump said he had also asked South Korean President Lee Jae Myung whether Seoul would like to join the effort to denuclearise Iran, and was told: “No thanks.”

On Saturday, Trump posted a photo on social media of him meeting Kim at the inter-Korean border village of Panmunjom in June 2019, repeating his claim that he and Kim “get along great”.

In June, Trump also posted photos from his first meeting with Kim in Singapore in June 2018, their second summit in Hanoi and their Panmunjom meeting, fuelling speculation about his desire to rekindle ties with the North Korean leader.

Pyongyang has long condemned the Ulchi drills as a rehearsal for war.

The current 11-day joint exercises are a core part of the allies’ deterrence posture on the Korean peninsula, where about 28,500 US troops are stationed.

A South Korean defence ministry spokesman said the exercises were proceeding as scheduled.

Yang Moo-jin, a professor at the University of North Korean Studies, urged South Korea to consolidate its alliance with Washington while pursuing balanced diplomacy with China, Russia and other major powers.

“South Korea should maintain its role as a peacemaker and pacesetter in efforts for the settlement of peace, while making clear that it will safeguard and take the lead in determining its own destiny,” he said.

Old playbook

Trump has previously used the suspension of US-South Korean military exercises as part of his diplomatic outreach to Kim, including after their summit in Singapore. At the time, he abruptly announced that the US would halt the drills, saying the move would save Washington “a tremendous amount of money”.

The White House has also repeatedly said in recent months that Trump remains open to dialogue with Pyongyang “without any preconditions”.

Cho Han-bum, a senior analyst at the Korea Institute for National Unification, said: “President Trump appears to be setting the stage for a new reality show in diplomacy to divert attention from the war in the Middle East.”

Another summit with Kim would be a diplomatic coup for Trump, who has failed to deliver on his promises to avoid a prolonged Middle Eastern conflict and quickly end the war between Russia and Ukraine, Cho said.

Sydney Seiler, a senior adviser at the Centre for Strategic and International Studies in Washington, said Trump could have crafted the statement as a move to “lure” Kim back to the negotiating table.

Trump’s previous move to cut military drills with South Korea helped elicit a less aggressive posture from Pyongyang, including fewer long-range missile launches, a freeze on ICBM launches and a halt to additional nuclear testing, Seiler said.

But he added that the North had made clear it was not interested in talks about its nuclear programme and that it was unlikely to ease its nuclear ambitions.

“Expectations for substantive progress for diplomacy remain low,” Seiler said.

Arms-control talks with Washington on an equal footing as nuclear-armed states could be appealing to Pyongyang if Trump could be swayed about the matter, according to Seiler.

“Kim’s argument is clear: No one else accepts limits to their arsenals, why should the DPRK [North Korea]?”

Leif-Eric Easley, a political-science professor at Ewha Womans University in Seoul, said that downsizing the drills could damage the alliance and weaken deterrence against potential conflicts in Asia.

“The cost savings from downsizing drills are marginal, while the diplomatic benefits for relations with North Korea are doubtful,” he said.

Trump’s recent social media posts suggest his willingness to re-engage with Kim, but North Korea is “busy profiting” from Russia’s war against Ukraine as it modernises its military, according to Easley.

“The Trump administration’s ongoing difficulties negotiating with Iran do not enhance its credibility with the Kim regime, which doubled down on nuclear weapons development after the collapse of the 2019 Trump-Kim summit in Hanoi,” he added.

Doo Jin-ho, director of the Eurasia Research Centre at the Korea Research Institute for National Strategy, said Trump’s call to scale back the drills could also be a conciliatory gesture to encourage China and Russia to play a more constructive role to end the war in the Middle East.

Following North Korea’s deployment of troops to Russia and the resulting changes in Pyongyang’s strategic position, relations between Pyongyang and Washington could no longer be salvaged at the bilateral level alone, he said.

Pyongyang could keep supporting Moscow, including through additional troop deployments, to increase its strategic value and bargaining power with the end of the Ukraine war in mind, Doo said.

“This could help North Korea secure a more favourable position in any future negotiations with Washington,” he added.

Victor Cha, Korea chair at the Centre for Strategic and International Studies, said Kim might be open to a meeting with Trump, potentially after a US midterm election loss for the party in power, to reaffirm the tenets of the 2018 Singapore summit agreement.

That agreement included Pyongyang’s commitment to work towards complete denuclearisation of the Korean peninsula, “new” bilateral relations and a lasting peace regime.

“Kim might push the conversation towards peace treaties, stopping US military exercises, and ending the hostile state of relations between the United States and North Korea, appealing to Trump’s resume-building for the Nobel Peace Prize,” Cha said.

Lim Eul-chul, a professor at the Institute for Far Eastern Studies at Kyungnam University, was more sceptical, saying Kim remained “deeply scarred” by the collapse of the Hanoi summit and had little reason to expect meaningful gains from another meeting with Trump.

“North Korea’s most important national priority is not reaching a diplomatic settlement with an unreliable United States but completing the modernisation and advancement of its nuclear forces,” he said.

Dr Zack Cooper, a senior fellow at the American Enterprise Institute, called Trump’s move a “remarkably irresponsible decision”.

“It decreases US-South Korea deterrence and defence capabilities while getting nothing in return. And it will undermine Seoul’s confidence in Washington, which will raise the prospects of South Korea acquiring an independent nuclear capability,” Cooper told This Week in Asia.

“President Trump walked away from his deal with Kim Jong-un in his first term because North Korea refused to give up its nuclear weapons. This reality has not changed. So any summit between Trump and Kim is likely to be just as fruitless as their first set of meetings.”
