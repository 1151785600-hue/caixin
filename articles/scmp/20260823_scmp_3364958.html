Licence to savour: Hong Kong craft brewers push for on-site beer permits

SCMP | FREE | 1012 words | 2026-08-23

When a 100-strong South Korean tour group approached a Hong Kong craft beer manufacturer for a tour and tasting session, the operator declined their request.

The refusal was not down to a lack of confidence in the product or a staff shortage. It was regulatory: the brewery simply lacked a licence to serve its beer on the premises.

“It is not easy doing manufacturing in Hong Kong,” said Luke Yardley, founder of local craft beer brewery Yardley Brothers.

“It is just a missed opportunity when in every other country – if you are in London, New York, Dublin – customers can go for a day out and visit the brewery. They get to have a nice experience where they can consume alcohol on site. And the key thing is they can buy alcohol on site,” he added.

Yardley is not alone. Many of Hong Kong’s craft beer manufacturers operate in industrial buildings under food factory licences to house their giant equipment and save costs, which makes applying for liquor licences difficult.

The situation has prompted calls for a review of the licensing regime governed by the Food and Environmental Hygiene Department (FEHD) to allow them to offer some samples for customers to taste on-site, which industry leaders and a lawmaker have said may help drive the city’s tourism economy.

Hong Kong has around 15 home-grown craft breweries producing more than 30 distinct local beer brands, with some being exported for international consumption.

In Hong Kong, catering operators have to obtain liquor licences from the Liquor Licensing Board to serve alcohol, which are usually granted to licensed restaurants and clubhouses with compliance certificates issued by the home affairs minister.

For premises without such documentation, their applications for liquor licences have to undergo extra scrutiny from other government arms, including the Planning Department, Fire Services Department and buildings authorities, leading to lengthier approval times.

Yardley has been running his brewery for 10 years, and the factory in Kwai Chung now produces around 15,000 litres of beer per month.

He said Hong Kong was home to a few top beer makers in Asia, but the regulatory hurdles made it difficult for producers to promote themselves “confidently”.

He called for a review on the current licensing regime, and urged authorities to explore the feasibility of introducing a dedicated liquor licence for breweries, allowing them to offer tasting samples to visitors, which he said might also attract more tourists to Hong Kong.

“A concern [could be] having it out of control where you have nightclubs in industrial buildings and that is not what we are proposing,” he said.

Also expressing similar frustration was Christopher Wong See-wai, head brewer and a co-founder of Heroes Beer in Yau Tong, who said it was a pity that all household beer brands in Hong Kong come from overseas.

“The reason why microbreweries in other countries could win support from their neighbourhood community, expanding their influence to a broader area, was through residents’ visits to learn what they were doing,” said Wong, who is also the co-founder of a few other local breweries.

“The biggest problem in Hong Kong is that people do not even know we exist.”

For a niche brand such as Heroes, which specialises in “experimental beer” and produces around 40,000 litres of beer per year, building a reputation to rival established names like Blue Girl, San Miguel and Carlsberg is difficult without local community engagement, according to Wong.

“There are many luxury homes in Yau Tong. If I have a licence, people can come to my brewery, sit down, have a cup of beer after work, and join a tour to learn more about us,” he said.

Also a restaurant operator himself, Wong said many breweries were able to meet the conditions for obtaining liquor licences. He questioned the government’s reluctance to expand the licensing regime to them, when there were already many retail and even gym operators in industrial buildings.

Lawmaker Adrian Pedro Ho King-hong said breweries in Hong Kong were facing “massive challenges” in running tasting tours because of the licensing regime as well as land-use restrictions.

He agreed that authorities should explore introducing a dedicated “taproom licence” with certain restrictive conditions such as their size and capacity to avoid turning factories into entertainment venues, under which licensees could lead visitors to tour the production premises and distribute small samples.

“Introducing a taproom licence regime can on the one hand enhance the international publicity of Hong Kong brands, and on the other hand, it is good for the tourism industry as many visitors would like to tour our local breweries,” he said.

During a recent public consultation session for Hong Kong’s first home-grown five-year plan, Chief Executive John Lee Ka-chiu acknowledged that the city needed globally recognised local brands and pledged to provide policy support.

Ho said he would meet authorities to discuss the matter after collecting information and opinions from the industry, but cautioned that progress required cross-departmental collaboration.

Even if the FEHD approved serving alcohol on-site at breweries, there could be further restrictions on land use stipulated by authorities and the Deeds of Mutual Covenant governed by the buildings’ incorporated owners, he added.

“It takes time and I think we need a starting point in promoting industrial building revitalisation,” he said.

In response to the South China Morning Post’s inquiry, the FEHD stressed that operations of commercial or retail activities in industrial buildings should also comply with other statutory requirements, such as zoning plans, government lease conditions and building and fire safety legislation.

“The FEHD will continue to work closely with relevant policy bureaus and departments to examine feasible options that support local food trade while upholding regulatory standards and community interests,” it said.

The Development Bureau said catering services were permissible in wholesale converted industrial buildings under its revitalisation scheme.

It added that it was reviewing the scheme and would explore the possibility of widening non-industrial uses in non-wholesale converted industrial buildings, taking into account emerging market needs and interdepartmental feedback.
