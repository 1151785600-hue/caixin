Is China’s Shenzhen ready to host Xi, Trump and Putin during Apec summit?

SCMP | FREE | 1008 words | 2026-10-17

In Shenzhen, drones are banned, shiny new hotels have opened their doors and taxi drivers are carrying devices that can translate nearly 150 languages.

Since President Xi Jinping announced last year that China’s southern tech hub would host Apec meetings in November, the city has done all it can to get ready under the watchful eyes of the local, provincial and national authorities.

While every Apec summit draws the world’s attention, this year’s edition is considered to be especially high-stakes as Xi, US President Donald Trump and Russian President Vladimir Putin are all expected to attend. Last year, Putin did not join the summit in South Korea.

In the past few weeks alone, Guangdong’s provincial Communist Party chief Huang Kunming has inspected Shenzhen twice to make sure preparations are going as planned.

“Shenzhen must, on one hand, maintain focus on details to comprehensively plug any gaps, optimise and refine service guarantees, and go all out to ensure the conference is held safely, smoothly and successfully. On the other hand, it must take the hosting of the Apec meeting as a new starting point to accelerate the expansion of high-level opening up,” he said last week.

Compared with Shanghai and Beijing, which hosted Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summits in 2001 and 2014, respectively, Shenzhen is less internationally known. However, analysts say the city could benefit from the event in the long term.

When Xi made the announcement, he noted that Shenzhen had “transformed from a backward fishing village into a modern international metropolis in just a few decades”.

“Together with surrounding areas, it forms the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macau Greater Bay Area, which can be considered a growth pole of the world economy,” he added.

This narrative emphasised Shenzhen’s alignment with the meeting’s agenda, said Zhang Jingjia, an economist at the Apec Study Centre at Nankai University. China has made “openness, innovation and cooperation” priorities for the summit, and Shenzhen was a living example of that, she said.

When Beijing and Shanghai held the meetings, they focused on diplomacy and international finance, said Han Yonghui, executive dean of the Guangdong Institute for International Strategies. But Shenzhen’s tech companies, manufacturing system, ports and border crossings could show how the digital economy can function in real-world scenarios, he added.

Hosting an inter-governmental meeting would test a city’s organisational abilities, Han said. It required proper venues, hotels, ports, railways, telecommunication networks and security, as well as diplomatic coordination, multilingual services, volunteers and community support, he added.

The city has made security its top priority. Since July, local police have imposed a strict no-fly zone covering the city centre and stepped up random checks of subway passengers.

A statement from the provincial public security bureau said at the time that hidden conflicts and risks should be detected, while street crimes – including thefts and robberies – needed to be “completely eradicated”.

“Ensuring Apec safety is a major political responsibility for police around the province,” the statement said. “We need to plan early … and act with a heavy fist.”

Special teams have also been equipped to patrol the region. Shenzhen police have formed a low-altitude drone unit to patrol more than 600 routes and reach any point in the city centre within three minutes, authorities said.

This week, the government also called for residents to take leave or work from home to reduce traffic during the summit.

The venue for the summit – the Shenzhen International Exchange Centre in Futian district – was built especially for the event. In July, it hosted the Apec Forestry Ministerial Meeting, which Zhang described as a “test run” for the leaders’ meetings.

Shenzhen previously hosted major exhibitions, including the China Hi-Tech Fair, the International Cultural Industry Fair and the China International Optoelectronic Exposition, which showed that the city had the capacity to host large-scale meetings, said Peng Peng, executive chairman of the Guangdong Society of Reform, a prominent think tank affiliated with the provincial government.

According to the Shenzhen authorities, the city has focused on upgrading its airport, crossings and railway, including this week’s opening of the revamped Huanggang Port that connects the city to Hong Kong.

Language is also a concern. More bilingual signs have been erected in the airport and bus stops, while taxi drivers have been issued with translation devices able to handle 146 languages.

Downtown Shenzhen’s Huaqiangbei – the world’s largest electronics marketplace – was offering language training sessions to vendors, and multilingual volunteers were stationed in community service centres across the city, a report on the local government website said in August.

According to media reports, parks, gyms and commercial centres have also been renovated. More than 1,500 training sessions were organised for officials, business owners, delivery riders, residents and volunteers on languages, diplomatic etiquette, business English and public service.

“In addition, Shenzhen’s tech enterprises and industrial achievements are themselves content that can be showcased to all parties during the meeting, which is a feature other cities are unlikely to have,” Zhang said.

Shenzhen could benefit from hosting this meeting, just like Beijing and Shanghai did, analysts said. After hosting Apec, Beijing’s suburban Huairou district developed a whole industry around international exhibitions and hosted several more global summits, Zhang added, while Shanghai saw greater development in tourism and commerce.

Shenzhen’s residents are mostly happy to see parks revamped and other infrastructure improved, but are also looking forward to the return of normality.

A local doctor said she and her colleagues were placed on call for the duration of the summit to handle potential emergencies, and all leave was prohibited. But she said she “welcomes and supports” the city hosting a meeting of such importance for the first time.

“For ordinary people, what’s most useful isn’t the buzz during the meetings, but the public services and environment that stay,” Zhang said.

She added that the new exhibition centre could host public events, while the summit would raise the city’s international profile and foster global cooperation – ultimately driving investment and job growth.
