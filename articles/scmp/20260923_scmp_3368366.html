Indonesia’s UN Security Council bid tests non-aligned diplomacy

SCMP | FREE | 1005 words | 2026-09-23

Indonesia’s bid for another term on the United Nations’ premier security body stands a good chance of success given Jakarta’s growing global engagement, analysts say, but its balancing act between competing powers could complicate efforts to speak for the Global South.

Jakarta’s Foreign Ministry said on Thursday that it would launch its campaign this week for a fifth term as a non-permanent member of the United Nations Security Council for 2029–2030.

The ministry said it was seeking the seat to represent the priorities of developing countries at the UN’s most powerful body and that its campaign would focus on peacekeeping, preventive diplomacy, conflict prevention and civilian protection.

“Indonesia aims to act as a bridge builder, establishing common ground, maintaining dialogue within the council and seeking practical solutions. We will also bring the voice and priorities of the Global South,” Foreign Ministry spokeswoman Yvonne Mewengkang told reporters on Thursday.

“Indonesia [also] wants a Security Council that delivers results. It might sound overly ambitious, but why not? That is precisely what Indonesia aims to bring: an effective and responsive Security Council,” she added.

The council has 15 members: five permanent members with veto powers – China, France, Russia, Britain and the United States – and 10 non-permanent members elected for two-year terms.

Indonesia has previously served four terms on the council: 1973–1974, 1995–1996, 2007–2008 and 2019–2021.

Rifki Dermawan, an international relations lecturer at Universitas Andalas in Padang, West Sumatra, said the country’s bid stood a good chance considering its active global engagement under President Prabowo Subianto.

“Popularity does matter,” he said, adding that securing the seat could help strengthen Prabowo’s goal of making Indonesia an active contributor to the international community.

Since taking office in October 2024, Prabowo has visited nearly 30 countries and attended a number of high-profile summits to position Jakarta as an active mediator and independent middle power.

Apart from joining Brics as its first Southeast Asian member, Prabowo has actively advocated for the Global South at international forums such as the G20, emphasising fairness and strategic autonomy for developing countries.

Radityo Dharmaputra, a foreign-policy analyst and lecturer at Surabaya’s Airlangga University, said the odds of a fifth term on the council “are in Indonesia’s favour”, adding that Jakarta was one of the most consistent contributors to UN peacekeeping troops.

He said Indonesia was expected to push hard for Palestinian statehood, humanitarian access and for any stabilisation mission to remain anchored in a UN mandate rather than in a US-led framework.

“On Ukraine, I would expect Indonesia to present itself as a bridge builder calling for a ceasefire and dialogue,” Radityo added.

However, Indonesia will still need to mount a strong diplomatic campaign, taking lessons from the Philippines’ defeat by Kyrgyzstan last June. Manila lost to Bishkek for a non-permanent seat by a wide margin of 142 to 49 votes – a loss partly resulting from Manila’s treaty alliance with the US, which worked against it among non-aligned nations.

Yohanes Sulaiman, an associate international relations professor at Jenderal Achmad Yani University, said that while Indonesia stood a good chance of getting a seat, the UN might look to consider other countries. He pointed out that former Indonesian foreign minister Retno Marsudi was appointed UN special envoy on water in November 2024.

The Security Council bid also reflected the “personal ambition” of Prabowo, who had often sought high-profile arenas to “showcase his leadership”, Yohanes added.

A voice for the Global South?

Rifki said a seat on the council might not automatically make Indonesia the voice of the Global South due to Jakarta’s mixed positions on several international issues.

“Jakarta’s external approaches under Prabowo have been confusing for many Indonesians. Many have criticised his unclear direction in exercising foreign policies,” he said.

“How can Indonesia support the freedom of Palestine while sitting side-by-side with Israel on the ‘Board of Peace’, a problematic organisation led by [US President Donald] Trump?”

Indonesia strongly supports Palestinian independence and a two-state solution, maintaining that it will only formally recognise Israel as a sovereign state once Palestinian statehood is established.

In January, Indonesia joined Trump’s “Board of Peace” to oversee post-war governance, reconstruction and demilitarisation of the Gaza Strip, but Prabowo later suspended his country’s participation.

Rifki said that Indonesia’s ability to exert influence on behalf of the Global South would also be constrained by the “privileges of veto power” held by the five permanent members of the council.

“To make the council more effective, reform is a must,” Rifki said.

Critics have long called for reform of the council as its permanent members, in place since 1945, each wield a veto power that often paralyses its ability to resolve international conflicts and crises.

Apart from sharp disagreements among nations on how to expand the council, amending the UN Charter governing its make-up would require a two-thirds majority in the UN General Assembly as well as ratification by two-thirds of member states – including all five permanent members of the council.

Radityo said that other countries might see Indonesia as a “fence-sitter” or a “hypocrite” for championing Palestine’s statehood while remaining neutral on Ukraine. Indonesia had consistently framed the Ukraine war “as a tragedy to be ended through dialogue rather than as an act of aggression”, he said.

Abstaining from votes would be harder to hide on a 15-member council than in the General Assembly, Radityo said.

“So ambiguity becomes much harder to maintain,” he said, adding that Indonesia could contribute credibly as a non-Western, non-aligned voice in humanitarian areas – such as helping with prisoner exchanges, returning deported Ukrainian children and ensuring nuclear safety in war zones like Zaporizhzhia.

“By 2029, the council may be dealing with monitoring a ceasefire rather than the war itself,” Radityo said, adding that Indonesia’s peacekeeping experience would serve it well should this outcome come to pass.

For Prabowo, winning the council seat would indicate “to the world and to the domestic audience that Indonesia is respected”, he added.
