Hong Kong’s 700-year-old oyster industry goes hi-tech with ‘super’ specimen

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 3334 words | 2026-10-31

Out on Deep Bay, between Hong Kong and Shenzhen, the first thing you notice is what doesn’t seem to belong there.

Many of the oyster-farming rafts here are old. Weathered wood, greyed by salt and sun – the kind that has floated in these waters for generations. A few look abandoned, but this is inevitably the appearance a 700-year-old industry takes on when it is running out of time.

And then, there are the newcomers – bobbing buoyantly in a bright, unmistakable shade of blue – made of fibreglass. Pacing back and forth on one in a pink shirt and dark trousers is Professor Thiyagarajan Vengatesen, a marine biologist from the University of Hong Kong (HKU) who is better known as Professor Rajan.

Born in India’s Tamil Nadu state, Vengatesen came to Hong Kong in 2001 to take up seven years of advanced research into larval biology at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology. His interests lay in how oyster larvae were struggling with environmental adaptation and building their shells at a time of increased ocean acidification.

Vengatesen’s mentor at the time predicted that oysters would become vulnerable to climate change, but the gravity of the situation only sank in when he witnessed it first-hand. After Vengatesen joined HKU, in 2008, a colleague connected him with an oyster expert, who took him to Zhanjiang, in Guangdong province, in 2009, and showed him an industry suffering without scientific involvement. Vengatesen became one of the first scientists in the region to study how climate change would affect oyster farming, but meeting the growers in Zhanjiang – seeing how their livelihoods depended on a species they could not protect – convinced him this was worth a life’s work.

Little did he know he would spend the next decade and counting looking for a solution to save Hong Kong’s oysters.

Lau Fau Shan sits on the northwestern edge of the New Territories, a rural neighbourhood of narrow strips of small businesses and stalls pressed between the hills and the bay. Its main street smells of dried seafood, salt and the mustiness of oyster shells left in the sun. Stacks of prized golden oysters – semi-dried bivalves, with a soft texture – line the shopfronts, along with bottles of oyster sauce and oyster-shell crafts.

Oyster cultivation is part of Lau Fau Shan’s cultural, historical and economic tapestry. Abutting Deep Bay, this area has been farmed for these molluscs for centuries, the local species itself named after the city: Magallana hongkongensis – the Hong Kong oyster.

In the 1960s and 70s, this area was the heart of Hong Kong’s booming oyster-farming industry. Hundreds of families worked the rafts through the “off-bottom” aquaculture technique, growing oysters on ropes or in suspended cages, with the resulting products being shipped across the region and the world. Lau Fau Shan’s seafood restaurants drew crowds from across the city. Feasting on a banquet here was a status symbol.

Walk the alleyways of the neighbourhood today and ask who farms oysters, and the answer is almost always the same: “My father used to”, “My grandfather used to” or, occasionally, “I used to”.

But oysters are serious business, and not just for Lau Fau Shan growers. Hong Kong-based food giant Lee Kum Kee, founded nearly 140 years ago, built its brand on oyster sauce as an indispensable pantry staple. Beyond mere ingredient, the bivalve represents identity and tradition. So influential is Lee Kum Kee’s flagship condiment as a cornerstone of Cantonese cuisine that the craftsmanship that goes into making it was inscribed as an intangible cultural heritage of Guangdong province in 2022. For a story that began with oyster sauce in 1888 and has since spread to more than 100 countries and regions globally, Hong Kong’s declining oyster industry represents a loss not just for producers, but for a unique food culture.

Most of the oyster products sold in Lau Fau Shan’s shops now come from across the water, grown in mainland China and shipped to the town that popularised Hong Kong oysters as a trade. The Hong Kong industry is still around, but mostly as a memory. A statue of an oyster overlooks the rafts at the end of the main street, its paint washed in the sun.

Even before climate change took its toll, the industry was in decline, beset by an ageing workforce, outdated methods and young people leaving.

And then, the oysters started dying.

Chan Kwok-leung remembers the winter of 2007, when the water in Deep Bay did not get cold enough. “Brother Leung” had grown up in the 70s and 80s, when the winter water was “cold as melted snow”. The chill helped the oysters build up their flesh, leaving them plump and sweet in time for the Chinese New Year market. It was how it had always worked.

But that year, almost all of the oysters withered.

“I felt so lost,” he says. “In an instant, a whole year’s livelihood was gone.”

With his crop devastated, Brother Leung left the industry and went to work in construction, saving until he could afford to buy new oyster spats and start again the following year. It was not the last time his oysters would die.

Historically, oyster rafts in Deep Bay tended to cluster to the east of the Shenzhen Bay Bridge, in zones that are further from the ocean, with moderate salinity levels. Hong Kong oysters are adapted to these estuarine conditions, to the brackish area where the Pearl River meets the sea, growing for centuries in water that is neither fully fresh nor fully salty.

But over the years, more growers moved in, triggered in part by Shenzhen’s ban on oyster aquaculture, in the early 2000s. Rafts crowded Deep Bay, numbering around 5,000 by the mid-2010s, and most of the new arrivals were to the west of the Shenzhen Bay Bridge, in saltier water.

Oysters struggle to survive prolonged periods of high salinity. As they expend energy trying to balance the salt in their bodies, less is left for growth.

Climate change only made it worse.

“Warmer winter conditions now last more than 15 days longer than usual,” Vengatesen explains. Prolonged warmth disrupts the oysters’ biological clock, prompting them to prepare for reproduction earlier, diverting energy from building up their flesh to developing their gonads. It is a fatal combination: poor meat quality, then death.

But figuring out how science could help was only half the battle. When Vengatesen first started visiting Lau Fau Shan, the farmers were wary and kept their distance. Vengatesen is an Indian scientist who could not speak Cantonese, while the locals had generations of experience and a lunar calendar that had steered them for centuries. What could this man possibly know about their oysters?

Unconvinced, the farmers would not let him near their rafts. Only licensed boats are allowed out to the oysters, and the growers control access.

Vengatesen recalls the time he tried to swim out to the rafts: “I wasn’t successful, but it showed them that I was serious about wanting to help.”

Personal scepticism was only one obstacle. Negative public image was another concern. By the late 2000s, local media reports of bacterial contamination and heavy metals had eroded consumer confidence in Hong Kong-grown oysters. In 2013, the Shenzhen government threatened to ban sales of the shellfish as unfit for consumption.

“Local oysters have a limited market,” says Vengatesen. “People still perceive them as contaminated”; the stigma “has to be broken down scientifically”.

Brother Leung, meanwhile, kept an open mind and said yes to Vengatesen’s involvement in 2010. He had returned to Lau Fau Shan after the 2007 die-off, and knew the old ways no longer worked. “I was one of the first oyster growers to talk to Rajan,” he says. “Also, because I was one of only a few who could speak English.” (He had picked up the language during stints working in the seafood industry in Fiji and Australia.)

In 2016, something shifted. A South China Morning Post article covered Vengatesen’s collaboration with the growers – “the first positive report in a very long time”, he calls it. It outlined plans to revive Lau Fau Shan’s oyster industry, with scientists assisting in its recovery, a purification system in the works and a hatchery on the horizon.

While the story did not solve everything, it changed the atmosphere. As Vengatesen puts it, it made things easier for him.

First, Vengatesen had to address food safety. Farmers needed a way to prove their oysters were safe to eat. He helped them secure a government loan to establish Hong Kong’s first commercial depuration plant in Lau Fau Shan. Opened in 2018, the plant is said to have the capacity to process 10,000 oysters a day. At the facility, harvested oysters sit for up to four days in seawater sterilised by ultraviolet light to reduce bacteria levels to meet safety standards – though the government still advises cooking local oysters before eating.

Chan Shu-fung – a third-generation oyster farmer and the chairman of the Deep Bay Oyster Cultivation Association – invested more than HK$1 million (US$127,430) of his own money in the project. His depurated golden oysters are stocked in City’super and Sogo, the first locally farmed oysters to reach Hong Kong’s high-end supermarkets. He has become one of the industry’s most vocal advocates, as he is familiar with the difficulties of operating in this field. Running the depuration plant costs, on average, four to five times more than ordinary oyster production. He now primarily powers it up based on orders he receives, rather than on a regular basis.

Next problem: the oysters themselves. Because the local species could not adapt to the rapidly changing climate, Vengatesen set out to breed a hardier version that could survive. Vengatesen and his team at HKU developed a genomic tool – the SNP (single-nucleotide polymorphism) chip – that allows them to scan an oyster’s genetic material for markers linked to stress tolerance, disease resistance and growth. Instead of waiting years to see which oysters would survive in current conditions, they could identify the strongest ones in the larvae stage, breed them and repeat the process to further refine the selection for the ultimate stock.

“It used to be like a slow, careful family-tree project,” says Vengatesen, describing the old strategy. “Now, we have a short cut: we look at the oyster’s DNA – its instruction manual – to scan for tiny markers that are like ‘good-luck charms’ in the genes, which tell us which oysters have [already developed] natural protection against sickness and stress.”

Each oyster is given a rating based on how many “good” markers it has, and the team then picks the highest-scoring individuals and conditions them to spawn.

Finally, after years of trials and tests, came the result: the HKU Super Oyster, bred by Vengatesen and his team, is a climate-change-tolerant strain of Magallana hongkongensis tough enough to survive the mass mortalities that wipe out regular oysters.

Vengatesen made significant breakthroughs in the lab, but scaling up production and testing the oysters under real-world conditions would require the right facilities and support. Enter the Hong Kong Oyster Hatchery and Innovation Research Unit, the first such hub in the city dedicated to developing sustainable aquaculture solutions. Opened in 2022, the HKU Oyster Hatchery – established through funding from Lee Kum Kee and the Sustainable Fisheries Development Fund – cultivates resilient HKU Super Oyster seed stock and supplies them to local growers.

For Lee Kum Kee, backing the project was a clear-cut decision.

“It gives us an opportunity to contribute to the long-term sustainability of an industry that is closely linked to our own history,” says Clarence Chung Shiu-cheong, director of technical and procurement at Lee Kum Kee.

As a business that is deeply rooted in “oysters and the communities behind them”, he says, “the challenges faced by local farmers” affect not just the producers, but the legacy of local food culture.

“Preserving Hong Kong’s oyster-farming traditions is about much more than preserving an industry. It is about preserving generations of knowledge, craftsmanship and a way of life that forms part of Hong Kong’s unique food heritage, built over generations through the experience and dedication of local oyster farmers,” Chung explains.

“It is important not only to respect that heritage, but also to help create the conditions for it to continue.”

A sea of blue rafts out in Deep Bay is what that safeguarding work looks like today – physical proof that the bivalves Vengatesen has bred in a Petri dish can survive in the same waters that have been killing their ancestors. One man believed in Hong Kong’s oysters when the city had written them off as a sunset industry.

Vengatesen is also developing a triploid version – oysters with three sets of chromosomes instead of two. “With that extra set, the cells get confused and can’t divide properly to make sperm or eggs,” he explains. “So, these oysters give up on reproduction altogether. Instead of pouring 80 per cent of their body into making babies, they put all their energy into growing meaty bodies, year-round. And they handle heat and stress better.”

Millions of seeds are produced per year at the HKU Oyster Hatchery, and the blue rafts are home to the first field test for Vengatesen’s triploids, estimated to be ready by next summer.

In the summer of 2025, Brother Leung and other farmers stocked more than 30,000 ropes with HKU Super Oyster seeds, which will take 18 months to mature. In Zhanjiang’s high-salinity waters, where regular oysters tend to die, the survival rate of this new strain was 40 to 50 per cent, he had been told. In Deep Bay, they hope for a similar outcome this winter.

“We were very surprised by this,” says Brother Leung of the Zhanjiang experiments. “We truly believe that Professor Rajan’s research and the HKU Super Oysters may become our lifeline in the near future.”

“When oysters can thrive despite these [environmental] pressures, it strengthens the entire industry and helps preserve Hong Kong’s oyster-farming heritage for future generations,” adds Chung.

Besides, the science isn’t simply making a positive impact on people – the benefits also ripple out to the broader marine ecosystem. “Oyster reefs provide habitat and shelter for fish and other marine life, and they play a role in maintaining the biodiversity of coastal waters,” he says.

Whether the HKU Super Oysters will be introduced into large-scale production lines, such as at Lee Kum Kee, remains to be seen. First, the science and cultivation practices must be allowed to mature, Chung asserts, “but, naturally, as a company whose heritage began with oyster sauce, we would be pleased to see these efforts one day come full circle”.

In the meantime, Brother Leung is already requesting more HKU Super Oyster seedlings for the next round. He and his fellow farmers are exploring the possibility of building their own hatchery, so they can produce the seeds themselves.

What Vengatesen has achieved has also reshaped the industry’s governance. For years, the number of rafts in Deep Bay had grown unchecked, reaching more than 14,000, according to a survey conducted by the Hong Kong government in May 2023. After his research identified overcrowding as a contributing cause of mass mortality among local oysters, the government commissioned him to evaluate raft management. He recommended cutting the total by nearly half.

“We strongly support the government’s proposal to reduce the number of oyster rafts to 7,000,” says Chan. “Professor Rajan’s research helps us understand what a reasonable number of rafts should be in Deep Bay.”

A new management framework was introduced, beginning with a registration system in 2023 and moving towards a licensing structure, overseen by a committee drawn from the industry itself.

Hong Kong’s Tang clan has held farming rights in Deep Bay since 1667, when the imperial government granted them a deed to rent out the oyster beds to farmers. For centuries, anyone wanting to cultivate oysters along the Lau Fau Shan coast paid rent to the Tang pioneer settlers, and later to the Tang ancestral hall, the Yau Kung Tong. Under the new licensing system, the age-old organisation is taking on a formal management role. “It is functioning like a property-management company,” says Tang Yu-kei, the association’s general manager.

He, too, is a convert. Having witnessed how the old method has failed, with fewer farmers and dwindling incomes, he sees the new approach as offering the clan a more sustainable role than collecting rent from a trade in decline. “I don’t fully understand the details, but we’re beginning to believe in science,” he says.

Vengatesen’s ambitions go further still, extending beyond the bay: he wants to build a “Made in Hong Kong” oyster brand, one that can compete with the major mainland producers. With seeds bred in the city, oysters grown in Hong Kong waters and processing and purification carried out locally, the brand would be owned and exported from here. Right now, most farmers still buy their spats from the mainland. Vengatesen wants the whole chain to be local.

“Oyster farming in Hong Kong carries over 700 years of history,” he says, “and this deep-rooted heritage must be preserved and leveraged. Hong Kong’s golden oysters are a unique speciality that has already become a recognised local brand.”

Hong Kong’s reputation for quality and trust, he argues, is an asset that cannot be replicated. “Why not build on this reputation to promote Hong Kong oyster cultivation and exports under a ‘Made in Hong Kong’ label?”

Vengatesen also believes that the brand cannot be built by scientists and farmers alone. It will only work if Hongkongers themselves start believing again, if they stop reaching for imported oysters and start asking for local ones.

For Deep Bay oysters to succeed, “we have to change their image in the eyes of Hong Kong people”, says Chan. Part of that is answering a simpler question: can these new oysters taste like the old ones?

At a Lau Fau Shan cha chaan teng, an oyster omelette arrives, its edges crispy and golden. A local classic served for generations, this version is made with HKU Super Oysters, freshly shucked by Brother Leung and brought to a diner that usually sells imported molluscs from the mainland. Under the expectant eyes of the scientist and the farmer, I take a bite: the HKU Super Oyster tastes almost identical to the regular kind, but is slightly less rubbery, with a distinct, metallic flavour.

All in all, there is nothing remarkable about it – and, for an industry trying to prove it can still compete, that might be precisely the point. Vengatesen’s HKU Super Oyster does not need to be a revelation. It needs to survive the winters that killed its predecessors, grow fat in water that once starved them and taste like something a Hong Kong family would be happy to order.

It will take until next summer for the triploid, the next generation, to be ready. Tang has his doubts about those. “We’ve only encountered triploids from the Pacific Ocean,” he says. “I’ve personally eaten them and I didn’t like them. But if it’s triploids from Hong Kong oysters, it could be promising.”

Vengatesen offers a practical take. While some in the industry worry that the triploids will taste worse than wild oysters, quality aside, he says, they need the triploid to survive – because “almost all of [the farmers] are jobless in the summer”. More than just tasty seafood, it is a lifeline for those who still depend on the industry to put food on their own table.

In the waters between Hong Kong and Shenzhen, tradition is learning to believe in science. Although the verdict is still out on what comes next, for the first time in years, there might be something worth believing in.
