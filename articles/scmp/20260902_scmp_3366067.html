EU trade enforcers descend on Beijing demanding China fix ‘untenable’ imbalances

SCMP | FREE | 1197 words | 2026-09-02

A delegation from the European Commission has been locked in talks with Chinese officials in Beijing, as the sides look to find a diplomatic off-ramp from a looming trade war before an October deadline set by Brussels.

The EU team was spearheaded by Denis Redonnet, the bloc’s top trade enforcement officer, who was recently made point person on China trade policy – a recognition that in EU circles, talk of partnership has largely been usurped by rivalry and competition.

Later this month, the European Union’s top trade official, Ditte Juul Jorgensen, will follow Redonnet to the Chinese capital, she confirmed on Wednesday, as Brussels assesses whether Beijing is willing to make the sort of concessions that could see them avoid sliding into a damaging tit-for-tat exchange in the autumn.

“We see an engaged China in these talks, but of course the challenges are very, very significant,” Jorgensen told a hearing at the European Parliament on Wednesday.

The commission wants Beijing to commit to reducing its surging exports to the bloc, which have expanded at breakneck speed in recent months. In July alone, China extended its trade surplus over all but three EU members compared with a year earlier.

“What we see in relation to China is … increasing concentration dependencies, including in strategic sectors – that is a risk to the EU,” Jorgensen said.

“We see that as a result of a number of Chinese domestic policies, but of course also of China’s competitiveness in a number of these sectors, we have an increasing trade deficit,” she added, describing it as an “untenable situation”.

Brussels suspects that the exports are turbo-boosted by undeclared subsidies, an undervalued currency, and rampant industrial overcapacity caused by weak domestic demand, years of investment-led growth and state support that has kept production expanding even as consumption has lagged.

It fears that these factors propel products to European shores at prices that are undercutting local producers and that could, if left unchecked, deindustrialise swathes of the European economy.

“We are working with very concrete proposals. Let’s see how our Chinese counterparts would react to them … But if simply there will be no action, then of course it will be much more difficult for us just to talk about these issues,” EU Trade Commissioner Maros Sefcovic told Politico on Monday.

But in the Belgian capital, expectations are well managed as to what can be achieved before October, when it must decide what to do. The face-to-face meetings followed a stream of online contacts through August, but these sessions produced no breakthroughs.

While Beijing has made conciliatory noises around reducing its gaping trade surplus with the EU, it is thought to prefer to do this by buying more European goods. Such a dynamic would resemble, in part, what US President Donald Trump agreed with China during his first term – the “phase one” trade deal that analysts say did little to address structural problems in the relationship.

“These ongoing talks are not just talks – they have to give a proof of concept by October. We have been very clear that these global imbalances that exist cannot continue and there has to be a rebalancing,” Jorgensen said.

The Beijing meetings are expected to inform how tough European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen talks on China during her State of the Union speech on September 16, which will in turn help set the agenda for a meeting between Sefcovic and Chinese counterpart Wang Wentao, planned for the second week of October.

In the past, von der Leyen has used her annual address to announce investigations into subsidies in China’s electric vehicle sector, a ban on products made using forced labour, and a rival to Beijing’s core infrastructure drive, the Belt and Road Initiative.

There is speculation that if negotiations do not make progress, she could announce new instruments or investigations. Jorgensen confirmed on Wednesday that the bloc was developing a “diversification instrument”, which could compel companies in critical sectors to increase their number of suppliers, but added that it was still “early days” in the tool’s development.

“That is a consideration relevant for climate, relevant for the energy transition, relevant for our economic security, and the need to rebalance. To be clear, it is that degree of concentration, that degree of dependence on any one supplier – whoever that supplier is – that is in and of itself a risk,” Jorgensen said.

During a speech last week von der Leyen warned that “being a partner [with China] does not mean accepting permanent imbalances”, noting that while Chinese imports into the EU had risen 45 per cent in five years, “our exports to China are falling”.

“We have seen that this dependence can be used as leverage against us. We must therefore ensure that fair conditions are respected. Dialogue with China remains necessary, but it must produce results. And when dialogue is not enough, we must be ready to make full use of our instruments,” von der Leyen said at the annual conference of France’s main employers’ federation, MEDEF.

Her State of the Union remarks later this month will help set the tone for a debate between the bloc’s national leaders in Brussels in mid-October, when they are expected to bless defensive measures against Chinese goods. Already, influential member states have been laying the groundwork for this scenario.

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz, who has flip-flopped dramatically on China policy since coming to office, appears to have come closer to Brussels’ thinking in recent weeks. Asked last week whether he backed car industry calls for EU tariffs on Chinese plug-in hybrid electric vehicles, Merz said he had instructed his cabinet to come up with proposals.

“I have asked the Federal Cabinet to now quickly gather and document all proposals that we could consider in terms of appropriate responses. We note that German industry has also apparently changed its opinion regarding these global imbalances. We will not only address this issue, but we will make decisions,” Merz said after a cabinet retreat.

At the same press conference, Vice-Chancellor and Finance Minister Lars Klingbeil said that “when we see that others are not playing by the rules, we cannot be naive”.

“The issue now is protecting the steel industry, the automotive industry, the chemical industry and the pharmaceutical industry, both in Germany and in Europe,” Klingbeil said.

“We had a very serious debate about where we need to improve in order to reposition ourselves economically, but also about how we face competition, especially the increasingly unfair competition we are experiencing. Therefore, we agreed – the chancellor confirmed this – that we will discuss appropriate measures in the cabinet soon.”

Also last week, an advisory board to the Dutch government urged The Hague to back tougher measures on China, or see industry hollowed out.

“Doing nothing in this situation therefore also comes at a high price. Our dependencies will increase and we will be hit hard economically. A strong response also comes at a price,” read the report from Wetenschappelijke Raad voor het Regeringsbeleid, an independent advisory council.

“The longer we wait, the greater the pain will be in the future.”
