ArchipelaGoGo! The Inter-Island Festival is back to celebrate Hong Kong’s more laid-back lifestyles

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2317 words | 2026-11-05

What do the curators and organisers of the 2026 Inter-Island Festival have in common? All three of them – Yip Kai-chun, Chan Chun-kit and Gloria Furness – traded life in urban Hong Kong for one of the city’s outlying islands for similar reasons: to be close to nature, experience a sense of community and enjoy a quiet lifestyle.

However, they got more than they bargained for: connection. Peng Chau specifically “has a lot of people from the creative industries who move out [of the city] and start over”, says Furness, the newer resident out of the three. It’s how the compact land-mass, measuring less than one sq km, became ground zero for the Inter-Island Festival, a month-long event celebrating the coastal societies on Hong Kong’s outlying islands through exhibitions, workshops, installations and talks.

Founded in 2019, the Inter-Island Festival takes inspiration from the inter-island ferry (“cross-water ferry”) connections between the outlying islands of Cheung Chau and Peng Chau and Lantau’s Chi Ma Wan and Mui Wo. Its first iteration, in 2021, came to fruition through the efforts of more than 50 collaborators, who put together 40-plus tours, workshops, exhibitions, performances and screenings, attracting more than 10,000 visitors to experience island culture.

After a three-year hiatus, the Inter-Island Festival finally makes its return for a third edition, taking place on Cheung Chau, Lantau and Peng Chau across five consecutive weekends, from November 1 to 29. Yip and Chan are among the founding members, while Furness joins the team for the first time after previous involvement as a volunteer coordinator.

When asked how the trio got together, they point to the very connection already present among the creative communities of the outlying islands. “I feel like everybody knows everybody,” says Yip, an independent artist and curator who has organised other events on the island. It was Chan, a co-founder of social enterprise and research lab Islanders and a long-time resident of Peng Chau, who introduced Yip to life on the island, leading the latter to eventually make the move from Sham Shui Po.

Furness became acquainted with Chan and Yip through various arts and cultural gatherings, and they invited her to join the Inter-Island Festival team this year to “deepen their collaboration” and leverage her experience in events planning and curation, gained from working with the biennial Hong Kong International Photo Festival exhibition and Koon Man Space, a photography-resource centre in Tsuen Wan.

“We all have similar backgrounds and networks,” says Furness. “We moved at various times [to Peng Chau] from the same circle, which is also part of our lore now.”

Yip conceived the idea for the Inter-Island Festival after moving to Peng Chau in 2017 and learning of the ferry network, little known to non-residents. “We mostly think of Victoria Harbour when riding the ferry [in Hong Kong], but the inter-island ferry is different. You see unfamiliar mountains and waters; that’s very special,” he says.

It sparked something in him, encouraging him to explore more of the city’s outlying islands.

Moreover, learning of the Lantau Tomorrow Vision project, a government land-reclamation initiative formally proposed in 2018, with the goal of building artificial islands off Lantau near Kau Yi Chau and Hei Ling Chau, further ignited his fervour, as the anticipated marine-habitat destruction and carbon emissions were expected to cause irreparable harm and “irreversibly change” the treasured environment of the islands. “Maybe a festival [about the islands] will create a bond between each of the ports that will be affected by the construction” and demonstrate their need to be preserved, Yip decided.

Dr Michael Rivera, a biological anthropologist at the University of Hong Kong who grew up in Mui Wo, shares that recent urban developments and events that “highlight traditional island culture” have also accelerated the desire for preservation. Largely, Hong Kong’s outlying islands are “marketed as tourist destinations”, he says. “But, at the same time, that [creates] an urgency, where people on those islands or who care about those islands feel that they need to share and conserve the narratives, livelihood and memories that are in these places.”

Conceived as a response, the Inter-Island Festival becomes a physical anchor to the stories of the islands’ inhabitants – such as the kaito captain, surnamed Wong, and his team, who bring Peng Chau residents across the water, and a fish-farm owner, Mo Lo, who commutes from Mui Wo to his farm in Chi Ma Wan – in living form. Between the islands, the connections are felt through daily life: a Peng Chau resident hikes in Chi Ma Wan, goes to the beach in Cheung Chau and dines in the cooked-food market in Mui Wo. Yip, Chan and Furness, together with local curators, exhibitors and artists, preach the gospel of their islands as a self-sustaining network of people and culture rather than being dependent on the city.

But it wasn’t smooth sailing upon launch, despite the evident passion driving the project. Yip admits that “the first edition was a pain”. Originally scheduled for 2020, the Inter-Island Festival was postponed three times due to Covid-19 social-distancing measures. In the weeks leading up to the event, there was concern that “attracting more visitors to the islands could overwhelm the community and inconvenience the residents” during the pandemic. After multiple delays, they decided to host it anyway, over three days on the last weekend of March 2021, focusing on the art, tradition, nature and environments of the four locations. Yip even organised an open house showcasing his life as an islander and his artworks.

A reprise followed in November 2023, with an expanded agenda taking place across four weekends. As part of the programme, visitors could listen to the sounds of the islands’ non-human neighbours – a running river, the chirping of birds and a buffalo bathing – and examine an installation designed as a home for bees. Peng Chau resident Jiming Lu, who serves as a local curator and coordinator for the upcoming edition, held two guided tours, one of Peng Chau cinema and the other of the island.

It took longer than expected for the curator-organisers to come back with the next instalment. “We really needed to take a break after the last edition, because it was quite overwhelming. It’s a lot of work,” says Chan.

Instead, they used the time to reflect on how to improve the event. One change that Yip, Chan and Furness are making this year is to “centralise” the festival on a different island each weekend, rather than host programmes simultaneously across all four, as it was done in the past, to help reduce the inter-island travel strain on visitors during their day trips.

Another challenge is timetabling. Entirely volunteer-run, the Inter-Island Festival is “really a bottom-up initiative, so we have to organise it according to everyone’s schedule”, says Furness. Plus, the islands are going through post-pandemic changes. “People are developing new creative spaces”, which take time to be incorporated into the event. In a way, the economic and structural development of the islands – such as the opening of new shops and businesses, adding another layer of self-reliance for the islanders who choose to live outside urban Hong Kong – and the festival have a “symbiotic relationship”. Even as a co-founder, Chan still encounters something new each time he visits the other islands to meet people who can represent the communities and business owners. “I’m still discovering more about these islands, including Peng Chau, through the festival.”

Next month, the Inter-Island Festival will include local co-curators and coordinators who reside in or are deeply familiar with Cheung Chau, Chi Ma Wan and Mui Wo, as the core team members all live on Peng Chau.

“We [want to] give more curatorial power to the local [collaborators] and allow our neighbours to be more involved within the umbrella of the festival,” says Yip. Compared with other public revitalisation projects, the Inter-Island Festival focuses on uplifting residents. “We want them to realise how precious island life is” and not take it for granted, he adds.

Centred on the theme “ArchipelaGoGo!”, this year’s edition will explore what living together as a cluster of islands looks like in 2026 through the lens of inter-island mythologies and the tidal beings that live on, around and under the ocean. Feature Projects, which pair researchers with creative practitioners over a six-month period to develop works around a specific theme, will include a literature scholar from Lamma Island working with a dancer, a woodworker partnering with a bamboo artist and a marine paleobiologist teaming up with two ceramic artists.

Moreover, the festival team selected 20 Micro Projects and 20 self-initiated Satellite Projects from the open call. Some of these include a hands-on workshop using oyster shells as a palette to create textured artwork in Peng Chau; a photography exhibition on the Cheung Chau Bun Festival; a community walk of Mui Wo to map out the neighbourhood through smell, touch and sound; and a tour across the mudflats in Pui O, which are home to snails, mangroves and fiddler crabs. Local businesses will also organise their own activities and discounts during the Inter-Island Festival.

Recruiting on-the-ground expertise is essential to fully represent the diversity of these islands. The four communities at the heart of the Inter-Island Festival each have their own distinct culture and rich tradition, though they share deep historical roots tied to maritime trade, fishing and, eventually, integration through the inter-island ferry network.

Peng Chau’s past can be traced back to the Neolithic period; a substantial fishing community called the island home, drawn by its sheltered bays and freshwater sources. In the 1960s, Peng Chau became a manufacturing powerhouse, with more than 30 types of factories, including the Great China Match Factory, once Southeast Asia’s largest producer of matches.

Mui Wo’s history has been recorded as far back as the Southern Song dynasty (1127-1279), when fleeing imperial court officials sought refuge from the invading Mongols in the area in 1277. Mui Wo’s silver mine, used to extract galena, operated until the end of the 19th century. In the 50s, the community blossomed as a farming hub, focusing on rice and vegetables.

Chi Ma Wan, a bay on southeastern Lantau, was a melting pot for Hong Kong’s early settlers, including the Hakka, Hoklo, Punti and Tanka. Farming and fishing dominated the peninsula, and its Shap Long Reservoir was once the main water source for Cheung Chau, Peng Chau and Hei Ling Chau. Later, camps housing Vietnamese refugees were built there.

Cheung Chau flourished as a fishing and trading port during the early Ming dynasty (1368-1644). Given its propensity for attracting mariners, it’s little wonder early-19th century pirate Cheung Po-tsai chose to hide his loot in one of the caves there – or so the legend goes. Nowadays, it’s the annual Cheung Chau Bun Festival, celebrating Pak Tai, the Taoist water deity and protector of fishermen and seafarers, that sees travellers scrambling to the island.

“We have always been a maritime society, reliant on coastal resources and seafood to survive. About a century ago, the population really expanded when more and more waves of boatpeople moved to these islands,” says Rivera. With the inception of the inter-island ferry route, closer ties between the islands were established, allowing them to have a connected, archipelagic relationship, rather than exist as isolated places.

But just as the islands have evolved into the coastal societies they are today, there has also been a shift in the way people see them, particularly during and after the Covid-19 pandemic.

In 2021, homes on Hong Kong’s outlying islands saw an almost 30 per cent increase in transactions, according to Midland Realty. While reports from the Census and Statistics Department do not detail urban-to-island migration trends, demographic data from last year reveals that the Islands district, which includes Cheung Chau, Mui Wo and Peng Chau, grew to about 196,000 residents. (Chi Ma Wan, while also part of the Islands district and once populated with villages and settlements, is largely uninhabited these days. Some descendants visit regularly but don’t “live” there year-round any more.)

“Part of what makes the [outlying] islands attractive is the cheaper rents,” says Rivera. “You can also expect a stronger network with your neighbours and a stronger sense of place.” He adds that, on the islands, “your entire space is a third space”, in that “your house is also part of the third space – there’s no barrier between them any more”.

In September 2025, the Hong Kong government shelved the Lantau Tomorrow Vision blueprint until further notice, but the mission of the Inter-Island Festival continues.

For Yip, Chan and Furness, living on Peng Chau is an exploration of how individuals can grow together, away from gentrification or “city mentality”, mirroring the connection, community, culture and creativity they hope to preserve with the Inter-Island Festival.

Furness believes that one can only fully understand the “islander” way of life through lived experiences. What might seem unusual to urbanites is second nature to them. By simply looking out the window, “I can tell the time based on which boat is coming in”, she says. For Yip, he can guess the typhoon signal by observing how the trees move in the wind.

Chan hopes that the Inter-Island Festival can grow to become like the Hungry Ghost Festival, where “people get together regardless of their backgrounds”. Different generations nurture their own traditions while still growing organically, much like the stories and lives on the islands. When you live the “island life” long enough, says Chan, “you start changing to its ways”.
