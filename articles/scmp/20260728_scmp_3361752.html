Apple’s biggest-ever leak happened in India. Will firms rethink their China relocations?

SCMP | FREE | 1382 words | 2026-07-28

When Apple set its sights on India as its next promising market, CEO Tim Cook planned to take a leaf out of the company’s China playbook – a retail and manufacturing strategy that had paid hefty dividends to the US tech giant.

“We are, in essence, taking what we learned in China years ago and how we scale to China and bringing that to bear,” Cook told investors in February 2023. The company had just seen record quarterly sales in the Indian market, and was just weeks ahead of the opening of Apple’s first stores in the country. “India is a hugely exciting market for us.”

At the time, Cook’s optimism in India also carried a degree of pragmatism. To navigate the rising trade tensions between the world’s two largest economies and avoid US tariffs on China-made goods – both the duties already in place and those likely to be imposed in the future – Apple had bet on India to become its next manufacturing hub.

India began to deliver. In 2022, the country produced around 6 per cent of all iPhones, but by 2026, that figure was expected to surpass 25 per cent, according to data from Counterpoint Research.

However, this year Cook’s vision faced a sobering reality check. In June, a massive cyberattack by hacking group World Leaks hit Tata Electronics, Apple’s Bengaluru-based partner for iPhone parts and assembly, leaking more than 200,000 files on the dark web. Analysts called the leak the biggest in the company’s history.

“India has proven it can scale production; now it must prove it can scale trust,” said Christopher Tang, a professor specialising in global supply chain management at the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA).

While India managed to ramp up labour-intensive assembly, attract major suppliers such as Foxconn and Tata, and leveraged government incentives, the incident revealed that the ecosystem still needed to catch up in three vital areas: information governance, supplier discipline, and cyber-operational integration, Tang noted.

“India can become a major global manufacturing hub,” Tang said. “But overtaking China as the world’s premier factory is a much higher bar.”

The breach may slow Apple’s willingness to transfer its most sensitive product data

Christopher Tang, UCLA

Made in India?

The files from the Tata leak included Apple’s component and supplier lists, as well as photos of the firm’s unreleased iPhone 18 Pro, exposing sensitive details of the chips on its main circuit board as well as hundreds of parts, according to Reuters.

Apple, Tata and the Indian authorities were reportedly investigating the incident. Neither company immediately responded to requests for comment.

“This leak is significant because Apple’s secret sauce that creates its competitive advantage depends not only on Apple’s own systems but also on every node in its manufacturing network,” Tang said.

“The breach may slow Apple’s willingness to transfer its most sensitive product data or highest-complexity product ramps too quickly to newer hubs,” he added.

This year’s leak was only the most recent test of India’s fast-expanding manufacturing capabilities. In 2024, Tata suffered a fire that led to a production halt at its facilities in Tamil Nadu, a state in the country’s south.

Despite the fallout, analysts said they did not expect the breach to diminish the importance of India in Apple’s ongoing efforts to diversify its global manufacturing.

“The current situation will be very much of a remediation-and-move-on situation, rather than an existential threat to Tata’s partnership with Apple,” said Tarun Pathak, research director at Counterpoint Research.

“Ultimately, we believe this doesn’t halt India’s momentum, but it will surely lead to an industry-wide re-evaluation” of the information technology infrastructure of electronic manufacturers, Pathak said.

Still, the breach cast a shadow over Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s “Make in India” initiative to transform India into a global manufacturing and innovation hub, challenging China’s dominance as the “world’s factory”.

The expansion of Apple’s manufacturing network in India helped drive the country’s broader industrial upgrade. In recent years, the US tech giant’s major suppliers, including Foxconn and Tata, have scaled up operations heavily across the country from Tamil Nadu to neighbouring state Karnataka.

Foxconn, also known as Hon Hai Precision Industry, injected another US$1.5 billion into its India unit last year.

However, Apple’s aggressive expansion plans in India also faced pressure from its home country. US President Donald Trump said publicly last May that he had asked Cook to stop building plants in India and step up production in America.

Beyond Apple, India has also attracted South Korea’s Samsung and Chinese handset makers including Xiaomi, Oppo and Vivo to build production bases there.

India has surged to become the world’s second-largest mobile phone maker by volume, with 99.2 per cent of its locally sold handsets produced inside the country, according to official data.

Just last week, New Delhi unveiled a new US$6.5 billion initiative, the Mobile Phone Manufacturing Scheme, to incentivise smartphone brands that produce their devices locally and source domestic components. The initiative targets US$460 billion in domestic mobile phone production over the next five years, with a significant increase in exports.

Hedging or diversifying?

India has been gaining an increasingly significant role in Apple’s supply chain. Last year, Cook revealed that most of the iPhones sold in the US were produced in India. In the meantime, almost all iPad, Mac, Apple Watch and AirPods products sold in the US market were made in Vietnam.

China, on the other hand, would continue to manufacture the vast majority of products sold in the rest of the world, according to Cook.

Apple’s geographic diversification efforts, known as the “China plus one” strategy, sought to shift away from total reliance on the Chinese manufacturing powerhouse amid geopolitical tensions and a supply bottleneck.

The diversification efforts accelerated during the Covid-19 pandemic, when virus control protocols in China dragged some of Apple’s product shipments to a halt at one point.

“For Apple, ‘China plus one’ is primarily a hedge against geopolitical risk, rather than a decision driven purely by commercial efficiency,” said Sun Chenghao, a fellow at Tsinghua University’s Centre for International Security and Strategy.

“This incident will not reverse its strategy, but it reminds Apple that diversification is not a simple copy-and-paste of the China playbook,” Sun said.

When talking about China’s manufacturing advantages, observers have usually focused on China’s low cost, efficiency and sheer scale, while underestimating its suppliers’ ‘soft power’ in commercial confidentiality, rapid response and systemic risk control, he added.

“Supply chain security is, in itself, a core manufacturing capability,” Sun said.

While Apple has previously faced product leaks in the form of unauthorised photos and smuggled parts from factory floors – including facilities in China – the scale of the recent cyber breach was unprecedented, according to analysts.

Every added node is an added point of vulnerability

Sun Chenghao, Tsinghua University

Sun noted that a leak of this magnitude was highly unlikely to hit long-established Chinese Apple suppliers, although the possibility could not be wholly dismissed.

“The key is not just firewalls; it’s the organisational discipline, tiered authorisation, physical isolation, supplier compliance auditing and a culture of secrecy forged over decades of collaboration,” Sun said.

A global supply chain alert

Analysts noted that the strategy, adopted to mitigate geopolitical risks, inadvertently exposed the supply chain to other risks.

“The more fragmented a supply chain becomes, the more a company must share blueprints, test data, supplier lists, quality standards and internal workflows across more touch points,” Sun said.

“Every added node is an added point of vulnerability.”

A source within Apple’s supply chain noted that cyberattacks are a universal threat, which is increasingly prevalent after the explosion of artificial intelligence technology.

“It’s not just Tata; all suppliers have been under attack in some way,” said the person, who asked to remain anonymous due to the sensitivity of the matter. “What happened to Tata sounded an alert across the supply chain globally.”

While India faces immediate scrutiny, experts stressed that cybersecurity required an ongoing arms race extending beyond any single company or region.

“No country or supplier ecosystem is immune,” UCLA’s Tang said.
