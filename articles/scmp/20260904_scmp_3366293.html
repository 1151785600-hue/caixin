Could Roman concrete have clues on how Pyramid of Giza was built – and saved?

SCMP | FREE | 1073 words | 2026-09-04

The enduring mystery of how the ancient Egyptians built the Great Pyramid of Giza – with its 2.3 million multi-tonne stone blocks – has puzzled researchers for centuries.

Mainstream archaeology holds that the pyramids were constructed from natural limestone or granite blocks precisely cut and hauled into place.

An alternative scientific theory suggests the blocks might have been cast from an early form of concrete, but that idea has long faced a critical question: how could such an artificial material survive for more than 4,500 years, while some modern buildings could suffer wear and damage in merely a few decades?

Now, a new study on nearly 2,000-year-old Roman concrete may provide a materials-science clue that breathes new life into the concrete hypothesis.

Published in the peer-reviewed academic journal Science Advances in July, the research reveals that ancient Roman concrete not only resists decay but actually grows stronger over centuries through a self-healing process.

A team led by Zhu Xiaohong of Beijing University of Technology, with collaborators from the University of California, Berkeley, the University of Michigan, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, analysed concrete taken from a communal latrine at Hadrian’s Villa in Tivoli, Italy.

Using high-resolution imaging, they discovered the material’s remarkable durability stems from the gradual formation of calcite crystals that fill pores and microcracks.

The healing process was driven in part by carbonation, in which calcium-rich compounds in concrete react with moisture and atmospheric carbon dioxide to form calcium carbonate.

Carbonation is often considered a durability problem in modern reinforced concrete because it lowers the material’s pH, potentially allowing embedded steel to corrode.

But the researchers found that the same chemical process can contribute to mineral growth and densification in ancient unreinforced concrete.

“For unreinforced concrete, carbonation may be a good thing,” Zhu said.

This insight could extend far beyond Rome. Zhu draws a speculative connection to the Pyramid of Giza, pointing out that ancient Egypt and Rome shared a deeper technological lineage than often assumed, including the use of lime or volcanic-ash-based binders.

If Egyptian builders used a calcium-rich, concrete-like mixture, the same sort of long-term mineral growth seen in Roman concrete might have transformed initially workable material into the dense, rock-hard blocks we see today.

“The builders of the pyramids may not have deliberately poured the hard stone blocks we see today, but may instead have discovered through experience a mixture that could ‘grow’ over time,” Zhu said.

He suggests that some calcium-rich building materials could have continued to evolve after construction through carbonation, mineral deposition and pore filling, producing increasingly dense structures over long periods.

Under this hypothesis, dense calcite crystals could form around older mineral phases and inside pores and fractures, potentially reinforcing the material while reducing pathways for water to penetrate.

Such mineral growth could continue for centuries, allowing parts of the material to become progressively denser, slowing further deterioration.

He compared the proposed mechanism with natural calcite formation, in which calcium dissolved in water can move through pores and fractures before precipitating again as crystalline material.

“In other words, time is not only an eroder; it can also be a sculptor,” Zhu said.

But Zhu stressed that he had not directly studied the Pyramid of Giza and his proposal did not establish that the pyramid blocks were made from concrete or artificial stone.

“This is only a reasonable hypothesis from the perspective of materials science,” he said.

Zhu’s judgment stemmed from research in the field of cement and cooperation with field authority Ian Richardson, of the University of Leeds.

“In fact, all ordinary Portland cement, as well as natural lime-clay or volcanic ash rich in calcium sources, undergo carbonation,” he said.

Portland cement is the most common type of cement used around the world.

“It has only been around for 200 years, yet you can see it almost anywhere on Earth. It is the second-most-used material by humans after water,” Zhu said.

“I and Professor Richardson have also studied the patented cement from 1824, where modern Portland cement was patented in Leeds in Britain.

“We analysed its production technology and composition, and the related research has been incorporated into textbooks on cement chemistry by Professor Richardson.”

The ancient Rome sample contained volcanic rock fragments, lime and volcanic ash materials commonly used in Roman concrete, but the researchers found that mineral growth within the material played an important role in maintaining its long-term integrity.

Over time, the continual growth of calcite creates a self-healing effect, closing cracks and mitigating further damage caused by environmental stresses or mechanical loads

Zhu Xiaohong, Beijing University of Technology

In particular, dense layers of calcite formed around older mineral phases and within pores and fractures, reinforcing the material while reducing pathways for water to penetrate.

Rather than occurring only during construction, this mineralisation could continue over long periods, filling voids and small cracks and making parts of the concrete progressively denser.

“Over time, the continual growth of calcite creates a self-healing effect, closing cracks and mitigating further damage caused by environmental stresses or mechanical loads,” the study’s authors wrote.

Zhu said the process occurred in a wide range of calcium-rich cementitious materials.

But whether carbonation was beneficial depended strongly on how the concrete was used. In ancient unreinforced concrete, carbonation could instead contribute to densification and long-term durability.

The Roman example could eventually inform the design of modern concrete by shifting attention away from initial strength towards long-term behaviour.

For the future cement design, “experiments on a thousand-year timescale are impossible, but with a three-dimensional validation system combining accelerated testing, numerical modelling and geological analogues, we can make credible predictions”, Zhu said.

The approach could allow researchers to engineer controlled mineralisation so that concrete responded to its environment by gradually filling pores and microcracks rather than simply deteriorating.

“For ordinary buildings, ecological design that is ‘sufficient and continuously sufficient’ is far more valuable than engineering that pursues extremely high strength without durability,” Zhu said.

He said he was continuing to examine historic construction materials, including samples from Hagia Sophia in Istanbul.

“Cement and concrete have always been rather remarkable and great materials, but people may overlook them because they are so common,” Zhu said. “Although it is not exactly a hot research topic, I feel that our research is quite meaningful.”
