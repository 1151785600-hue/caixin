Historian Wang Gungwu on US credibility and why Xi thinks ‘only the party can save China’

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 4640 words | 2026-08-16

Professor Wang Gungwu is regarded internationally as an authority on Chinese history. The 95-year-old is best known for his explorations of Chinese history in the long view, and for his writings on the Chinese diaspora. He has been a professor at the National University of Singapore since 2007 and emeritus professor at the Australian National University since 1988. He was vice-chancellor of the University of Hong Kong from 1986 to 1995.

This interview first appeared in SCMP Plus. For other interviews in the Open Questions series, click here.

The US-China rivalry is now quite prominent on the world stage. Do you think these two countries are facing the Thucydides Trap? How do you see their rivalry unfolding?

At one level, it is really similar to the United States and Soviet Union Cold War because both have nuclear weapons.

The question of having to fight one another and the possibility of using nuclear weapons makes it unlikely that they will fight each other. Fighting through proxies would be the same as during the Cold War.

I think military strategists would think that the way to deal with the situation when there’s US and China rivalry is to have another Cold War, and for the US to use the same methods that they used to destroy the Soviet Union.

They would destroy the China threat in the sense of undermining its power. If they can do that without fighting, that would be ideal because fighting is dangerous.

I think the strategists ever since the fall of the Soviet Union have decided that there’s no power out there that can threaten the United States. The only possible one was China in the long run.

They have been preparing for this for quite some time and have had arguments within the United States as to how to deal with China.

There are those who say that if you give China the chance to join the World Trade Organization, the Chinese economy will develop more like a capitalist economy. Then one day China will have a large middle class, no ideology to divide us. We can coexist and there’s no need to fight.

That view took precedence, and China was allowed to join the World Trade Organization. And the Chinese developed economically at a fantastic rate.

Some US analysts said that was the wrong decision because China is now strong enough to challenge the United States. How do you see the future US-China relationship?

What they discovered was that China was not like the Soviet Union. The Soviet Union had military power but was economically weak. One key reason was that they had no access to the oceans. The Baltic was blocked. So was the Black Sea. They never managed to develop an economy like that of western Europe.

The Chinese people were not only entrepreneurial but also had easy access to maritime trade. That has given them the capacity to fund military expansion. When the Russians tried to match the United States in their arms race, their economy collapsed. China can manage an arms race to defend itself if its economy continues to do well.

But I think the Chinese believe that they will never benefit from fighting the Americans. They just want to be secure from any aggression from the oceans.

In the past, China’s enemies had come only from the continent. But in the 19th century, the British and the French attacked from Southeast Asia where they had their naval bases.

The Americans joined in when they took the Philippines. When the whole of Southeast Asia was in the hands of foreign powers, the Chinese coasts were vulnerable.

That forced China to accept all those unequal treaties. But it taught the Chinese a lesson. They will never neglect maritime power again. They are now prepared for any threats by land, sea or air.

They are very concerned about attacks by sea. You can see how, in the South China Sea, their focus is on missiles that can deter powerful naval forces like aircraft carriers from getting near China. You can make a long list of what the Chinese have done. It is basically defensive to make sure that the Americans can never attack China from nearby.

As the US marks the 250th anniversary of its independence, some are asking if America’s golden days are over, especially after what has happened in Iran and the Strait of Hormuz. What’s your take on this?

They have certainly lost their credibility. In other words, in the past when they said they would do something, they succeeded because they had the power to do so.

In the Iraq-Afghanistan conflicts after 20 years of fighting, spending billions of dollars and achieving absolutely nothing, they lost credibility. The Iran thing looks a bit like that. They want to control and destroy the regime in Iran, but they find they can’t do so.

The only way they can do so is to invade Iran, but the Iraq-Afghanistan affair taught them a lesson. Don’t attack on land.

They are trying to destroy everything that Iran has, hoping Iranians will give up and just surrender. That has turned out to be much more difficult than they expected. The only way they can win is to land troops.

I think [Donald] Trump knows that he has made a mistake to have gone this far and is trying to end the war by persuading the Iranians to do a deal. But the Iranians are making it difficult for him to say that he has won.

Their main weapon is to control the Strait of Hormuz. It’s not a legal problem any more. It’s a question of whether they have the capacity to control the strait. In the meantime, the allies of the United States are also having a hard time, shipping is almost completely blocked, and the whole world is being badly affected.

So we cannot conclude that the US is in decline yet – we can only say it lost the Iranian war?

The word “decline” is not the right word because it is still the most powerful military force in the world. The US spends far more on their military than most of the major powers added together.

I have lost count of how many military bases they have in all continents. They have aircraft carriers in every ocean, and they have tremendous air power. And nuclear weapons which only a few other countries have.

I would say that in military terms and in terms of the economy, the Americans are not doing badly despite all these problems. They are still in charge of so many economic levers, imposing tariffs on everybody.

They’re probably not doing as well as they want, but there is no doubt that the military and the arms industry are doing extremely well. It is obvious that the richest people in America are getting richer every day.

Also, the capacity to be inventive and make technological advances is still very great. I think that the Chinese know that they’re catching up but they’re still behind. The Chinese are still learning from the people they sent to America to learn.

Furthermore, the US has one advantage that China and others don’t have. It is totally safe in its hemisphere. No country in the Americas can threaten the United States. Therefore, they are free to send their aircraft carriers anywhere around the world to exercise their hegemonic power.

In comparison, China has problems in its neighbourhood. On land, China tries hard to keep good relations with Russia and North Korea. At sea, they have to make friends with the countries in Southeast Asia and are trying very hard to make sure that their borders are safe.

China is working closely with Russia to promote a multipolar world order, which the US probably doesn’t like. Do you think this multipolar world order will happen and the US will have to face it?

The Chinese say that they want a multipolar order and believe that that’s safer for them.

The Americans believe that a multipolar order would be a very difficult one to control and the disorder would be distracting. If they can’t have universal hegemony, they would prefer a bipolar order.

With bipolarity, they could concentrate on China. They have learned lessons from the way they destroyed the Soviet Union and would focus on undermining China’s economic growth.

They can limit China’s rich markets in North America and western Europe. They could allow the Belt and Road Initiative to help China reach the markets and resources of the underdeveloped world, but those countries cannot buy much from China.

The Chinese could go on producing manufactured goods cheaper and faster, but if the West can limit the Chinese sales, China’s economy would slow down significantly.

In one interview you talked about continuity in Chinese history – how the rulers highlighted the importance of a centralised bureaucracy and the people believed it and this was the case for hundreds of years. Do you think the way modern China is governed continues this pattern, and is there anything unique about the governance of modern China?

I think China is unique in its sense of continuity from the Shang-Zhou dynasty down to the present. And although it had dynastic changes, many foreign invaders, and was even under total foreign rule, it has always been China. Even the Mongols and the Manchus had to rule China primarily in the Chinese way – what the Chinese literati believed was the best way to rule China.

In historical records, they kept a picture of China as having had one history as a centralised bureaucratic state.

It was the Qin-Han empires that produced the mix of Legalist and Confucian ideas by which everything had to be decided from the centre. Officials were appointed to ensure total control.

The Qin dynasty did not last long. The people couldn’t stand its harsh Legalist system and revolted against it after Qin Shi Huang’s death.

The Han dynasty was wiser. Its founder led the rebellion against the Qin. He and his successors developed a softer regime. They retained the Legalist central bureaucratic state but brought in Confucian officials to reform the structure.

The new officials placed emphasis upon the emperor being a good man, being moral and caring, serving and looking after the people, making sure there is social harmony, and so on. That way, you have security within the country and the people won’t rebel.

Confucius advised that rulers must be good. They must make their people feel that they care for their livelihood. For example, they should worry about them and make sure, for example, that their taxes are very low. They should on the whole leave people alone to live their lives with minimum government interference.

They appointed officials from the centre, but these officials were not asked to do much. Don’t do much more than just collect the taxes and make sure that there were no rebellions. Those were the two main jobs they had to do well so the people were left very much alone to do whatever they wanted for their families.

Every part of the country could develop in its own way. The idea was that in this way everyone looked to the centre for a caring ruler who would always have the mandate of heaven. Although the mandate of heaven was normally won on the battlefield, heaven was on the side of the people, and the mandate was given to the person whom the people believed would be a good ruler.

If you were a good ruler, you would have the mandate. If you were a bad one, the people had the right to rebel against you. That was the mechanism of dynastic change whereby the people could determine who should head the new dynasty.

Another test was whether the ruler could defend the country against the enemy. Rulers who could always do so would retain the people’s respect. Their dynasties would then survive for longer with the people’s loyal support.

The Confucians described the various ways the rulers could best care for their people. That was the most important contribution to the good governance that was so valued throughout all Chinese history. In short, performance record was the key.

In modern times, the 1911 revolution threw out the emperors of the dynastic system and became a republican state modelled on France and the United States. Sun Yat-sen’s Republic of China became a nation state – that is, the state of all the citizens of China.

It didn’t work because the Chinese simply didn’t understand what a republic was, what was a citizen, what were his/her constitutional rights, freedom, liberty, democracy because all these ideas were new. It took a couple of decades to teach people about modern politics.

In the meantime, the country was pressured by the Japanese and there were unequal treaties that hampered economic development. Also, the country was falling apart because of the civil war among the warlords and the ideological struggles between the Nationalists and the Communists. In the end, the Nationalist Party were ousted in 1949 by Mao Zedong’s Communist Party.

Now the Communists brought in something new. They brought in the idea of a very disciplined party dedicated to revolution to make sure that you don’t depend on the past. They set out on a new path to progress. As you know, the Marxist idea of historical progress was a European idea.

Karl Marx showed that in history, stages of change meant that there was progress. His example of progress was to prove that socialism was the stage after capitalism. Before that, there had been slavery and feudalism followed by capitalism.

By the 19th century, capitalism was all-powerful. But capitalism will eventually become socialist. This was what Marx predicted. The progress to socialism meant there would then be a state that was more caring.

In China, this was taken up by the Communists who set out to revolutionise society so that there would be no poverty, better distribution of wealth, with nobody too rich, nobody too poor and where social harmony prevailed.

In recent years, some leaders in the People’s Republic have revived the idea of a caring state organised to produce a fairer distribution of wealth. There are thinkers who have compared that to some Confucian references to rulers being committed to the well-being of their people.

The terminology used by the Communists is quite different but the sentiments behind the words can be compared favourably with some moral goals that would find favour with Confucian philosophers.

The caring is not by one man as a good and moral emperor, but a political party consisting of highly disciplined members. In the Chinese tradition, the emperor was above the law because he made the law. The Communist Party, like the emperor, is not subject to the law courts because it made the law.

When the Communist Party discovers that somebody in the party has done something wrong and party discipline has found him guilty, the person is sacked from the party. He would then be charged in a court of law because, as a party member, he is above the law. That would be very strange in most countries, but it is more like the way Chinese emperors were above the law.

As a result, serious problems can be created. With the country ruled in that way, it is understandable that people have little confidence that the legal system can be just.

When the economy was doing very well under Jiang Zemin and Hu Jintao, corruption was pervasive throughout the country. Xi Jinping saw it when he was in Fujian and Zhejiang. He didn’t have the power to do much, but he must have observed it with deep concern.

When he was given the power to act as secretary general of the party, the first thing he did was to tell the Communist Party to study what happened to the Communist Party of the Soviet Union.

The CCP was modelled on that, so look how quickly that party collapsed. There was corruption and factionalism and the leaders could not agree as to what they could do. They had lost their revolutionary ideals and the party did not have the sense of purpose to discipline its members.

Xi Jinping talked to the party about every member being subject to the law of corruptibility. In that sense, his legalistic intervention was to try and save the party. I think he genuinely believes that only the party can save China and make China strong so that it can recover its position in the world today.

He believes that, to save the party, the party must be clean and disciplined. Only then will the people love the party and believe that only the party can save China.

I think this is what he is determined to do and why he wants better control of everything in the country. It’s not easy because corruption is so pervasive.

He has been in power for about 14 years now and he is still finding corrupt people in high office. He is still catching thousands of party leaders every year, sending them to the law courts and putting them in jail, including having some of his most senior PLA generals sentenced to death.

That’s how serious it is after 14 years. This shows that fighting corruption is not enough. There are important institutions that need to be totally reviewed and restructured.

In the meantime, the system could be locked up so that few dare to take initiative with original projects. Officials would feel safer by continuing with projects that have worked in the past.

Everyone is wary of being caught in the anti-corruption campaign. If you were a bureaucrat or a party secretary, you would have to be brave to start a new project. If you start a project with other officials, how can you be sure that none of them are corrupt?

The net result is that less is being done and only safe decisions are being made.

Of course, not everything depends on the centre. It is in the Chinese tradition that the centre did not expect every part of the country to wait for the centre to tell them what to do.

There had always been decentralisation of power to provinces, cities, counties where officials were encouraged to familiarise themselves with local conditions and find the best way to make their respective areas successful.

Today, when GDP rises, everybody is happy, and if protests occur, they are resolved effectively and not allowed to spread. If the official manages these well, he can expect to be promoted.

If an official initiates something new, there are always risks. To be ever careful, it is probably wiser to do as little as possible.

In general, the industrial economy looks as if it is stagnating. Unemployment has become serious. The number of graduates looking for jobs is huge. As a result, the sense of security is not as good as the party leaders had hoped for.

And because of this lack of social security, there has been the trend to extend control over anything foreign – for example, restrictions on reading materials published outside. Professors refrain from teaching from textbooks that are not published and approved by the party. A picture emerges that such controls are actually making people less confident in their daily lives.

There are tensions in China, especially among the young generation, that the party has not been able to lighten.

My view is that China’s problems are more domestic. Outside, its leaders know how to handle the United States, like the way they managed the visits of Presidents Trump, [Vladimir] Putin and Kim [Jong-un].

In external affairs, the Chinese are managing alright. The domestic problems look much more serious. It is not a matter of having enough fresh talent. The people are very talented, but they need to be given more freedom and opportunity to be creative and make things even better.

What’s your view on Taiwan? How can it be resolved? Or do you think the tension will only worsen?

Taiwan reminds me of the “one country, two systems” issue that Hong Kong is familiar with. Taiwan was always part of China. After the war ended in 1945, everything taken by Japan had to be returned to China. That was internationally recognised.

When Chiang Kai-shek went to Taiwan in 1949, the issue was still clear and open.

A few months afterwards, however, North Korea invaded the South and United Nations forces were sent to war. China’s volunteers supported North Korea and drove the UN troops back to the 38th Parallel.

With China fighting the UN armies, conditions rapidly changed for the PRC’s [People’s Republic of China’s] chances to replace the ROC [Republic of China, Taiwan’s official name] in the UN.

As a result, the Republic of China in Taiwan was recognised as China for another 22 years.

The PRC was the enemy of the US in the Cold War and any question of the United States and China talking about diplomatic relations and admission into the UN was completely lost. The US could use its veto power to stop the PRC from taking over the ROC’s position in the Security Council.

It was not until the United Nations General Assembly voted with a two-thirds majority in favour that China was admitted.

At that point, America was prepared to recognise the ROC in Taiwan as an independent state and support its membership in the United Nations.

I believe that Chiang Kai-shek refused on the grounds that there was only one China and it was wrong to allow the PRC to take over from the ROC.

Once the PRC was in the UN Security Council, it would have used its veto against the ROC’s admission anyway.

What was significant at the time was that Mao Zedong would have agreed with Chiang that there was only one China.

Later, the local Taiwanese people found that the constitution of the Republic of China allowed for democracy. After Chiang Ching-kuo passed the presidency to Lee Teng-hui, the elections that followed could for the first time vote in a pro-independence party to form the government.

The new Taiwanese leaders were then able to claim that the Taiwan people had voted to say that they wanted to be independent. They now had a government that was of the people, by the people and for the people.

The PRC leaders did not accept that and made it quite clear that if the new leaders declared independence, they would have to take the island by force.

This led the Americans to intervene to tell the Taiwanese not to count on their support if they declared independence. They recognised that a declaration of independence would have crossed China’s red line.

For now, it is a balanced situation with the status quo acceptable for both sides. It allows the Taiwanese to say that they don’t need to declare independence as the Republic of China has been there since 1912. And the US Congress continues to vote for military equipment to be sold for Taiwan’s defence as long as it does not declare independence.

Taiwan continues to make friends with other countries that are anti-communist. Japan for one seems ready to help. This angers the Chinese.

But I believe that the Chinese do not want to fight over Taiwan because that would mean killing other Chinese and it would involve interventions by countries coming to Taiwan’s defence. No one would gain out of that war.

I think both Americans and the PRC would agree not to fight a war over Taiwan.

What Beijing wants to be sure of is that Taiwan is not used by its enemies in any attack on China.

If Taiwan remains friendly to Beijing or at least stays neutral or in sympathy with Beijing and recognise themselves as Chinese people with the same culture and civilisation, I think the status quo could remain for quite a while.

In any case, the Chinese can take a long view. There’s no immediate hurry.

With the long view, such divisions within China have happened many times in Chinese history. During those periods of division, the view was that China would always become one again.

How do you see your own legacy?

I don’t know that I have any legacy. I would like to believe that I have convinced some people that history is very important.

Everybody has history and everybody’s history is useful to know so that you have some idea of what it was like in the past. What mistakes were made, what would be the right things to do. What made for good governance, for example, would be very important in modern affairs in any kind of world order.

Good governance is extremely important and history tells us about that in the way that is part of the Chinese tradition.

History is not about the truth. Finding out what exactly happened is important. But it is not why knowing the past is often crucial.

History is what is known about the past that could help you understand the present and give you a clearer idea of what is likely and what can and cannot happen.

To make history meaningful to your own thinking about anything in the world could be very interesting and valuable anyway.

It would be very satisfying if I could convince people that everything has a past and every past has some usefulness. If they learn to use the past to get a better understanding of what is happening, they would find that enlightening.

I believe that knowing the past also helps good governance. History changes all the time and is often rewritten. Therefore, governments who pay no attention to history are likely to make mistakes.

Think of the number of times history has been rewritten in the last few decades. How many are written by the Europeans, by the Americans, by the Chinese Nationalists, by the Japanese, by the Communists? Every one of their histories can be changed over time.

During the early decades of the PRC, people wrote history using Marxist arguments about everything, but today nobody writes like that any more.

People don’t write Chinese history following Marx’s stages of change. What Marx did teach them was about the idea of progress. He showed that human beings have been progressing; they produced more and better goods, became better than slaves and feudal peasants.

In today’s terms, he could be interpreted to argue that capitalist wealth could lead to a better socialist redistribution of that wealth.

The idea of progress shows that the past helps you to understand where we came from and might be going to. This can be seen in the way the Chinese say, look at China today. Can you imagine the kind of science and technology that is happening in China today a hundred years ago? Unimaginable. Therefore, progress is possible and progress will continue to be possible.

I think this is what the Chinese learned by looking at their past. The past has taught them not only that China can survive but can also fall and rise yet again.

But whatever they did, if they put their heart into it, worked hard at it, learned from the past and provided good governance, there would be more progress. And to believe in that idea of progress would depend on their understanding of the past.
