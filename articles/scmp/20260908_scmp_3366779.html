Philippines wants to be a global semiconductor giant by 2030. Can it get there?

SCMP | FREE | 1042 words | 2026-09-08

The Philippines has launched a five-year road map to expand its share of the global semiconductor and electronics sector, but capacity challenges and political stability are potential stumbling blocks, analysts say.

The Philippine Semiconductor and Electronics Industry road map, launched by the Philippines’ Semiconductor and Electronics Industry Advisory Council (SEIAC) on September 2, aims to grow annual exports of chips and electronics to US$110 billion by 2030, and move beyond manufacturing and assembly into higher-end operations.

SEIAC chief and executive secretary Ralph Recto said the goal was to transition to chip design, engineering, research and innovation to boost regional competitiveness.

One target is raising the Philippines’ share of global assembly, testing and packaging – already the country’s top semiconductor activities – from 4 per cent to 7 per cent by 2030.

Other goals include growing the country’s share of the global electronics manufacturing services market from less than 1 per cent to 4 per cent and building an integrated circuit design sector that generates US$2 billion to US$3 billion in annual exports.

This expansion would translate to opportunities beyond factories, including jobs in engineering, research, design and other highly skilled fields, Recto said.

Over 60 per cent of the Philippines’ current merchandise exports are from the semiconductor and electronics industry, which generates over US$45 billion annually.

The industry is also being eyed as a major driver of the Luzon Economic Corridor, a US-Japan-Philippines infrastructure initiative and economic hub.

“Whether we act or not, this industry will continue to expand. And we must grow with it. That is the direction President [Ferdinand] Marcos Jnr has set for us,” Recto said.

The SEIAC chairman said the government would focus on removing barriers – such as skilled worker shortages, gaps in power, water and logistics infrastructure, slow permitting and regulatory bottlenecks.

All technical working groups would be held to specific targets and timelines, he said. “A road map is only as good as its execution. We have enough good plans in government. What the industry needs from us now are results.”

Explicit strategy

Josef Yap, a senior research fellow at the Ateneo School of Government, said the road map turned previous recommendations into a “much more explicit industrial upgrading strategy”.

It addressed a larger portion of the value chain and connected semiconductor policy to related sectors such as AI, data centres, consumer electronics, clean energy, infrastructure corridors, economic zones and international partnerships.

“This makes the road map more ambitious as an instrument of economic transformation,” he said.

Lawrence Dacuycuy, a professor of economics at the De La Salle University, said the US$110 billion export target was “possible but highly ambitious”. Electronics exports in 2025 hit US$49.64 billion – meaning a compound annual growth of over 17 per cent for five years would be needed to reach the mark.

The most “credible” target would be raising its global assembly, testing and packaging (ATP) share, due to its established base, he said. But here, the Philippines faces stiff competition from neighbours Malaysia, Vietnam, Thailand and India.

“Achieving 7 per cent would require the Philippines to outperform the current forecast and expand faster than global ATP capacity growth … This is especially challenging because Vietnam is projected to increase its share from 1 per cent to 8 per cent, and Malaysia from 7 to 9 per cent,” Dacuycuy said.

“The key is to strengthen the country’s supply chains … and design an investment architecture that would support upgrades, advanced manufacturing, diversification and financing.”

Though not the first, the road map was the Philippines’ “most ambitious and sophisticated one yet”, said Lianne Angelico Depante, a doctoral researcher at Tokyo’s National Graduate Institute for Policy Studies.

Its strength lay in recognising the need to build infrastructure such as national laboratories for design, advanced packaging and lab-scale fabrication. “The focus on building capabilities is an important shift in the way we think about industrial policy compared to before,” he said.

But the real test would be “whether we can actually implement it effectively and consistently,” he said. “This is where the Philippines has historically struggled.”

Bigger picture

To succeed, Manila should take a “whole-of-government” approach by developing related sectors and exploring more holistic financing, the analysts say.

Instead of being dependent on foreign direct investments, the industry should look to “embedded reciprocity”, Yap said. “The central policy question is not simply how to attract foreign investment, but how to convert the presence of [multinational corporations] into domestic … capabilities.”

State funding, such as sovereign wealth fund Maharlika Investment, could potentially play a role, particularly for projects that private capital may consider too risky or long-term, according to Depante.

“But ultimately, this will depend on whether the government has the risk appetite … because, in an industry like semiconductors, the willingness to take calculated risks is what determines whether a country remains in low-value activities or successfully moves up the value chain,” he said.

Krista Danielle Yu, an economics professor at the De La Salle University, said the government should prioritise talent development. “Without talent, investors will not come. The road map looks at the talent pipeline in the higher education part, when in reality, it should start as early as elementary school.”

She also stressed the importance of developing stable and cheaper sources of energy. The Philippines’ power rates are the highest in Southeast Asia, according to the Department of Energy.

“Industrial water costs about three times what it does in Malaysia. Logistics is also more expensive compared to other Asean countries,” she said.

To move up the value chain, the focus had to go beyond semiconductor firms, Depante said. “We also need to develop the industries that support them, from chemicals, metals, equipment, logistics and power to other upstream and downstream activities.”

Clark, a central hub in the Luzon Economic Corridor, could potentially play that role, concentrating all forms of support around it, he said.

Most importantly, the survival of the road map will depend on how it weathers leadership changes, according to Depante.

Launched with less than two years in the Marcos administration, the road map could be disrupted by shifting priorities under the next presidency.

“If we genuinely believe that semiconductors and electronics can be a strategic industry for the Philippines … then we need to build institutions and a policy consensus that can survive beyond any single administration.”
