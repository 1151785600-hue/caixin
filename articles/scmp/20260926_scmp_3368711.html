Ukraine’s POW transfer reveal deals North Korea a propaganda blow

SCMP | FREE | 1035 words | 2026-09-26

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky has put North Korea in a propaganda bind, analysts say, by publicly revealing that Kyiv recently transferred two North Korean prisoners of war to South Korea.

The disclosure – made during Zelensky’s address to the UN General Assembly on Wednesday – directly challenges Pyongyang’s narrative that its soldiers fighting alongside Russian forces would choose death over surrender.

North Korea is unlikely to acknowledge the transfer publicly, but may strengthen ideological controls over its troops deployed to Russia to prevent further defections, observers say.

“Recently, we sent two North Korean prisoners of war to the Republic of Korea,” Zelensky said during his UN address, using South Korea’s official name.

“These guys are not easy to capture alive. One of them, when he realised he was going to be taken prisoner, tried to kill himself and he was shocked that fate didn’t let him die.”

The two soldiers were reportedly captured by Ukrainian forces in January 2025 after being deployed to Russia’s Kursk region to support Moscow’s war effort. Zelensky said they subsequently expressed a desire to travel to South Korea, as indicated in interviews and handwritten letters sent to organisations representing North Korean defectors.

South Korea’s Foreign Ministry declined to confirm any specific details regarding the POWs, citing concerns for the safety of the men and their families in the North.

But it did say in a statement that the issue should be resolved in a manner that “respects the free will of the individuals concerned and is consistent with international law and humanitarian principles”.

“In line with this position, the government has been engaged in necessary consultations with Ukraine and other relevant countries and organisations,” the ministry said, adding that this was “a sensitive matter” and it could not divulge details about bilateral consultations.

South Korean President Lee Jae Myung expressed regret about the disclosure in a social media post on Thursday, calling it “a highly sensitive matter” that could have serious implications for diplomacy and security on the Korean peninsula.

“Considering the various issues that could arise from making the matter public, keeping it confidential was the reasonable course of action in every respect, and that was what we agreed upon,” Lee wrote in Korean.

“While I do not know the specific circumstances that led the Ukrainian side to break the agreement and go public, the situation is deeply regrettable.”

South Korean opposition lawmaker Yu Yong-weon said on Thursday that the two prisoners had left Ukraine last weekend, travelled via a third country and arrived in South Korea three to four days earlier, likely under the protection of South Korean intelligence agencies.

In July, Lee and Zelensky agreed on the sidelines of the Nato summit in Ankara to handle the North Korean POW issue based on humanitarian principles while respecting the prisoners’ wishes.

‘Propaganda nightmare’

Zelensky raised the POW issue while accusing Russian President Vladimir Putin of recruiting foreign fighters to prosecute his war on Ukraine.

“Putin is always looking for someone who can help him kill more, kill more people for every kilometre of land, take more territory … instead of simply stopping and running his own country,” Zelensky said, adding that Moscow had recruited citizens from 47 countries, including Ghana, Nepal and India.

Yang Moo-jin, a professor at the University of North Korean Studies in Seoul, said Pyongyang would likely remain silent to protect the narrative of absolute loyalty and self-sacrifice among its troops.

“North Korea is likely to ignore the issue or show little interest, given the potential burden of allowing it to become a major political issue,” Yang said. “At the same time, Pyongyang is expected to strengthen ideological education and internal controls over North Korean troops in Russia to prevent similar defections.”

Oh Gyeong-seob, a senior research fellow at the Korea Institute for National Unification, described the situation as a “propaganda nightmare” for Pyongyang, as it had consistently eulogised its soldiers as heroes who take their own lives rather than surrender.

“For North Korean soldiers in Russia, this news opens a different option that is not death when they risk being captured,” Oh said, adding that Ukraine was likely to use the defection in psychological warfare targeting North Korean troops.

Questions of trust

Yang said that Zelensky’s decision to go public with the POW transfer could have broader geopolitical implications – potentially driving a wedge between Seoul and Kyiv.

“Bringing such a sensitive humanitarian issue directly into the international spotlight at the United Nations could undermine trust in relations between South Korea and Ukraine,” Yang said. “The Ukrainian president could also face criticism that a sensitive humanitarian issue is being politicised.”

Respecting prisoners’ free will should have included getting their consent before revealing their transfer, Yang added, given the risk of retaliation against their families remaining in North Korea.

“Announcing their defection without first securing their consent would constitute an inhumane act,” he said. “Zelensky himself should make clear whether the prisoners’ consent to the public disclosure was explicitly obtained.”

Yang dismissed speculation of a secret deal between Kyiv and Seoul, pointing to President Lee’s stated preference for diplomatic transparency.

Oh said the disclosure could also serve Ukraine’s broader strategy of highlighting the deepening military relationship between Russia and North Korea as Kyiv sought continued international support for its war effort.

While Seoul has provided humanitarian aid to Ukraine, it maintains a strict policy against supplying lethal weapons to any country at war.

Asian Games defections?

Zelensky’s disclosure coincided with an unconfirmed report that two members of North Korea’s delegation to this year’s Asian Games in Japan were seeking asylum.

South Korea’s Monthly Chosun magazine reported on Tuesday that the pair – part of an estimated 180-member North Korean delegation – had approached Japanese authorities expressing a desire to seek asylum in Japan rather than South Korea.

Citing a source linked to Japan’s Cabinet Intelligence and Research Office and the pro-Pyongyang General Association of Korean Residents in Japan, known as Chongryon, the magazine said the individuals’ identities and roles had not been disclosed.

Japanese authorities have not publicly confirmed the report and it is unclear whether any defections have occurred at the Games.
