The ‘G2’ illusion? After Xi-Trump summit, China wants equality while the US seeks deals

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1655 words | 2026-10-08

Washington and Beijing may have signed an agreement built around “reciprocity”. But a more consequential question remains unresolved: does it mean equal status for Beijing? Countries without a seat at the table are watching that ambiguity closely.

The spectacle of Chinese President Xi Jinping’s state visit to Washington – the tarmac greeting, the flyovers, the state dinner – has been interpreted less as courtesy than as a statement of rank. Some argue that China is no longer being treated as part of the Global South, but as the US’ peer.

But “reciprocity” does not necessarily mean the same thing to both sides.

For the United States, reciprocity is transactional: a concession matched by a concession, a deal measured by what each side gets in return.

For China, it means equal standing: the recognition that Beijing should be dealt with not as a subordinate power being managed by Washington, but as a peer.

Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi made that meaning explicit as the visit ended. In remarks released by state news agency Xinhua on September 26, he said reciprocity meant the two countries were “equal in sovereignty”, that neither side stood above the other, that concerns were handled “on an equal basis” and that cooperation developed “in a balanced manner”.

According to analysts, US President Donald Trump’s embrace of “G2” diplomacy and the extraordinary choreography of his reception for Xi give Beijing an opportunity to turn the language of a transaction into the image of parity.

Sun Chenghao, a fellow at Tsinghua University’s Centre for International Security and Strategy, said the reception showed that Washington valued Beijing’s international weight and was willing to “invest political resources into improving ties” – a signal that could “set the stage for future negotiations and inter-agency cooperation”.

But he cautioned against reading the symbolism too far. “Reception protocol alone is not enough to conclude that both sides have accepted a ‘G2’ framework,” Sun said, referring to the idea of a “Group of Two”, or a global duopoly with China and the US as the world’s leading powers.

“If ‘G2’ merely emphasises the importance of US-China coordination to global affairs, that indeed reflects reality,” he said.

“If it implies the two countries jointly dominating the world and making decisions on behalf of other nations, that goes beyond any conclusion this meeting can support.”

“China is more concerned with whether the relationship is equal, whereas the US side is more focused on whether specific trade-offs are worthwhile,” Sun added.

Trump’s warmth towards Xi appears personal rather than institutional. The White House “still stresses that the US should exercise leadership from a position of strength”, he said.

What matters, he said, was “whether the US can respect China’s right to development and core interests in concrete policies, and accept addressing differences through consultation on an equal footing”.

That gap is the “G2” illusion: the two sides can sit across the same table while attaching very different expectations to what the relationship means.

Sourabh Gupta, a senior fellow at the Institute for China-America Studies, was blunt: a US-China “G2” is not emerging.

“Trump aside, much of his administration and the beltway political establishment seek rivalry with China. But China won’t play ball,” he said.

“Just as one cannot clap with one hand, so also the two sides are stuck between uneasy coexistence and the drift towards rivalry.”

The same logic applies to the economy. China wants interdependence; much of the US would rather decouple.

The result, Gupta said, was “a mediocre and deteriorating economic equation that might appear stable at a given moment, but which masks a deeper strategic hollowness”. In that condition, a “G2” was “a far-fetched assumption”, he added.

Xi Jinping does not want a G2 that governs the world together with the United States

Ken Moriyasu, Hudson Institute

Sarang Shidore, director of the Global South Programme at the Quincy Institute, said “a sweeping G2” was unlikely. “[But] limited G2s may emerge down the road that create bargains in certain spaces in which outright competition is too costly or risky for both. AI is such an area,” he added.

The chances of a resolution rise if neither side can prevail easily – which he noted was the present reality – and if both see mutual benefit, a condition he said had yet to be observed.

Ken Moriyasu, a senior fellow at the Hudson Institute, argued that Beijing did not even want to be part of a duopoly. “Xi Jinping does not want a G2 that governs the world together with the United States,” he said.

According to Moriyasu, Xi wants a “new model of major-country relations” in which the two powers respect each other’s political systems, core interests and spheres of concern while avoiding confrontation.

He said Beijing’s preferred order was more multipolar than a “G2” – closer to the UN Security Council, with five major powers standing above the rest.

As the weaker power, Gupta said, Beijing “harps on sovereign equality in its dealings with Washington, much as New Delhi continually does so in its dealings with Beijing. It is a function of weakness, and strict reciprocity viewed as an equaliser.”

He added that the US had stood “head and shoulders above its peers for the past 75 years” and treated sovereign equality as “a notional concept” while leveraging its advantages for “transactional gain”.

The Global South question

These observations land on a larger question: whether China still belongs in the Global South category it has long claimed.

Shidore drew a distinction between the treatment and the label.

“Most certainly, Washington treats China very differently from any other Global South middle power,” he said. “China is being recognised as a great power, a recognition that China has itself long craved. With President Trump, it has received it without any reservations.”

Moriyasu went further. “I doubt many countries in the Global South now look at China and see a fellow Global Southerner,” he said. “If it walks like a duck and quacks like a duck, it is a duck. China is now a full-fledged great power.”

Gupta rejected the binary. China “can be a great power and still remain very much a part of the Global South”, he said, calling it a “bundle of contradictions” that could sit at the global head table and still be “a great power within the confines of the Global South’s rise”.

If Washington deals with Beijing as a peer, however, China begins to look less like the most powerful member of that camp and more like one of the powers sitting above it. That reading has landed hardest in New Delhi.

Indian officials and commentators watched the Chinese leader’s Washington visit with a familiar dread: a US-China “G2”, long feared in private, was being staged in public. Trump was receiving Xi as a peer.

India in the audience

Ivan Lidarev, a visiting research fellow at the National University of Singapore’s Institute of South Asian Studies, said the fanfare had sparked “unease and jealousy” in India.

“New Delhi has long feared the emergence of a US-China G2 through which the two sides will manage the world and South Asia,” he said.

Trump has refrained from using the phrase “G2” in recent days, though he has framed his encounters with Xi in those terms since their summit in South Korea last autumn.

To Brahma Chellaney, a New Delhi-based strategic analyst, Trump’s language was more revealing than the pageantry.

“The writing on the wall is clear: Trump sees America and China as the world’s two pre-eminent powers that should cooperate in managing global affairs, despite their intense rivalry,” he wrote on social media.

“For America’s allies and strategic partners, especially in Asia, that signals a profound shift in Washington’s strategic world view.”

For some Indian observers, the summit pointed to “how far China is ahead of India”, Lidarev said, with the US increasingly treating China as an equal superpower. He said it also threatened the two-decade bedrock of US-India ties – which were “built on counterbalancing China” – just as those ties were strained over trade and oil sanctions.

Jabin Jacob, a professor at Shiv Nadar University, said India would not read much into the pageantry.

“It certainly understands that [the meeting] does not fundamentally alter the trajectory of US-China relations,” he said. “This is competitive more than it is cooperative.”

Lidarev noted that India’s fears about the Xi-Trump meeting included the possibility of joint Sino-American oversight of the Indo-Pacific, a technological duopoly in AI and semiconductors, and US concessions on Taiwan that could embolden Beijing.

“Fortunately for India, none of these concerns were justified,” he said.

But more frequent Trump-Xi meetings still raise the prospect of a real “G2” at India’s expense. Lidarev said he did not expect a major recalibration following the summit, but Delhi would pursue a thaw in ties with Beijing and adopt a “less US-leaning position”.

Jacob said India had to reset its policy towards both powers anyway, because its compromise-heavy stance, driven by economic strain and reliance on Russian oil, was “suboptimal”.

That need would become more urgent if US-China ties evolved into a genuine duopoly.

Shidore said that if such an arrangement emerged, even in just a few sectors or specific regions, other countries would find less room for independent policy.

“Rather than exploit the competition between Beijing and Washington, these states will have to conform to rules jointly defined by the two great powers, rules against which they will have little capacity to resist,” he said.

He added that the pressure would be less about choosing a side than “falling in line with an arrangement they had no voice in shaping”.

“We are not there yet,” he said. “It remains to be seen if we will get there.”
