Stella Ma’s mission to make Cantonese opera relevant to Hong Kong’s children

SCMP | FREE | 1264 words | 2026-11-12

I WAS BORN in Sham Shui Po. I have one older sister and three younger brothers in Hong Kong. My father had another family in Guangzhou before coming to Hong Kong, so I have three older siblings there. My father first came to Hong Kong in the 1940s and worked at his uncle’s preserved-fruit factory as a clerk, then moved to an aluminium-pots manufacturer and worked as an accountant – this is where he met my mother. She was born in Dali, Guangdong province, and came to Hong Kong in 1950 with one of her fellow villagers. My mother told me how my father took her to see The Sound of Music after they got married.

I WENT TO Ying Wa Girls’ School, in Mid-Levels. Then, I attended Polytechnic University to study institutional management and catering, but I realised working in hotels wasn’t what I wanted as a career. In 1982, I finished university and then spent two months travelling to Xinjiang and Xian. After that, my father sent me to stay with the Guangzhou family. I was 20, and I was the first representative of the Hong Kong family to go and see them. I remember walking along the railway to their house – they worked for the railway. We were strangers to one another, so when we met, we were very polite and cautious. I sent them greetings from my father. A few years later, my mother, father and siblings in Hong Kong went to Guangzhou to visit the other family. My father and his first wife had not seen each other for more than 40 years.

AT THE AGE of 21, I went to work as a deputy warden at the China Coast Community, in Kowloon Tong, taking care of elderly residents of different nationalities. There was a Russian lady, who loved to smoke. When the doctor advised her not to smoke any more, our social-worker warden said, “She’s 90! Let her do what she likes.” Learning that there are different angles to a discussion was new to me. In 1986, I joined the Hong Kong Tourist Association (HKTA) in its tour-development department. I learned event management from organising the International Dragon Boat Festival and developing tours focused on heritage and feng shui.

IN THE EVENINGS, I studied Chinese and English on a part-time Chinese University degree course for six years. I have always loved Chinese music, so I also took up music lessons while working and studying. In 1989, I left the HKTA and joined a public-relations company run by Doreen Leung Dor-ling. She was the first woman to win the JCI (Junior Chamber International) Ten Outstanding Young Persons of the World award, in 1984. I enjoyed working for Doreen. One of our pet projects was the Hong Kong Outstanding Students Award. Soon after joining Doreen, I had the chance to accompany 10 awardees to Singapore, and I still stay in touch with some of them.

WORKING IN PR was very demanding and I was still studying in the evening. In 1992, I decided to set up my own company, Management Service Centre, the idea being that I would find more time for myself. I thought I could do several things at the same time, so why not? I was writing letters for people and doing Chinese-to-English translations.

MY SECOND COMPANY was called the Cultural Link Centre, which works with a high-school exchange programme supported by the American government – it’s a chance for kids who don’t always fit the Hong Kong education system. It’s unlike the British or the Hong Kong system and it helps many students who don’t thrive here. Kids go to study in the United States, often in a small community or town where they are the only Chinese student, and they come away speaking fluent English, with their confidence increased and often a much better relationship with their family in Hong Kong on their return. A high percentage of them even get into world-class universities. I loved the exchange programme so much that, in 2011, I started a scholarship programme, called the Yes I Can Scholarship. Over the years, it’s had more than 50 awardees. We are now in our 16th year.

I GOT MARRIED in 2011. My husband runs a family business and is a philosopher. He is a life bonus for me! I’m retired from the overseas-studies business. I now focus on children’s Cantonese opera. My early memories of Cantonese opera are vague – I remember going with my mother to a Cantonese opera and finding it boring. In my 20s, I saw on the news the funeral of Cantonese-opera star Yam Kim-fai, with throngs of people crowded outside the funeral parlour. I wondered at the power of Cantonese opera, that it would draw such a crowd. I started watching old Cantonese-opera films.

BY THEN, I was interested in Chinese music, so I studied for a master’s degree in ethnomusicology at Chinese University. I took a unit in Cantonese opera under my sifu, Tsang Kin-man. He was a very good violin and pipa player and advised me on my dissertation on Cantonese-opera percussion music. When I was growing up, there was no opportunity to learn Cantonese opera at school; our education did not include Chinese music culture. Tsang encouraged me to write my own Cantonese opera. I realised that Cantonese-opera audiences were beginning to dwindle, and that young people could not relate to it, because the stories were often rather highbrow: Romance of The Three Kingdoms, Dream of the Red Chamber and so on. My grandparents’ generation loved Cantonese opera – they would hear it on the radio and then rush to the theatre to watch the show. But now, kids don’t understand the storylines because they are too removed from our lives.

CANTONESE OPERA IS so much a part of our cultural treasure – we need to make it known to the younger generation. In 2002, I started the Cha Duk Chang Children’s Cantonese Opera Association and began writing a full-length Cantonese opera for children, focusing on their daily lives, rather than the usual characters and plots in Cantonese operas, such as emperors, generals, beautiful ladies and love affairs.

MY FIRST CANTONESE opera for children was called The Quest for the Moon Fairy. It was about five children who were forced to stay home and study during the Mid-Autumn Festival. But when they looked outside, they could see fireworks and other children with lanterns, so they decided to sneak out. The story is about the adventure they had. Another one, The Three-Eyed Little Immortal in Distress, follows a character in the heavenly world who fails an exam and is sent down to the human world to take classes, so he can retake the test. That’s another story about children’s daily lives in a singing-and-acting style that is strictly Cantonese opera.

I WANT TO bring the joy of Cantonese opera to Hong Kong kids. We go into kindergartens and primary schools to do classes and workshops. We also organise an annual Cantonese-opera singing contest. We had our annual performance in September at the Xiqu Centre, in West Kowloon Cultural District, and our next performance will be in late December, in Zhuhai, Guangdong province.

WITH THE KIDS, Cantonese opera and the scholarship, I have been lucky to live a life where I can work on the things that really interest me, things that I really love.
