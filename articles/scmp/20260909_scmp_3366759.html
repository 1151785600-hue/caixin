Could China sanction US for illegal international seabed mining? A study says yes

SCMP | FREE | 1136 words | 2026-09-09

For decades, the United States has used economic and financial restrictions as major foreign policy tools.

But as China and other countries gain greater economic and technological strength, they are also acquiring more ways to push back against US actions, with deep-sea mining possibly becoming a testing ground for that shift.

China could join other countries in taking coordinated countermeasures, including sanctions against companies, if the US proceeds with seabed mining in violation of international law, according to Bian Yongmin, a professor of international law at the University of International Business and Economics in Beijing.

“[However,] if the United States insists on ignoring international law, it would indeed be difficult to restrain them,” said a Beijing-based international law expert familiar with the proposal who asked not to be named, citing the sensitivity of the matter.

Bian’s analysis was published in the June issue of the peer-reviewed China-based Pacific Journal.

The US remained bound by customary international law governing global seabed resources, despite not being a party to the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (Unclos), the professor wrote.

The Donald Trump administration has been moving from policy to concrete preparations for seabed mineral development after issuing an executive order in April last year promoting offshore critical-mineral extraction and subsequently advancing proposed lease sales in Pacific waters.

On July 16, the Marine Minerals Administration, an agency within the US Interior Department, published a proposed leasing notice for a potential mineral lease sale offshore American Samoa, with a proposed sale date of November 19 this year.

On August 17, the agency issued another proposed leasing notice for potential mineral leases offshore the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands, with a proposed sale date of December 16.

Even though the US was not a member of Unclos did not mean Washington had a free hand on the international seabed, Bian wrote.

Several principles contained in Part XI of Unclos had developed into customary international law, she added, including the principle that the international seabed was the “common heritage of mankind”, the obligation to protect the marine environment and the requirement that benefits from seabed mineral resources be shared internationally.

In addition, the US’ own past conduct could weaken any argument that it had consistently rejected those rules, Bian said.

In 1970, Washington voted in favour of a UN General Assembly declaration recognising the seabed beyond national jurisdiction as the common heritage of mankind. It later supported arrangements recognising benefit-sharing from deep-sea mineral development.

Bian argued that the US could not, therefore, simply invoke its non-party status to Unclos to escape customary rules it had previously accepted.

The legal question is likely to hinge on how US-authorised mining is ultimately carried out, including whether it appropriates international seabed resources, causes serious environmental harm or allows mining operators to retain benefits that should be shared, according to Bian’s study.

The Clarion-Clipperton Zone, a mineral-rich seabed area in the northeast Pacific, covers about 4.5 million sq km (1.74 million square miles) and is estimated to contain about 21 billion tonnes of polymetallic nodules rich in nickel, cobalt, copper and manganese.

The Metals Company, a Canadian firm, has an American subsidiary that applied this year for a US deep-sea mining permit to operate in the zone.

The zone lies on the seabed of the high seas, and companies and organisations holding exploration licences there have agreed to follow mining rules set by the International Seabed Authority, according to Bian’s research.

The deep ocean is particularly sensitive because polymetallic nodules can take millions of years to form, and experimental mining has been found to leave lasting ecological effects.

For China, the issue creates a tension between defending the multilateral system and protecting access to critical minerals, with Beijing already deeply involved in international seabed development.

“If the United States starts mining first, China may follow,” the unnamed expert said, adding that this was an industry view rather than a personal one.

That said, China’s response would not necessarily mirror the US’ approach, according to Bian’s analysis.

China could conduct seabed mining while continuing to work with the International Seabed Authority and following existing multilateral principles on the common heritage of mankind, benefit-sharing and environmental protection, Bian wrote.

China has multiple exploration contracts in the international seabed area and capabilities in deep-sea equipment, mining machinery and mineral processing, with Chinese participants including China Minmetals Corporation and the China Ocean Mineral Resources Research and Development Association.

Other countries are pursuing their own approaches, although they differ in scope and legal circumstances.

According to Bian’s research, exploration licences in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone are also held by Belgium’s Global Sea Mineral Resources, Norway’s Loke and Green Minerals, Germany’s Federal Institute for Geosciences and Natural Resources, France’s French Research Institute for Exploitation of the Sea, Kiribati’s Marawa Research and Exploration Ltd, South Korea’s Ministry of Oceans and Fisheries and UK Seabed Resources.

“If the United States unilaterally authorises mining, it cannot take the zone, part of the zone or other areas of the international seabed for itself or improperly prevent other countries from legally mining in the area,” Bian wrote in her study.

The unnamed expert said that “Japan and the United States have already signed an agreement to develop rare earth resources on Japan’s continental shelf, which I think is an attempt that faces relatively fewer legal obstacles”.

“The European Union, because of environmental concerns, may not begin deep-sea mining in the foreseeable short term,” the expert said, adding that “India’s technology is not up to it”, when asked about the South Asian country.

But the most immediate response to a US breach would still be negotiation and consultation, the professor said.

Coordinated action could follow if diplomacy failed.

Bian wrote that countries firmly committed to upholding the multilateral mechanism for deep-sea mining could at least refuse to cooperate with American mining companies.

They could also avoid buying deep-sea mineral products sold by American deep-sea mining companies and their processed products, she wrote.

“Companies such as Apple, Google, Microsoft and BMW have promised not to purchase deep-sea minerals in their supply chains.”

And even if American companies obtained permission, mining could not begin immediately.

“Seabed mining is highly risky,” Bian said. “Even if American companies obtained permission, it could not be implemented immediately. I estimate that environmental impact assessments and other procedures will take a lot of time.”

She also said environmental impacts could affect market demand for seabed minerals.

“If the environmental impact is really significant, this kind of mineral may be resisted to a certain extent by the market,” Bian added. “People are reluctant to be associated with problematic products, just as they resist forced-labour products.”
