Houthi blockade of Saudi trade threatens wider Middle East conflict

SCMP | FREE | 1091 words | 2026-07-25

Saudi Arabia and Yemen’s Tehran-allied Houthi movement edged closer to opening a new front in the Iran war overnight, after Riyadh bombed the port city of Hodeidah in retaliation for a Houthi missile strike on one of its ships in the Red Sea.

The exchange marks the latest escalation since the Houthis, known formally as Ansar Allah, imposed an embargo on Saudi shipping on Monday, disrupting maritime traffic through the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, which connects Asia to Europe via the Suez Canal.

A near-identical Houthi missile attack on an oil tanker owned by the National Shipping Company of Saudi Arabia on Thursday had already prompted threats of American military intervention from US President Donald Trump.

“If they do this again, the US will hold Iran responsible, in that the Houthis are a surrogate and/or proxy of Iran, and major military punishment will be inflicted upon Iran and, of course, the Houthis, themselves,” Trump wrote on Truth Social.

The Houthis claimed a missile attack on the southern Saudi city of Jizan on Saturday, after media the group controls accused Saudi Arabia of a “dangerous escalation” and said its actions “will not go unanswered”.

Pakistan, which shares a mutual defence pact with Saudi Arabia, warned it would “take all necessary measures, including lawful use of force”, should its own “maritime interests” come under threat.

Despite these warnings, the Houthis struck and lightly damaged a tanker operated by Saudi state-owned National Chemical Carriers on Friday, shortly after the group’s official spokesman Mohammed Abdul Salam clarified that the blockade applied exclusively to Saudi shipping.

“There is no closure of the Bab al-Mandeb Strait as some promote,” he posted on social media, as several Asian-owned tankers loaded with Saudi crude oil and petrochemicals, which had previously been turned back by the Houthis, were belatedly allowed through to the Indian Ocean.

Houthi ground forces have also launched attacks on two fronts in southwest Yemen held by Saudi-backed government troops and factions supported by the United Arab Emirates.

Houthi brinkmanship

Observers describe the Houthi strikes on Saudi shipping as “brinkmanship” rather than a coordinated effort with Iran to choke off both Gulf states’ oil export routes to Asia – though they say that possibility cannot be ruled out.

For now, further escalation “is not inevitable”, according to Adam Baron, a policy analyst and fellow with the future security programme at Washington-based think tank New America.

“We’re certainly looking at a very tense moment,” he said. “This brinkmanship not only reflects a willingness to potentially use force, but a wider posture that creates greater risks of things spiralling into conflict.”

Political scientist Annelle Sheline, a former US State Department official and Middle East fellow at the Quincy Institute for Responsible Statecraft, sees Trump as “the figure most likely to escalate”.

She said the Houthis “do not necessarily want a return to full-scale war” with the Saudi-led coalition that intervened in Yemen’s civil conflict in 2015 to prevent the government’s overthrow. A tenuous UN-brokered ceasefire has been in effect since April 2022.

Rather, Sheline said the Houthis “are trying to increase pressure on Riyadh” to lift the restrictions imposed on flights and shipping links with Iran, which has long supplied the group with weapons such as anti-ship ballistic missiles.

Both analysts agree that the Houthis are opportunistically exploiting the Hormuz crisis for their own advantage.

“The Houthis view Iran’s success against the US and Israel, and the renewed closure of the Strait of Hormuz, as an opportunity to press Saudi Arabia to lift restrictions on their territory,” Sheline said, adding that this explained their decision to welcome the Iranian commercial flight to Sanaa from Tehran on July 3, which carried a Houthi delegation to the funeral of Ayatollah Ali Khamenei.

Riyadh reportedly saw this flight as an attempt by the Houthis to re-establish logistical supply routes with Iran’s Revolutionary Guards. The group wants regular flights between Sanaa and Tehran and other airports, as well as unrestricted maritime traffic through the ports it controls.

When an Iranian airliner attempted to bring home the Houthi mourners on July 13, Sanaa airport was bombed to prevent it from landing. Yemen’s Saudi-led backed government formally claimed responsibility, but then Houthis blamed Riyadh.

‘Opening another front’

Baron said the current escalation in the Red Sea “broadly fits into a long-standing pattern of tensions rising as the Houthis aim to get further concessions on key issues”.

With hostilities between the US and Iran also mounting, “this is certainly an opportune time for the Houthis to join the battle by opening up another front”, he said.

Sheline believes the Houthis will likely impose a full closure of the Bab el-Mandeb if Trump “restarts an air war” against them. The US and Britain previously conducted air strikes on Houthi targets in retaliation for attacks on Red Sea shipping between 2024 and 2025.

Renewed fighting would pile further pressure on oil markets and disrupt imports of food and other essentials to Saudi Arabia via Red Sea ports, Sheline said.

With access to its Gulf port of Dammam largely cut off, Saudi Arabia is currently importing roughly 80 per cent of its food through Jeddah on the west coast.

Baron said both Riyadh and the UAE would “certainly prefer to avoid being sucked into a war” with the Houthis, particularly given that the Iran conflict “remains hot”.

Yet he warned that a Red Sea blockade “certainly constitutes a significant risk not just to their economic interests, but also national security”.

Should the US launch an air campaign against the Houthis, Sheline believes “Riyadh and Abu Dhabi may be willing to join a coalition against them”, in order to reopen the Bab el-Mandeb Strait sooner.

The looming threat of a wider conflict has already prompted tanker owners to begin re-routing cargoes from Saudi Arabia to Asian markets via the Suez Canal and Africa’s Cape of Good Hope, significantly increasing both delivery times and costs.

Saudi Aramco, the desert kingdom’s state oil giant, has meanwhile begun ramping up crude shipments via Egypt’s Mediterranean Sidi Kerir oil terminal, in a bid to reduce the vulnerability of its exports to Houthi attacks.

In the months since the US and Israel first attacked Iran on February 28, leading to Hormuz’s closure, much of the oil previously exported via Saudi Arabia’s Persian Gulf ports has been diverted through a pipeline to the Red Sea port of Yanbu.
