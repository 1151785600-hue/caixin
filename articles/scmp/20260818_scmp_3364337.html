Pentagon orders 30 US universities to scrutinise ties with Chinese research partners

SCMP | FREE | 1008 words | 2026-08-19

The Pentagon has ordered 30 US universities to conduct sweeping audits of their foreign research partnerships, including collaborations with Chinese institutions and organisations associated with former Confucius Institutes, or risk becoming ineligible for future federal funding.

The Department of Defence said on Monday that the unnamed universities must review their academic, financial and research relationships with foreign “entities of concern” and determine whether sensitive or export-controlled research had been exposed.

The institutions must report their findings and proposed mitigation measures – including the termination of partnerships deemed problematic – to the department by August 31, giving them two weeks to complete the reviews.

The department did not identify the universities or specify how many of the notifications concerned ties with China rather than Russia or Iran.

Responding to the announcement, a spokesperson for the Chinese embassy in Washington said China opposed “the overstretching of the concept of national security and the politicisation or instrumentalisation of normal scientific, educational and academic exchanges”.

“Scientific innovation and academic development require open cooperation; restricting normal research exchanges will ultimately harm the interests of all parties, including US researchers and institutions,” the spokesperson said.

“The US side should abandon the Cold War mentality and foster an open, fair and non-discriminatory environment for educational, scientific and people-to-people exchanges between China and the United States.”

The Pentagon did not respond to a request for comment.

The audits concern collaborations with institutions included on a list issued under Section 1286 of the 2019 National Defence Authorisation Act, as well as organisations the department described as being associated with “rebranded Confucius Institutes”.

The reference to Confucius Institutes – Chinese government-backed language and cultural programmes that faced mounting political scrutiny in the United States before many closed or changed names – places China at the centre of the latest research-security action.

“The Department of War has zero tolerance for academic partnerships that compromise our national security,” Emil Michael, undersecretary of war for research and engineering, said in announcing the audits, using the Trump administration’s preferred name for the Defence Department.

“Institutions that receive funding from American taxpayers must uphold the highest standards of research security, and these mandated audits will ensure accountability across the board.”

The department said the reviews were intended to protect taxpayer-funded research from unauthorised technology transfers, intellectual-property theft and exploitation by foreign adversaries.

Universities are “critical partners” in a broad range of Pentagon research programmes, said Joseph Jewell, assistant secretary of war for science and technology.

“The department must therefore ensure that our research investments are well protected from foreign exploitation,” he said.

In July, the Pentagon placed 130 universities and research organisations in China, Russia and Iran on its updated Section 1286 list, including 88 institutions in mainland China.

The list included some of China’s best-known civilian universities, such as Fudan University and Shanghai Jiao Tong University, alongside institutions with more explicit connections to the country’s military and defence establishment.

Other additions included Shandong University, Hangzhou Dianzi University, Shenyang Aerospace University, the University of International Relations and the Chinese military’s Joint Logistics Support Force Engineering University.

The Pentagon previously told the South China Morning Post that the determinations applied to entire institutions rather than particular laboratories, departments or individual researchers.

US researchers were advised to exercise “strict caution” before entering collaborations, funding arrangements or data-sharing relationships with institutions on the list.

Under the department’s research-security rules, Pentagon funding is generally prohibited for fundamental research projects that involve collaboration with a listed foreign institution. Existing relationships may be subject to risk reviews, while some non-fundamental research can continue under limited circumstances if it does not involve collaboration or equipment supplied by a listed entity.

Monday’s announcement marks a significant escalation from warning researchers about the foreign institutions to requiring American universities to examine – and potentially end – their relationships with them.

It remains unclear whether the 30 universities were selected because the Pentagon found evidence of unauthorised technology transfers or other misconduct, or merely because they maintained relationships with blacklisted institutions.

SCMP has asked the Association of American Universities whether any of its members received notifications and whether the August 31 deadline gives universities enough time to conduct the required audits.

The Department of Defence said it was pursuing the initiative with the House and Senate Armed Services and Appropriations committees and the House Select Committee on the Chinese Communist Party.

The announcement came four days after the House China committee and the House Education and Workforce Committee released a report accusing Harvard University of failing to adequately scrutinise relationships with Chinese institutions associated with the People’s Liberation Army and China’s defence-industrial sector.

The Republican-led investigation alleged that Harvard-affiliated researchers had co-written work with scholars at the National University of Defence Technology, Beihang University and Nanjing University of Science and Technology, among other Chinese institutions with military connections.

It also accused Harvard of lacking the centralised infrastructure needed to identify and screen foreign research partnerships.

The report was co-written by a congressional committee that has consistently advocated tighter restrictions on US academic and commercial engagement with China. Its findings do not establish that Harvard is among the 30 institutions notified by the Pentagon.

SCMP has asked Harvard whether it received a notification and sought its response to the congressional report.

The congressional committees recommended that Harvard centralise its research-security operations, overhaul its due-diligence procedures and expand its screening of international partnerships. They also urged Congress to enact legislation imposing more extensive disclosure and research-security requirements on universities.

The Pentagon did not say whether that investigation contributed to its decision to order the 30 audits or whether the universities had been recommended for review by congressional committees.

The department said the audits would help it anticipate emerging threats and respond to “evolving adversarial tactics” while preserving the United States’ technological advantage.

Critics of Washington’s expanding research-security restrictions have warned that overly broad rules could discourage legitimate scientific collaboration, stigmatise researchers of Chinese origin and weaken American universities’ access to international expertise.
