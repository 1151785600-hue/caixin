From kitchen waste to sky-high costs: inside Hong Kong’s quest for green jet fuel

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1979 words | 2026-10-10

China’s once-notorious “gutter oil”, recycled from kitchen and drainage waste, has almost vanished from dining tables in recent years and now carries the nickname liquid gold.

The substance has become a highly sought-after feedstock for producing clean energy, especially sustainable aviation fuel (SAF), with biofuel producers such as Hong Kong-based Ecoceres sourcing gutter oil across the Greater Bay Area.

EcoCeres’ co-chairman, Alan Chan Ying-lung, witnessed how the restaurant waste transformed from a nationwide food safety concern into a valuable commodity.

“I can recall that the gutter oil we collected was basically free 10 years ago, and authorities even offered subsidies [to collectors], but now the unit cost is even more expensive than fuel,” he said.

Gutter oil is among a selection of feedstocks, such as animal fat and agricultural waste, that can be used to produce renewable jet fuel, an alternative that will reduce life-cycle carbon emissions by up to 80 per cent compared with conventional jet fuel.

SAF, a “drop-in” substitute, can be safely blended with jet fuel and used in aircraft engines without modification, but the challenge is the price.

The Hong Kong government has set a 2030 target for the use of SAF, but has yet to clarify who will pay for it.

“Producers need confidence that demand will be there. Airlines need fuel that is available and affordable. Investors need clarity on the direction of policy. A well-designed policy can connect these needs,” the CEO of Hong Kong flag carrier Cathay Pacific Airways, Ronald Lam Siu-por, said at a clean jet fuel conference in September.

A ‘cautious’ target

The city government initially set a goal for SAF to account for between 1 and 2 per cent of all fuel consumed by flights departing from Hong Kong International Airport by 2030.

But in the city’s first five-year plan released in September, Chief Executive John Lee Ka-chiu revised the target to 1 to 3 per cent. He also said the city would aim to develop an internationally competitive SAF value chain in the bay area.

Niki Wang Yangwen, Asia-Pacific associate director of biofuels at S&P Global Energy, said Hong Kong’s aim was considered achievable, with Asian economies generally setting targets of 1 to 5 per cent for 2030.

“From our conversations with the market, the general feeling is that 1 to 3 per cent is a fairly cautious target, and people do not have much concern about it,” she said of the city’s goal.

Some industry players expected the city to be a trial site for mainland China to gauge market reactions and, if things worked out, gradually roll out similar measures, she explained.

Hong Kong, like Singapore, is a key aviation hub in Asia, with a dominant home carrier and mostly international traffic, Wang said.

Hong Kong’s airport is one of the busiest in the world, last year recording 61 million passenger trips and a sector-leading 5.07 million tonnes of cargo throughput.

According to the Civil Aviation Department, the number of flights departing from Hong Kong, including cargo and passenger services, jumped by 8.6 per cent to 197,374 in 2025 from a year earlier, while the number in 2024 was 181,663, up by 31.6 per cent from 138,056 in 2023.

In addition, 197,325 flights arrived in 2025, representing an 8.7 per cent increase from the previous year.

Hong Kong is not alone in setting green fuel targets. Elsewhere, demand for SAF is being driven more by mandates than pledges by airlines.

The International Civil Aviation Organisation has adopted a collective goal to cut global aviation emissions by 5 per cent by 2030 on the path to net zero by 2050.

The European Union requires fuel suppliers at its airports to use SAF, starting at 2 per cent of overall fuel in 2025, rising to 6 per cent in 2030 and to 70 per cent by 2050.

Singapore has confirmed a goal of SAF accounting for 1 per cent of overall fuel by 2027 and 3 to 5 per cent by 2030, subject to global developments and SAF availability, while other Asian countries, including Thailand, Japan and South Korea, have also set targets.

How can Hong Kong achieve its goal?

In its latest policy address, the government committed to studying a mandatory consumption mechanism, and launching targeted publicity and educational campaigns by 2028. It will also develop a local blending facility to mix neat SAF with conventional jet fuel into a ready-to-use supply to support uptake.

Separately, Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing’s Core Climate carbon marketplace will explore offering green energy and other environmental equity products to the market, including SAF certificates and green hydrogen certificates.

In a reply to the South China Morning Post, the Transport and Logistics Bureau and the Environment and Ecology Bureau said the local blending facility was expected to begin operations by 2030.

“This will complete an end-to-end regional value chain, streamline logistics and enhance SAF price competitiveness,” the bureaus said.

As part of the broader value chain in the bay area, the government has also spearheaded the establishment of EcoCeres’ SAF production base in Dongguan, which will begin production by 2030, producing 450,000 tonnes of SAF and renewable diesel per year at full capacity.

EcoCeres’ Chan said the company’s current production of about 770,000 to 800,000 tonnes, well above the 3 per cent requirement of around 200,000 tonnes, was manageable.

“I also believe we will not be the sole supplier. There will be other suppliers in a fair market, so I don’t think there will be any pressure on the overall supply, even at 3 per cent or up to 10 per cent,” he said.

EcoCeres, a clean energy unicorn backed by the family office of Henderson Land Development co-chairman Peter Lee Ka-kit, is the world’s second-biggest producer of SAF, after Finland’s Neste.

Chan said the supply chain was end-to-end, with Dongguan as its starting point. Used cooking oil is collected from nearby bay area cities and pre-processed in the area, while Hong Kong handles blending, research and development, and financing.

Who pays the extra cost?

Chan said the government should have a longer-term plan beyond 2030 and provide details of its mandatory mechanism, noting that the EU had set goals for 2050.

Merlin Lao Man-chi, the head of policy and research at the Business Environment Council, said the government’s mandatory mechanism was crucial, as it would determine market confidence and who would ultimately bear the cost.

“It is important to determine the cost allocation and who will be in charge of the cost as it could scale up the whole industry,” he said.

According to the International Air Transport Association (IATA), global SAF production is expected to reach 2.4 million tonnes in 2026, representing just 0.8 per cent of aviation fuel use, at a cost of US$4.3 billion to airlines.

The South China Morning Post contacted four Hong Kong-based airlines – Cathay, its budget arm HK Express, Hong Kong Airlines and Greater Bay Airlines – but none offered any comment.

Singapore introduced the world’s first green levy, with passengers bearing the cost, while the EU holds suppliers responsible through penalties.

Lao said the government should offer short-term subsidies, but it would also need to determine how to allocate costs in the longer run.

He said the levy mechanism in Singapore helped encourage acceptance among consumers, but such a move would be “a long shot” in Hong Kong and the government would need to work to garner broad public support.

Lao used Hong Kong’s ill-fated waste-charging scheme in 2024 as an example. Officials and lawmakers initially had good thoughts initially and all agreed on the proposal. But when it came to implementation, residents opposed the measure and the scheme was eventually scrapped.

Asked whether imposing a levy would threaten the city’s status as an international aviation hub, Lao said the fee could be imposed only on passengers or cargo departing from Hong Kong, not those in transit.

Hong Kong’s airport currently charges a HK$200 (US$25) departure tax. In the past few months, airlines have added fuel surcharges on the back of price hikes caused by the Middle East war.

“The mechanism will ensure that we do it first and decide the transshipment and transition later, as they are very price sensitive,” Lao said, noting that a green levy would also hedge against future price volatility.

Lawmaker Chan Siu-hung said he was not worried that the aviation industry would lose customers, as the city’s airport should fight for its own “target customers”.

“Everyone has to understand that protecting the environment comes at a cost. If we do not pay it today, our children and grandchildren will have to pay it in the future,” he said.

However, Chan argued that the government should not offer subsidies because Hong Kong relied on both local and international airlines. Subsidising local airlines could be regarded as unfair competition, while taxpayers’ money should not be used to fund foreign airlines, he said.

Alan Chan of EcoCeres said the extra cost of SAF should be seen as “green value” rather than a “green premium” as each tonne of its fuel would cut life-cycle greenhouse gas emissions by up to 94.4 per cent compared with conventional jet fuel depending on feedstock composition. This was a benefit that everyone would enjoy, he argued.

“If you do not realise it is a value, the product itself will have no value,” he said. “Why would you pay more for the same thing?”

How to lower the price of SAF?

The IATA estimated that the price of SAF would surge to US$2,872 per tonne in 2026, about 2.5 times the price of jet fuel, while S&P Global Energy’s data showed that the SAF price in China was US$2,340 per tonne on October 6.

Lawmaker Chan said scaling operations was essential to bringing down SAF costs.

“Hong Kong is a small market without the scale or critical mass, but the Greater Bay Area has many airports, and their combined demand can reach critical mass,” he said.

If there was a clear demand for SAF, the region’s many facilities and suppliers would naturally be willing to invest in scaling, he added.

The legislator said the next step was developing a carbon trading market that could help bring down costs as the industry scaled up.

One example, he said, was the SAF certificate mentioned in the policy address: airlines could trade the certificates in the market, while companies short of carbon credits could buy them.

Samuel Wong, Asia-Pacific vice-president for sales at PAC LP, a technology supplier in the United States, said used cooking oil alone would not be sufficient to produce SAF on the scale the aviation industry would eventually require.

“Used cooking oil can be turned into SAF, but it can also be used to produce biodiesel and other renewable fuels, so different sectors are competing for the same limited supply,” Wong said. “That competition has pushed prices above US$1,000 per tonne in some markets.”

He said the supply of used cooking oil was inherently limited, meaning the industry would have to develop alternative feedstocks and production technologies as SAF demand increased.

“There are fundamental constraints on both supply and cost,” Wong said. “The amount of used cooking oil that can be sustainably collected is finite, so used cooking oil alone cannot provide all the feedstock needed to scale SAF globally.”

HEFA, or hydroprocessed esters and fatty acids, is currently the main commercial pathway used for producing SAF and uses feedstocks including used cooking oil, vegetable oils and animal fats.

But Wong said achieving much larger SAF volumes would require the industry to diversify beyond HEFA.

“HEFA is essential for scaling SAF today, but it cannot be the only solution,” he said. “The industry needs a portfolio of pathways.”

Lawmaker Chan said Hong Kong needed a detailed action plan beyond 2030.

“[The government] needs a mandatory target by 2030, and then have a clear direction in 2035 to raise the target along the way,” he said.
