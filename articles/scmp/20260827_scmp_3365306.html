Is ‘resource curse’ to blame in regions hit harder by Xi’s anti-corruption campaign?

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2258 words | 2026-08-27

The South China Morning Post has launched a major data project diving into President Xi Jinping’s signature anti-graft campaign. In the third part of a series accompanying the data project, William Zheng looks at the relationship between rich regional resources and corruption.

Beneath the desolate Gobi Desert and sparsely populated mountains of China’s less developed regions lie abundant mineral resources, ranging from coal, the most basic fuel for China’s economy, to critical minerals needed for cutting-edge industrial machinery and state-of-the-art military products.

These regions are not only rich in natural resources critical to the world’s second-largest economy, but also appear to be fertile ground for deep-seated corruption.

Research by the South China Morning Post shows that China’s resource-rich and border regions have been the hardest hit in the country’s decade-long corruption crackdown, as Beijing tries to avoid succumbing to regional “resource curses”.

The SCMP research examined every detention notice, investigation conclusion and career path of “tigers”, or high-ranking officials, released by the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection (CCDI) – China’s top anti-corruption and political supervision agency – from 2013 to 2025.

It shows that China’s mineral-abundant regions, including Jiangxi, Liaoning, Inner Mongolia, Shanxi and Hebei, top the chart for the most senior officials sacked for corruption in that period.

The research also finds that the highest per capita toll is among the leadership of regions receiving the highest per capita financial support, including the country’s largest ethnic minority autonomous regions.

Observers say the results suggest Chinese regions with rich natural resources or that receive large sums in financial support from the central government are more susceptible to so-called rent-seeking, thus drawing fire from anti-graft fighters in Beijing.

The numbers

Jiangxi province, which is immensely abundant in natural resources, especially rare earth minerals and tungsten, was home to the highest number of senior corruption crackdowns, with 15 tigers, according to the tally.

Among them was Su Rong, the former Communist Party boss of several provinces, including Jiangxi. He was sentenced to life in prison in 2017 for taking more than 116 million yuan (US$17.3 million) in bribes in exchange for granting promotions, government contracts and land rights, making him the highest-ranking official taken down during the early years of the anti-corruption fight. Mainland Chinese media reported in 2014 that Su’s wife had also dabbled in Jiangxi’s mineral resources, land transfers and real estate development.

Su’s conviction triggered a domino effect within the regions he previously governed, particularly Jiangxi, where a sweeping purge followed, leading to the arrest or demotion of dozens of local officials in his network.

Liaoning – which has the country’s largest reserves of iron ore, magnesite and boron, as well as large deposits of critical minerals for modern manufacturing and clean energy, such as vanadium, zinc, molybdenum and manganese – ranked second with 14 deputy provincial or higher-ranked officials facing corruption investigations.

Like Jiangxi, Liaoning also saw the downfall of its party boss, Wang Min, who served in the position from 2009 to 2015. On July 7, 2014, after its first investigation of Liaoning, Beijing’s inspection team reported “prominent problems”, such as “leading cadres interfering in project bidding, land and mineral resource transactions”.

Wang was removed the following year and investigated in 2016. He was handed a life sentence in 2017 for taking 146 million yuan in bribes, embezzlement and dereliction of duty.

Beijing’s inspectors also said they unearthed vote-rigging in Liaoning’s legislature in 2014. Some 523 of 619 deputies in the province’s people’s congress were implicated in the scandal, either by taking bribes or allowing the vote-rigging.

Inner Mongolia, Shanxi – which is also among China’s leading coal-production zones – and Hebei, the country’s foremost ferrous metal hub, tied for third, each with 13 tigers hunted down.

Actions in Inner Mongolia were roundly criticised under the unprecedented “looking back 20 years” anti-graft initiative launched by Beijing in early 2020. Under the initiative, the resource-rich autonomous region – home to more than a quarter of China’s coal reserves – became the central target because of an extraordinary concentration of wealth and a frenzy in the resale of state-owned mineral resources at artificially low prices, as well as illicit rent-seeking opportunities in coal and other mining sectors.

Before the push, the region saw the downfall of a string of top officials in the late 2010s, including Yun Guangzhong, the former party secretary of the capital, Hohhot, former regional security boss Xing Yun and deputy government chairman Bai Xiangqun, revealing a deeply entrenched, multi-decade network of government-business collusion.

Under the initiative, Beijing issued an unprecedented retrospective order to Inner Mongolia: audit every single coal mining transaction, lease and regulatory approval stretching back to 2000.

In a campaign review in November 2021, authorities investigated more than 730 corruption cases in Inner Mongolia’s coal sector alone, with 1,023 officials facing investigations and more than 52 billion yuan of the economic losses recovered.

Earlier in Chinese President Xi Jinping’s anti-graft push, Shanxi was the first coal economy shaken up by graft fighters from Beijing.

During China’s “Golden Decade of Coal” from 2002 to 2012, when coal mining firms gained tremendously from skyrocketing coal prices, Shanxi produced about a third of the nation’s coal.

However, the energy boom turned the province into the epicentre of what the Chinese anti-corruption body called “avalanche-style corruption” – a state of total institutional decay where entire government networks were co-opted by private mine owners, known as the “coal bosses”, or mei laoban in Chinese.

State media said the nexus between local mine inspectors, police, county officials and mine owners created a lethal moral hazard. When fatal mine explosions or cave-ins occurred, local officials routinely helped private mine owners cover up casualty information, aided by hush money and forged reports, to prevent being shut down by the central government.

By 2014, Beijing said graft in Shanxi was no longer about individual bad apples, with the CCDI sending teams to Shanxi with unprecedented authority.

What followed was a historic near-complete downfall of a provincial political establishment: Beijing almost effectively removed Shanxi’s entire top leadership and parachuted in external replacements to clean house from the top down.

In 2014, seven incumbent or former senior officials from the top provincial leadership were removed, an extremely high number for any province during the anti-corruption drive, ranging from the province’s top economic officials, party affairs chief, top anti-graft fighters and heads of the largest cities in the province.

Ling Zhengce, the elder brother of former Chinese presidential aide Ling Jihua, was brought down from his post as the vice-chairman of Shanxi province’s political advisory body the same year.

Wang Rulin, who was parachuted into Shanxi in 2014 as provincial party boss to clean up the mess, said in March 2015 that the sweeping anti-corruption fight resulted in nearly 300 vacancies at the deputy municipal level and above.

There is no sign that the CCDI will lessen its pressure and attention towards resource-rich provinces.

During the past five CCDI annual plenums, the national gathering of Xi and China’s top disciplinary officials has repeatedly stressed their intensifying efforts to combat corruption in areas where “power, funds and resources are concentrated”.

‘The resource curse’

In keeping with a phenomenon known as “the resource curse” or “paradox of plenty”, abundant resources do not guarantee better growth, more equitable income distribution and clearer government, according to Alfred Wu, associate professor at the Lee Kuan Yew School of Public Policy in Singapore.

While such a concept was mainly discussed at the national level – countries with plentiful non-renewable natural resources such as oil, gas, gold and minerals tend to experience less economic growth and worse governance than countries with fewer resources – it could be applied to regions or provinces within the same country, Wu said.

In a resource-rich region, the local government tended to fund itself by collecting “rents”, royalties or revenues directly from mining companies in contracts involving large amounts of money, and the local leading officials could have a strong influence, he added.

“The easiest way to get rich in a resource-cursed province isn’t to build a great business with a great invention or a new technology; it’s to secure a government contract. That encourages rent-seeking and shifts the region’s brightest minds to political plundering.”

Xie Maosong, a senior researcher at the National Institute of Strategic Studies at Tsinghua University, said the campaign not only shattered the unspoken “safe landing” rule in which officials were not prosecuted for past corruption after retiring, but also marked the first time the CCDI had targeted entire critical industrial supply chains.

“The total-cleansing model pioneered in Inner Mongolia’s coal sector has since been replicated across other strategic areas, including the financial sector, the national healthcare apparatus and the judicial-security complex,” Xie said.

Xie also noted that despite Xi naming environmental protection as a top priority in his policy metric, in Inner Mongolia, corrupt officials routinely turned a blind eye to massive, illegal open-pit mining operations that caused severe desertification and ecological destruction, while local cadres falsified environmental audits to keep the coal revenue flowing.

“By breaking the political stranglehold of the coal money, Shanxi finally began shifting its monoculture economy away from pure mine extraction, redirecting state investment towards clean energy transitions, like solar, hydrogen, coal-bed methane, hi-tech manufacturing and cultural tourism,” he said.

“That’s unimaginable without the crackdown.”

Border and minority regions

The SCMP analysis found that the Tibet autonomous region, Qinghai, Hainan, the Ningxia Hui autonomous region and Inner Mongolia had the highest number of corruption cases relative to their population size. Some of these regions are also among the biggest per capita recipients of central government financial support – measured in transfer payments from Beijing.

China’s central government transfer payments to provinces are massive fiscal allocations – consistently exceeding 10 trillion yuan annually – used to bridge regional fiscal gaps, helping poorer regions gain parity in basic public services such as education, healthcare, and social security.

They also include targeted funds for specific national policies and infrastructure projects, helping cash-strapped local governments fund their development needs.

According to data released by the Ministry of Finance, Tibet received 74,700 yuan per capita in transfer payments in 2025, far exceeding other regions. This figure was 2.55 times that of second-ranked Qinghai’s 29,300 yuan per capita, with Xinjiang ranked third on 20,170 yuan.

The other top regions with high per capita transfer payments were Ningxia, Heilongjiang, Inner Mongolia, Gansu, Jilin, Hainan and Guizhou – all border provinces that are less developed. Besides Qinghai and Ningxia, the remainder received more than 10,000 yuan transfer payments per capita.

Singapore’s Alfred Wu said management of the large transfer payments had long been a problem in China, particularly in less developed regions where a few top officials could dictate the use of this massive amount of money.

“Since 2015, the State Council started a series of reform measures aiming at reducing wastage and strengthening supervision. But even with that, we still see corruption cases where local officials in less developed provinces frequently embezzled or misappropriated funds intended for poverty alleviation, agricultural subsidies, basic medical care and basic social security,” he said.

China’s border and autonomous regions with large minority populations have also been home to the downfall of a string of their ethnic-Han party bosses and ethnic-minority government heads in the past few years.

The most prominent downfalls include that of Ma Xingrui, a Politburo member and the former party boss of Xinjiang in April and, in 2024, the former party chief of Tibet Wu Yingjie, who had spent 47 years in the autonomous region. In July last year, Wu was sentenced to death, suspended for two years, after pleading guilty to accepting 343 million yuan in bribes.

More ethnic minority government heads are facing corruption investigations. In January 2025, Qizhala, the ethnic-Tibetan former chairman of the Tibet autonomous region, was investigated, followed a few months later in May by a probe into Lan Tianli, the ethnic-Zhuang chairman of Guangxi Zhuang autonomous region.

In July, Liu Hui, the ethnic-Hui former chairwoman of Ningxia Hui autonomous region, came under investigation for alleged corruption, just as Qizhala was being expelled from the party.

A Chinese political scientist who declined to be named due to the sensitivity of the matter, said the anti-corruption campaign in border and minority regions, such as Xinjiang, Tibet, Inner Mongolia, Guangxi, and Yunnan, had given Beijing “opportunities to put in place trusted top officials to carry out the new ethnic policies”.

“Many Xinjiang and Tibet officials facing investigation were accused of being ‘two-faced’, meaning they publicly echo Beijing’s directives but privately sympathise with local religious movements, protect local traditions against assimilation, or show ‘softness’ in enforcing hardline security measures,” the political scientist said.

“Removing these Janus-faced officials allowed Beijing to put in place a slew of iron-fisted tough administrators to carry out Beijing’s ethnic integration plan.”

Beijing named and shamed former Xinjiang education chief Sattar Sawut and justice department chief Shirzat Bawudun in 2021 as typical “two-faced” officials.

Sawut was deemed to have taken advantage of the Han officials who were not proficient in the Uygur language to publish local textbooks “detrimental to national unity and ethnic solidarity”.

Bawudun was accused of “using his security and justice position as cover” and having “planned and carried out separatist activities”. Both were charged with separatism and each was handed a suspended death sentence in 2021.
