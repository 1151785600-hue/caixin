How a fuel crisis is threatening Bangladesh’s reform goals: ‘it hit our citizens hard’

SCMP | FREE | 1003 words | 2026-10-15

Faced with a fuel crisis beyond its control, Bangladesh’s government is spending the equivalent of hundreds of millions of US dollars each month to insulate its people from surging prices.

But as growth slows and inflation rises, the new government’s reform plans are being tested before it has had much time to clean up the country’s troubled financial institutions or retool its young, abundant – but underemployed – workforce for the AI era.

Prime Minister Tarique Rahman took office on February 17, elected on promises of sweeping economic and social reforms to turn the country of 175 million people into a US$1 trillion economy within a decade.

An interim government had steered the nation through the treacherous months following former leader Sheikh Hasina’s downfall in 2024. She was ousted by a student-led revolt after what they called 15 years of repression, corruption and bad governance.

Her critics say those years saw cronies freely dip into state banks, stuffing them with bad loans and leaving the healthcare and education systems in tatters, all while filling the civil service with loyalists to her Awami League party rather than competent administrators.

But just 11 days after Rahman – a scion of a rival political clan to Hasina – was sworn in, the US and Israel attacked Iran, sending the price of oil and gas skyrocketing upwards.

In Bangladesh, which imported about 65 per cent of its energy needs last year, that has meant power cuts, long queues at petrol stations and rising costs for everything from transport to food.

“Energy is not just buying oil, gas or fertiliser,” Rashed Al Mahmud Titumir, an adviser to Rahman, told This Week in Asia on the sidelines of the IMF–World Bank summit in Bangkok.

“It hit our citizens hard,” he said. “Our estimates are that from late February [to now] we have spent an estimated US$4.1 billion in additional costs.”

Some of the impact should have been cushioned by solar power, especially in a nation increasingly scorched by global warming.

But “the previous government didn’t build any sustainable energy security infrastructure”, Titumir said, part of a legacy of years of mismanagement and corruption.

The World Bank and Asian Development Bank have both provided hundreds of millions of US dollars in loans to help lighten the government’s energy cost burden.

The IMF, which has about US$1 trillion in loan capacity, has also dispersed nearly US$3.6 billion to Bangladesh since 2023 – a loan Rahman’s government is hoping to renegotiate this week in Bangkok.

But as the outside pressures build, the International Monetary Fund on Tuesday trimmed Bangladesh’s growth projection for 2027 to 3.2 per cent from 3.6 per cent this year, placing it behind Cambodia and climate-battered Nepal among developing Asian nations.

It also forecast inflation to nudge upwards to 10.9 per cent in 2027 from 8.7 per cent this year – although Bangladesh’s government said both projections had not fully taken into account the impact of its structural reforms of financial institutions, indebted businesses and a hollowed out welfare state.

“Unfortunately the people are suffering for the misdeeds of the previous regime,” Titumir said. “We have already brought five banks to be owned by the government … after fears there will be a bank run, that has now calmed. We want to bring discipline into the system [and] then provide finance for investment, finance for expansion.”

He said future growth would sprout from foreign investment into new manufacturing – including electronics and toys – infrastructure, solar power, deep-sea ports and new special economic zones, including the US$1.3 billion China-backed zone in Anwara, southern Bangladesh.

Bangladesh also has a young population, deep experience in manufacturing – including in its key garment industry – and a rich seam of cheap workers available to be tapped by firms looking to relocate from Southeast Asia, where costs are rising.

But questions remain over how Bangladesh will equip itself with the energy, skills and infrastructure needed to capitalise on the AI revolution that now undergirds much of global growth.

The IMF’s message in Bangkok this week pointed to a two-speed world economy: one weighed down by sky-high energy prices and another propelled by the trillions of dollars invested into AI and its spin-off industries, from data centres to semiconductors and printed circuit boards.

“Who is taking the biggest hit? Low-income countries. Especially those who are energy importers or left out from technology boom,” IMF managing director Kristalina Georgieva said on Wednesday.

She hailed Asia as “the engine of global growth” but warned nations hit by the energy crisis to run a tight ship, despite the political temptations to roll out sweeping subsidies.

“First protect your buffers, protect your fiscal space,” she said, advising governments to give out “temporary and targeted support to the most vulnerable”.

Bangladesh, South Asia’s second-largest economy, is now at a turning point.

Rahman’s Bangladesh National Party government swept to power with a two-thirds majority in February’s election on a platform of reform, but it now faces a populace anxious for immediate change after the toxic Hasina era.

His family has traded power with Hasina’s clan for much of the South Asian nation’s short but turbulent history.

The 57-year-old returned from years in self-exile in London to successfully contest this year’s election, following a transition period overseen by an interim government, led by Nobel laureate Muhammad Yunus, which navigated the chaotic and violent aftermath of Hasina’s ousting by student protesters.

“We have delivered in the past,’’ added Titumir, the prime minister’s adviser on finance and planning, noting that Rahman’s “political approval” had handed his administration a clear mandate for reform.

Rahman’s mother, Khaleda Zia, served three terms as prime minister between 1991 and 2006. She was detained several times for her political activism during a decades-long power struggle with Hasina – the daughter of independence icon Sheikh Mujibur Rahman.

Zia died last year. Her husband, the former army commander and war hero Ziaur Rahman, was assassinated in 1981 after leading the country for five years.
