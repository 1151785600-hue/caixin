Making tracks through China’s past and present

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2440 words | 2026-08-29

“Peking wore the green lace of spring, its thousands of willows and imperial cypresses making the Forbidden City a place of wonder and enchantment,” American journalist Edgar Snow wrote of Beijing in 1936.

Ninety years later, strolling before the vermillion ramparts of the Forbidden City, it’s clear that Beijing’s mass conversion to electric vehicles has rendered the city not only cleaner than it has been of late, but also quieter – allowing Zhongshan Park to bloom much as Snow described.

Snow’s description was included in his wartime account of a journey to Yanan, Red Star Over China (first published in 1937), his destination then being the base of the Chinese Communist Party. But the city has made headlines much more recently, with the December opening of the Xian-Yanan high-speed railway taking the length of China Railway’s operational network past the 50,000km (31,070 miles) mark.

It took Snow more than two days to cover the 1,000km (621 miles) from Beijing to Xian by rail, but China’s 21st century public transport will make my own pilgrimage to Yanan, with some history-imbued detours along the way, far smoother.

With tickets bought online, I set my sights first on Jinan, the capital of Shandong province, located on the upper reaches of the Yellow River and 400km (249 miles) to the south of Beijing.

At the massive, oval-domed Beijing South station, I board a G-class train that makes light work of the Central Plain, rocketing across an area the size of Paraguay characterised by undulating wheat and rapeseed-flower fields. It takes just over two hours to reach the far side of the mighty Yellow River, a reminder that Beijing was one of the few ancient Chinese cities that were not fed by the “mother river”.

Jinan’s urban district stands to the south of the river. First impressions upon arrival at dusk are of a typical second-tier city composed chiefly of modern high-rises strung together by flyovers. My mood isn’t much improved by the hotel I pre-booked, which is located down a gloomy Shizhong district alley, past street-side barbecue joints populated by drunks, with electrical wires dangling overhead and between adverts for spas and KTV (karaoke television) lounges. The labyrinth of hotel corridors has tobacco-stained carpets and room service comes courtesy of R2-D2-like robots.

Morning illuminates Jinan in a better light.

A short walk leads to Wulongtan (“Five Dragon Pond”), a classical garden comprising several natural springs. It was mentioned in the Spring and Autumn Annals, the official chronicle of the state of Lu (1042BC-249BC), which existed here before the unification of China under Qin Shi Huang.

Bamboo-lined pathways lead between concave-roofed pavilions and goldfish ponds sprinkled with water lilies. Some park-goers are writing classical verses in calligraphic characters on the ground with giant brushes dipped in water, while a group of aunties practises a traditional sword dance beside a man who, unfazed by their music, drifts through the elegant postures of tai chi.

Wulongtan is one of several public gardens fed by spring water that dominate Jinan and help explain its motto, splashed on numerous billboards: “City of springs, no worries for people”.

To the north, the Jinan Baili Huanghe Scenic Area flanks the south bank of the Yellow River, which, in truth, is more of a coffee colour. Here, elders stretch while courting couples watch the sunset from viewing points along the river’s edges. Farmers still cultivate the muddy riverbanks, using nutrient-rich loess soil that has flowed down from Shaanxi province to feed rows of green vegetables, as they and their ancestors have done since the dawn of Chinese civilisation.

Following the riverfront path eastwards leads to the Luokou Yellow River Railway Bridge. Conventional rolling stock still uses this century-old, German-built viaduct, which was completed in 1912.

Also of German vintage in Jinan are a few villas, a colonial-era post office and the former station house on the Jiaoji Railway – Shandong’s first railway, which connected with the port of Qingdao.

Jiaoji Railway Station, which now houses a museum, was completed in 1915 and built in early European Renaissance Revival style. It is distinguished by a prominent central colonnade beneath a large clock. The line itself has been modernised and no longer leaves from the station-turned-museum, but from Daminghu station, near Daming Lake Park.

Unfortunately, the art nouveau Jinan Railway Station depicted in the museum, and which stood 500 metres (1,640 feet) from Jiaoji station, came to be regarded as “a symbol of colonialism” and was replaced by an unexceptional building in 1992. It’s from here that I depart for Taian, just a 20-minute hop away by high-speed rail.

Taian would be an unremarkable provincial city were it not for a mountain considered so sacred for so many centuries, it has assumed the status of a god.

Mount Tai is the easternmost of China’s nine Daoist peaks, attracting up to a million pilgrims a year, who attempt to follow the emperors’ journey 8km (five miles) up the mountainside, paying homage to Heaven and Earth, at the summit and foot of the mountain, respectively.

I set out early to beat the crowds, but my 6am hotel alarm call is not early enough; everyone else has made the same calculation. It’s not so much a hike, then, as a communal walk along stone steps carved out of the mountainside as far back as Qin times, some two millennia ago.

Amid cypress trees and gushing streams, every rock we pass has been inscribed with poetic verse and it becomes apparent that the passage from the First Heavenly Gate to Jade Emperor Peak owes its glories – the temples and pavilions – to the patronage of the imperial court.

Past the Immortal Bridge, a natural formation of three huge boulders, the ascent to the Nantian Gate, some 1,500 metres (4,920 feet) above sea level, funnels hundreds of walkers between two walls of rock. Almost everyone is grasping the handrails as they haul themselves upwards at this point, cries of “jia you” (“add oil”) ringing out as pilgrims encourage one another to keep going.

There’s more than a sense of achievement to reward those who make it to the top of the mountain, where Tianjie (“Heaven Street”), a tourist strip with plenty of restaurants, awaits hungry hikers.

Unashamedly, I take the cable car back down to Earth, and the following day, I take the morning train to Kaifeng, in Henan province. There’s no fast service between the two cities, so I’m relegated to a second-class bunk in a half-full carriage, a sign that conventional rail might be dying out in the age of high-speed.

My fellow passengers are all tall northerners, their skin cracked by the sun of the high latitudes. Men in leather jackets and tightly fitting shirts walk back and forth along the gangway at 20-minute intervals, to find somewhere to smoke; women fondle their Xiaomi phones. Over seven hours of rail travel, there’s little to do but meditate on China’s heartland passing gently by while sipping tea and dipping into Red Star Over China.

Kaifeng is depicted in one of China’s great masterworks, Along the River During the Qingming Festival, a 12th century scroll painting that hangs in Beijing’s Palace Museum, as the Forbidden City is officially known, and reveals the bustling street markets and classical architecture of the capital of the Northern Song dynasty (960-1127) in its imperial heyday. The view from my taxi suggests the contemporary prefecture-level city is a few centuries past its prime: Soviet-style tenements jockey for space with modern towers, all of which appear to have adopted a colour code inspired by the muddy waters of the Yellow River.

The roads are the domain of e-bikes and e-trikes, which buzz like hornets around a nest within the refurbished city walls that frame the former capital.

In the middle of an anarchic roundabout stands the Drum Tower – three layers of green-tiled roofs atop a grey-brick foundation, the result of a complete 2013 rebuild – looking regal amid the shabby storefronts and chain hotels of downtown Kaifeng. At night, the eaves of the tower are illuminated with LED lights as it becomes the focus of a market. Hundreds of carts are pushed from side streets, to congregate around the tower, and from them issue the hissing and sizzling of cooking oil, with notes of garlic, ginger and pork fat, as diners feast on crispy wheat cakes and mutton noodle soup.

Kaifeng might not ooze the kind of laid-back prosperity I experienced in Jinan, but what it lacks in polish, it makes up for with heritage.

From the Old Town’s central access, Shudianjie (“Bookshop Street”), where some time-honoured book vendors are still in business, it’s easy to walk to some of the chief attractions, including the Shanshangan Guild Hall, a social hub established by northern merchants in the late 18th century.

Flanked by its own mini drum and bell towers, the hall has traditional roofing, the eaves of which have been decorated with lively scenes from the lives of travelling merchants and other wood carvings. One, in the Eastern Hall, depicts a man being dragged along the ground by his horse; others show golden bats, a symbol of luck, beneath animals and birds frolicking among bunches of grapes.

A 10-minute walk away, the Buddhist Daxiangguo Temple is still populated by robed monks 1,500 years after its founding. Watching the faithful prostrate themselves before Arhat statues at the back of the Main Hall, I’m reminded that train travel in China can be as much about time as it is space.

Kaifeng’s principal reminder of its Northern Song heyday is the 1,000-year-old Iron Pagoda, which towers 56 metres (184 feet) over the northeastern corner of the walled city. It is not made of iron but earned its name from the patterned glazed Buddhist tiles on its exterior, which give the tower the russet tones of rusted iron.

Despite Kaifeng’s plethora of historical marvels, it’s the tumble-down backstreets, where the rhythms of life remain unchanged by China’s high-speed dash towards a neon-hued future, that are hardest to bid farewell to when I depart from Kaifeng North station, bound for Xian.

Snow was received by the Kuomintang governor of the city when he visited Xian. I’m met with crowds pushing onto Metro Line 2, eager to get to the walled city centre. Yet, on emerging from Zhonglou station, which lies beneath the Bell Tower, it is impossible not to be impressed with what was, after all, the capital of 13 dynasties. Xian has the panache of somewhere that mattered, and mattered for a very long time – a self-confidence evident in the swagger of its denizens as well as the power architecture: the enormous Soviet-style Bell Tower Hotel, facing the Bell Tower; the “twin Tang towers” to the south of the city – the Giant Wild Goose Pagoda and Small Wild Goose Pagoda; the enormous walls and the broad boulevards that stitch it all together. Everything is on a scale that is only really rivalled by Beijing.

Having scarcely encountered another foreigner since leaving the capital, it’s something of a culture shock to witness legions of Western tourists, most of whom are taking advantage of the 30-day visa exemption policy to strike Qin Shi Huang’s Terracotta Army off their bucket list.

There again, Xian was the capital of the cosmopolitan Tang dynasty (618-907) and long served as the eastern gate to the Silk Road. This multicultural history is reflected in the Tang-era Great Mosque, which stands off a narrow, covered alley filled with vendors of tourist tat. One of the largest mosques in China, it was established in 742, although heavily restored during the Qing dynasty (1644-1911), and integrates Arabic features into a familiar Chinese design. Attracting mostly religious tourists from Central and Southeast Asia, the mosque’s tranquillity stands in contrast to the adjoining Muslim Quarter, which gets my vote for the finest street food in East Asia.

After braving the throngs several times for bowls of biang biang noodles, barbecued naan bread and persimmon doughnuts, I feel sufficiently energised to complete my railway quest.

Snow travelled north through fields of opium poppies that “nodded their swollen heads, ready for harvest, along the newly completed motor road” before sleeping “in a filthy hut […] with pigs and donkeys quartered in the next room”. I, on the other hand, cruise across the Loess Plateau at 250km/h (155mph) in an air-conditioned carriage.

The Yan River, a major tributary of the Yellow, bisects Yanan. The waterway and city are best viewed from Pagoda Hill, atop which stands the 44-metre (144 feet) Baota Pagoda, an octagonal brick tower that dates back to Tang times. Most of Yanan’s other tourist sites recall events that took place 90 years ago.

I taxi to the Date Garden, which was the site of the Communist Party Secretariat from October 1943 to March 1947. As well as various war bureaus, the former residences of officials, including Mao Zedong, Zhu De, Liu Shaoqi and Zhou Enlai, are located in the grounds of this erstwhile warlord’s manor, their humble furnishings still decorating their yaodong (“cave houses”), as earth-sheltered dwellings still common in Shaanxi province are called.

Concluding that to truly appreciate Yanan, I too should sleep in a cave, I consult a taxi driver parked outside the Date Garden, who takes me to Jingjiawan Village, where enterprising locals have converted family cave homes into guest houses.

A bright-eyed, middle-aged man, Xiao Zhang, greets me in the courtyard of the Old Yanan Cave Dwelling Hotel before showing me to my cavern for the night. Snow recounts sharing a kang, a traditional northern bed, with rats. But my kang has an electric blanket, while my cave is equipped with a widescreen television, an en suite bathroom and even voice-activated lights.

After a nap and a shower, I’m told to find an alfresco table on the edge of a cliff overlooking a large valley, a dramatic landscape of windblown yellow soil that has created steep ravines divided by deep gullies. The land here is rich in nutrients and has been cultivated for centuries, a history evident in crumbling terraces that were abandoned in the late 20th century as farmers emigrated to work in east-coast factories.

Lao Zhang, the family patriarch-turned-chef, takes my order and brings me a Wusu beer, with which I toast my journey back in time, from capital to cave.
