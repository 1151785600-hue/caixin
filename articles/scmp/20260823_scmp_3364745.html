The Mistral paradox: Europe’s push for tech sovereignty relies on China’s Z.ai

SCMP | FREE | 1106 words | 2026-08-23

On August 11, a tiny line buried in a lengthy press release from French company Mistral AI spoke volumes about the strategic and technological dilemmas facing the European Union, as the bloc prepares to navigate an autumn period full of geopolitical and trade upheaval.

In a bid to roll out “European infrastructure for sovereign AI”, Mistral – Europe’s great hope in the global artificial intelligence race – said it would begin offering other companies’ models, while keeping the service itself under European control.

Mistral’s model of choice is GLM-5.2, an open-weight large language model developed by Zhipu, also known as Z.ai, one of China’s six “AI tiger” companies, included since 2025 on the US’ entity list because of national security concerns regarding military modernisation.

The decision to plug a Chinese AI brain into what would effectively be a European shell has raised eyebrows given the shoddy state of EU-China relations, but on practical terms it makes some sense.

Despite a deluge of political chatter about creating a sovereign European AI offering, the bloc has lagged both the US and China in investment and innovation.

Geoeconomics expert Shahin Vallee from the German Council on Foreign Relations said that a pivot to “AI adoption rather than innovation” therefore represented “the right strategy for Mistral and for Europe”.

Europe “cannot compete or win on frontier models and raw computing power” and with US companies generally favouring closed models, “GLM-5.2 was simply the strongest option on the table”, he wrote in a note.

However, Mistral’s choice was also a demonstration of how Europe’s political and commercial incentives frequently pull the company in different directions.

While the EU is engaged in a bitter trade dispute with China and desperately trying to wind down its reliance on Beijing, European companies are finding themselves enticed by cheap and advanced Chinese technological offerings.

As well as Mistral, a series of European carmakers including Volkswagen, Mercedes-Benz, BMW and Stellantis, have partnered with Chinese outfits to tap what is seen as their superior electric vehicle technology.

Mistral’s was “a geopolitical move with profound ramifications that the US demanding more ideological alignment from its allies is unlikely to reverse and might in fact accelerate”, Vallee wrote.

It is a dynamic that places Brussels more firmly in the crossfire of a US-China tech rivalry that has already taken some unexpected twists.

While Europe’s governments have shown willingness to follow American tech initiatives – the European Commission and several member states signed up for the US-led Pax Silica initiative in June – there is some suggestion that US policies are pushing its companies into Beijing’s arms.

“When I was in Brussels in June, I was surprised that the mood towards America was more negative than towards China,” said Joerg Wuttke, a Washington-based partner at consultancy Albright Stone Group.

“Because the Chinese are predictable, practical, and you can do stuff with them, and they stick to it normally,” said Wuttke, who spent decades as the top European business lobbyist in China.

“Turnberry and all that happened afterwards has basically eroded any trust in the US administration, it’s just never ending.”

On August 14, three days after Mistral’s announcement, Reuters reported that the US planned to force Pax Silica signatories towards exclusive technological alignment – a move that could eventually make European use of Chinese models politically difficult or incompatible with the initiative’s direction.

On the same day, Bloomberg reported that Washington had sent letters to Nato allies suggesting the deployment of US military assets would be predicated on how supportive they were of Trump’s foreign policies, including his war on Iran.

A day earlier, a White House report named the EU among a “tier 1” group of trading partners facilitating the “illegal transshipment” of Chinese goods to dodge US tariffs.

The week before, the European Central Bank was said to have been “blindsided” by Washington’s dramatic sale of euros to prop up the ailing Japanese yen.

“There’s a squeeze on Europe from various fronts to extract more wealth out of it,” said Davide Oneglia, a London-based macroeconomist at research firm TS Lombard. “Europe is seen as still rich enough to pay for stuff, while not strong enough to defend itself industrially, technologically or militarily.”

At the same time, Beijing has been aggressively pushing an alternative to the US model of closed, proprietary AI platforms, presenting its own models as open, global goods.

Kyle Chan, an expert in China’s industrial policy, wrote in his High Capacity newsletter that “China’s main message to the world is: ‘We’re Android, and the US is Apple’”.

“The US offers a closed system where power and profits flow back to Washington and American corporations. China offers an open system where everyone, including the Global South, can participate and benefit.”

The ultimate goal of China’s policies is to dilute American influence in frontier technologies globally, according to Chan.

“Beijing casts China as the open partner and the US as the hegemonic gatekeeper willing to restrict access to technology for its own national interest,” he said.

“That argument likely resonates because many countries already worry about over-dependence on American technology platforms, a concern sharpened by recent US restrictions.”

According to Vallee, the extra wrinkle of AI could have “profound ramifications” ahead of an autumn in which the EU-China trade dispute is expected to come to a head.

“To me the combination of US aggression and China’s openness is pushing Europe into tighter cooperation with China,” he said in an interview.

“My sense is that the opinion in Europe, rightly or wrongly, over the last few years has evolved towards considering that the risk with the US is a similar, if not greater to the risk from China.”

However, deepened corporate exposure on Chinese technology could undermine Brussels’ efforts to take Beijing to task on trade, Vallee said. “I find it hard to imagine that the EU actively engaged in the highest level of technology integration with China through AI will attempt a very hardball strategy on trade.”

Alicia Garcia-Herrero, an Asia-focused economist focused at the Brussels-based Bruegel think tank, warned that using Chinese models just because of US export controls could be a dangerous strategy for European firms. “The idea that they’re open weight doesn’t mean that they’re free.”

Eventually, companies will be commercially required to charge for these models if they are to survive, according to Garcia-Herrero. “This is an additional dependence,” she said. “We also shouldn’t go there because that would give China an additional leeway on us.”
