China’s universities are approaching a significant wave of faculty retirements, which could strain the national pension system as retired staff numbers at some campuses rival active employees. An online estimate suggested Wuhan University’s over 7,000 retirees receive a combined 900 million yuan ($134.48 million) annually in pensions, assuming 10,000 yuan per month per person—though the university did not confirm this figure. Official data shows Wuhan University had 7,424 retired faculty and staff in January 2025, compared to 4,213 full-time teachers (excluding some staff) as of December 2024, underscoring the scale of the issue.

The debate highlights growing retiree numbers and persistent pension disparities. While China integrated pension systems for government-affiliated institutions and businesses in 2014, with a 10-year transition ending in 2024, gaps remain due to contribution compliance, supplementary pensions, and transition payments. In 2012, retirees from government agencies and public institutions received an average monthly basic pension of 2,904.7 yuan versus 2,027 yuan for enterprise retirees; by 2024, these rose to 7,735.5 yuan and 4,007.7 yuan, respectively. Rural and urban residents in the separate resident pension program received about 250 yuan monthly in 2024—roughly one-fifteenth of enterprise-worker pensions and one-thirtieth of public-institution retirees’ pensions, below the rural minimum-living allowance of 594 yuan.

Some universities already see retired populations comparable to active workers. Sichuan University reported 9,110 retired staff (including hospital workers) against over 9,300 active employees in 2025. China’s higher-education expansion since 1999 has enlarged payrolls, setting the stage for a larger retirement cohort. The number of full-time college teachers rose from 462,800 in 2000 to 2.22 million in 2025, with higher-education institutions employing 3.03 million people in 2024, including 1.95 million on official staffing rolls.

Institutions are experiencing hundreds of retirements annually. Zhejiang University honored 273 retiring employees in 2025, and Hengyang Normal University had 551 retired employees in 2026—nearly one-third of its workforce. Longer life expectancies are also expanding the retired population; Lanzhou University celebrated birthdays for 187 former employees aged 80 or 90 in 2024–2025.

The largest wave is still to come. About 150,000 full-time college teachers were under 30 in 2000, on track to retire around 2030, and 350,000 were under 30 by 2010, suggesting another wave around 2040. A 2026 study by Ren Zengyuan of Jilin University projected 1.53 million higher-education teachers will retire from 2026–2050, with a peak between 2036–2040 when 372,200 teachers are expected to leave. The study also forecast that shrinking enrollments combined with retirements could leave an excess of 357,300 teachers from 2036–2040 and 527,200 from 2041–2045.

The university retirement wave is part of a broader challenge for China’s public institutions. At the end of 2024, 61.95 million people were enrolled in the urban employee basic pension program for government agencies and public institutions, with higher-education employees comprising less than 5%. Since January 2015, public-sector employees share the same basic pension framework as enterprise workers, consisting of a foundational pension and an individual-account pension under broadly similar rules. However, government agencies and public institutions—including universities—also provide an occupational annuity, a supplementary retirement plan, making their retirement income consist of all three components.

Second-pillar coverage is far less common in the private sector. By the end of 2025, only 177,900 companies had established enterprise annuities covering 33.43 million workers, representing about 8.5% of the 395 million active contributors to the urban employee pension system, whereas occupational annuities cover all official staff in government and public institutions.

A simplified calculation illustrates the impact. Assuming an enterprise worker and a university employee each contribute for 35 years on a monthly wage base of 10,000 yuan (equal to the local average) and retire at 60, each would receive a foundational pension of 3,500 yuan monthly, plus an individual-account payment of 2,417 yuan (based on 336,000 yuan accumulated), totaling about 5,917 yuan—roughly 59% of pre-retirement income. The university employee’s occupational annuity, funded at 8% by the employer and 4% by the employee, would accumulate to about 504,000 yuan, providing another 3,626 yuan monthly, lifting total income to about 9,543 yuan, or roughly 95% of prior income.

If both retired at 63 under China’s delayed-retirement policy, the monthly gap would widen to about 4,300 yuan, as the annuity is paid over a shorter period, and including investment income would widen it further.
