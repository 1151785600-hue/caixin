China’s rocket boom has made Hainan a space hub. Can launches fuel wider growth?

SCMP | FREE | 1498 words | 2026-09-21

As technological developments augur an era of renewed space exploration, China is aiming to become a major player. In the first part of this two-part series, Wency Chen and Mia Nurmamat examine how the southern island province of Hainan is betting on a growing aerospace cluster to rewrite its economic playbook beyond tourism.

On Hainan’s northeastern coast, launch towers rise abruptly from a flat landscape of coconut palms and open sky. At Pad 1 of the commercial spaceport in Wenchang, an 83-metre (272 feet) service tower stands surrounded by four slender, steel lattice towers, designed to protect the launch site from lightning during the island’s frequent tropical storms.

No mission is scheduled, but an orange flame flickers from a gas vent at one facility.

Long known for its coconuts, its namesake chicken and generations of emigrants who left for Southeast Asia in search of a living, Wenchang has been thrust onto the frontier of national development. Beijing’s space ambitions have turned the city into a showcase for rocket launches – and given Hainan a catalyst to reshape an economy long reliant on tourism and consumer services.

Today, the island province is working to build a more diversified and sustainable growth model and, together with its ambition to host a premier free-trade port, is embracing a hi-tech future increasingly centred on the space economy.

From coconuts to rockets: Hainan’s economic reboot

“Commercial space flight could give Hainan a clearer manufacturing focus while complementing its traditional strengths in tourism and consumer services,” said Peng Peng, executive chairman of the Guangdong Society of Reform, a prominent think tank affiliated with the provincial government.

Home to more than 10 million people and comparable in size to Belgium, Hainan now boasts a growing industrial cluster known as Wenchang International Aerospace City. At its centre are two state-owned rocket launch facilities: one serving national programmes and another handling commercial missions.

The Wenchang Space Launch Site staged its first launch in 2016 and focuses mainly on lunar exploration and human space flight. Just a few miles away sits the Hainan Commercial Space Launch Site, China’s first dedicated commercial launch platform.

Since its inaugural mission in November 2024, the commercial site has completed 21 launches and has sent 221 satellites into orbit, according to Xu Xikai, deputy head of the development department at Hainan International Commercial Aerospace Launch, the site’s operator.

Two more general-purpose liquid-fuel launch pads were also under construction and could host their maiden missions by the end of this year, Xu noted. Longer-term plans envision as many as eight large coastal launch pads, together with tracking facilities and systems for recovering rockets at sea.

We want to attract more companies to Hainan, using the launch site to drive the wider industry and bringing in companies across the rocket, satellite and data chains

Xu Xikai, Hainan International Commercial Aerospace Launch

Wenchang’s coastal geography gives it a major advantage. Massive rockets can be delivered directly by sea, bypassing the road, tunnel and bridge constraints faced by inland facilities. And at about 19 degrees north latitude, rockets gain an extra momentum boost from the Earth’s rotation, reducing fuel requirements or allowing heavier payloads, Xu explained.

“For large-diameter, heavy-lift rockets in the future, coastal launch sites will increasingly have an advantage,” he said.

Geography, however, is also one of Hainan’s economic weaknesses. The island has historically lacked a heavy industrial base, meaning much of the specialised hardware and construction materials required for space manufacturing must be shipped in.

Inside Wenchang’s manufacturing ambitions

The challenge for Hainan is transforming launch sites into the nucleus of a wider industrial ecosystem – and early signs are encouraging.

Wenchang International Aerospace City recorded a surge in revenue amid an “unprecedented” pace of rocket launches, according to a June report by Nanhai Net, a state-run news portal operated by the Hainan Daily Press Group.

The aerospace park’s revenue jumped 85 per cent in 2025 to 23.53 billion yuan (US$3.5 billion), while manufacturing output from its rocket supply chain more than doubled to over 8 billion yuan, the report stated.

Under a provincial action plan from 2025 to 2027, Hainan also aims to raise annual operating revenue at Wenchang’s aerospace cluster to 10 billion yuan by 2027, while building an integrated supply chain spanning rockets, satellites and data services.

The expansion comes as Beijing presses ahead with reforms to open the island further and develop new sources of growth – though competition is intensifying. Two other commercial launch sites have already begun operating in Gansu and Shandong provinces, while at least four more are under development nationwide.

To stay ahead, Hainan’s strategy is to push further up the industrial chain. Its action plan prioritises a “super factory” next to the launch pads, designed to produce up to 1,000 satellites annually. The location would allow freshly assembled satellites to move straight from production line to launch pad with very little transit time, the Star Market Daily, a Shanghai-based financial news outlet focused on technology and capital markets, reported in December.

Described by local authorities as Asia’s largest satellite-manufacturing facility, the industrial complex is also expected to support China’s Qianfan low-Earth-orbit constellation. A planned special bonded zone for satellite data would further leverage Hainan’s free-trade policies to attract data from overseas, according to the report.

“We want to attract more companies to Hainan, using the launch site to drive the wider industry and bringing in companies across the rocket, satellite and data chains,” said Xu of Hainan International Commercial Aerospace Launch.

The infrastructure buildout is already reshaping launch activity. After more than two years of development, the Hainan Commercial Space Launch Site has moved from experimental testing to routine operations. It handled more than 30 per cent of China’s commercial satellite launches in 2025 and successfully carried out the country’s first offshore rocket booster recovery.

Business beyond the launch pad

Putting a rocket into orbit, however, is only one part of the emerging space economy. Ye Shiyang, a Wenchang native who chairs the company behind the Aerospace Supercomputing Centre in Wenchang International Aerospace City, argued Hainan must capture a larger share of the value generated after a rocket launches.

“Over the past few years, we have connected the entire chain – from collecting and receiving satellite data to storing, processing, applying and trading it,” Ye said.

Completed in 2023, the supercomputing centre occupies about 40,000 square metres (about 431,000 square feet), with about 22,400 square metres of floor space. A guide at the centre said it offered peak computing power of 100 petaflops – a measure of processing capacity – and was designed to accommodate about 2,000 server racks.

Meanwhile, three of the centre’s remote-sensing satellites operate as part of Jilin-1, which is described by its developer as the world’s largest commercial sub-metre Earth-observation constellation. The centre has also built 13 ground stations, according to a guide there.

The practical applications could be numerous. Satellite images processed in Wenchang can distinguish food crops from cash crops, identify fallow land and monitor crop growth, assess pest outbreaks and track the effects of typhoons. Such imaging tools can also help to detect water pollution, illegal logging and unauthorised land use.

These services, Ye argued, could generate steady demand from agricultural, environmental and land-management authorities, giving Hainan a revenue stream that is less dependent on the schedule of rocket launches.

The aerospace city is also developing a space tourism business. The Jixing Meteorite Museum, for instance, displays more than 400 specimens gathered from around the world, while exhibitions introduce visitors to satellite data and supercomputing.

“We are developing space tourism in response to market demand,” Ye said. “Visitors should not only watch a launch or see a rocket or satellite. They should also understand the industry and technology behind them.”

In 2025, tourism, modern services, hi-tech industries and tropical agriculture – Hainan’s four pillar sectors – accounted for 65.9 per cent of the province’s gross domestic product, according to the provincial government.

“The Wenchang launch site has already become a tourist attraction, and this could give Hainan an economic model built around tourism, consumer services and aerospace manufacturing – and give its manufacturing sector a much clearer direction,” said Peng of the Guangdong Society of Reform.

For a Wenchang native surnamed Xie, the transformation has been drastic. Growing up in a village in Longlou town, he once walked barefoot along unpaved red-earth tracks. Today, working as a taxi driver, he has watched the sprawling aerospace complex reshape the place he calls home.

As the launch site and surrounding projects expanded, local families were relocated and farmland acquired. While some households received hundreds of thousands of yuan in compensation, Xie noted how traditional livelihoods were quickly fading, leaving coconut processing as one of the few legacy industries remaining.

“Farmers around here barely farm any more,” he said.

In Longlou, the coconut palms still remain – quiet reminders of a past that existed before the rockets arrived, even as a new economy takes shape around them.
