How Asia’s 50 Best Bars expanded to 25 cities, from Kathmandu to Hangzhou

SCMP | FREE | 1047 words | 2026-08-15

Since Asia’s 50 Best Bars extended its rankings up to 100 in 2021, the number of countries and cities represented has expanded from an inaugural nine cities across eight countries in 2016 to 25 cities in 11 countries this year.

The effects have been largely twofold. First, no single city dominates as Singapore did in 2017, with its record-high 13 entries. Recognition is much more diffused now. Bangkok, the most represented location this year, fields a dominant but comparatively modest eight entries in the main list, including the year’s highest new entry – Lennon’s from Rosewood Bangkok, which landed at seventh place. A secondary effect has been that the 51-100 list serves as a hotbed for younger cocktail cities. Barc entered the list in 2023, putting Kathmandu on the map, while cities like Hangzhou and Galle made the rankings for the first time this year.

More cities on the list means more competition for stalwart venues like Singapore’s Nutmeg & Clove, debuting at 30th place in 2017, but it also means that cocktail lovers are expanding their palates rapidly. “Our guests are more well-travelled now,” says Nutmeg & Clove founder Colin Chia, “Cocktail tourism is a real thing. Consumers these days, they will travel to Sri Lanka, or go to Semarang in Indonesia for cocktails.”

Chia, who also started the popular bars #FindThePhotobooth and #FindTheLockerRoom in Bangkok, notes that increased recognition of bars from further afield has led to a cross-pollination of sorts, even in an established cocktail scene like Singapore’s. “When connecting with these overseas guest shifts, we learn the culture and the taste profile,” he says. “We’re not just making drinks for Singaporeans; we try to be as inclusive as possible.”

Even if countries with less of a cocktail tradition are starting to make their mark, that doesn’t mean the news is resonating everywhere. In Kuala Lumpur, represented by four bars across both the main and extended lists, Angeline Tan of Three X Co claims, “A lot of Malaysians don’t even know about the 50 Best [Bars] list,” and says the KL scene is still young and dominated by beer drinkers.

Tan has not been deterred, though, in her efforts to build Malaysia’s cocktail scene, and is leading the charge. She is affectionately known as the “mama” of Kuala Lumpur’s bar scene, which includes Bar Trigona at the Four Seasons Kuala Lumpur, the fermentation-focused Reka:bar, and the more classically-oriented Penrose at 64th on this year’s extended list. Together, the bars annually host KL Cocktail Week, while Three X Co focuses on cocktail-pairing menus and approaches Gen Z in addition to their regular service. Tan finds it crucial to bring in regional influence. “Every month we bring in a guest bartender; that is our education.”

It’s not just Tan who is pushing for more cross-border collaboration. Similar attitudes exist in Nepal, home to another developing cocktail scene. “The cocktail industry in Nepal is still relatively small … and remains largely concentrated in Kathmandu,” explains Barc co-founder Adarsha Tamrakar. “Increasing engagement with bars across Asia and the rest of the world through guest shifts, masterclasses, competitions and exchange programmes will help expand our community beyond borders while exposing Nepalese bartenders to new ideas and perspectives.”

Barc is Kathmandu’s most outstanding bar, and Tamrakar observes that the city lacks diversity of concepts more than it does quality – but says new concepts are on their way. “Before a city gets to multiple internationally recognised bars, you typically see a healthy ecosystem emerge: classic bars, neighbourhood bars, hotel bars, industry bars and experimental bars,” Tamrakar says. “Kathmandu is developing several of these simultaneously, which is unusual for a young scene … driven by bartenders adapting international techniques to local ingredients and traditions.”

This is a progression that all mature bar cities go through, Tamrakar says, adding that the future looks extremely promising. “We are not yet at the level of more mature cocktail destinations like Singapore, Hong Kong or Bangkok in terms of scale and volume. But the opening of new bars, the return of Nepalese bartenders who have gained experience in world-class bars abroad, and the rise of bartending schools in the city are all contributing to a more skilled and knowledgeable workforce,” he says.

A common problem cocktail bars in Asia face is reconciling a baseline of quality with a community capable of properly expressing local cocktail culture. Even bars in Bangkok find it a challenge to thread the needle between supporting local culture and ensuring their drinks appeal to those indulging in “cocktail tourism”. Lennon’s, this year’s highest new entry on the 50 Best list, focuses on signatures with regional ingredients like pandan or calamansi, but their advocacy for Thai spirits is enthusiastic but cautious.

In January, Lennon’s Spirits of Thailand guest shift featured 12 bars and nine spirit makers in the same night. “It’s a very young industry, [starting] three to four years after Covid-19,” says KT Lam, director of bar at Lennon’s. “They’re still finding ways to make their products better, so most of them are still very raw – it’s not stable yet.”

However, bars in Singapore are an example that struggling to overcome these difficulties is a worthwhile endeavour. According to Chia, Nutmeg & Clove opened as a “very interesting, casual neighbourhood street bar” at a time when the local scene was more about speakeasies and serious cocktail bars like Jigger & Pony. Though Chia lost a small fortune in the first 18 months, as Singapore “was not ready for an Asian, Singaporean-inspired cocktail bar yet”, Nutmeg & Clove survived and is now the flagship of a bar group that includes the Japanese-inspired Last Word and Draftland Singapore.

As cities follow these road maps and build their own robust cocktail culture, seeing more locales join Asia’s 50 Best Bars seems almost an inevitability, which means visitors get more destinations with quality bars they can depend on. “[The list is] inclusive, but we can do better,” Chia remarks. “We need to travel to places and search for the next best thing. When you travel, you need to eat, you need to drink. It’s really good that bars from different cities all over Asia highlight that it’s no longer just Singapore, Japan or Hong Kong.”
