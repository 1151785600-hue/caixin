US space weapons move has ‘broken taboo’, could spark new arms race, Beijing forum told

SCMP | FREE | 1194 words | 2026-09-19

The US deployment of space weapons has shattered a long-held taboo and risks igniting a fresh arms race, with the world at a “breaking point in history”, speakers at a major security conference in Beijing have warned.

Experts at the Xiangshan Forum this week also called for US-China dialogue on space security, just days before Chinese President Xi Jinping makes his first state visit to the US in more than a decade.

US Air Force Secretary Troy Meink – the civilian head of the department that oversees both the US Air Force and Space Force – acknowledged for the first time on Tuesday that the Pentagon had deployed weapons in space.

“We are increasing readiness against existing threats, and the United States has on-orbit space control weapons capable of defending the Joint Force against hostile adversary action,” Meink said, according to a US Air Force statement. He did not give details on the systems involved.

Almudena Azcarate Ortega, senior programme analyst on space law and regulation with the Washington-based think tank Secure World Foundation, said the weapons were likely to be jammers or spoofers, which are essentially non-kinetic technologies.

A kinetic weapon would conflict with US policy against debris-creating activities, Ortega told the Xiangshan Forum on Wednesday.

The Outer Space Treaty – the cornerstone of international space law adopted in 1967 – bars its more than 110 signatory nations from placing nuclear weapons or other weapons of mass destruction in orbit. However, it does not impose limits on the deployment of conventional weaponry.

“The Outer Space Treaty does not prohibit the deployment of conventional weapons in outer space, but … it has become a taboo, just like no first use of nuclear weapons in the eight decades since 1945,” noted Guo Xiaobing, director of the Centre for Arms Control Studies with the China Institutes of Contemporary International Relations.

Hosting a panel discussion on outer space security governance on Wednesday, Guo said: “We also have a space taboo. That’s the taboo about the deployment of conventional weapons in space.

“That taboo was broken yesterday. We are at a breaking point in history.”

Ortega said that while the US deployment did not violate the Outer Space Treaty, “if the threshold rose to the level of a use of force, that would be a violation of Article 2.4 of the UN Charter”.

“So the reason why this is sort of a big deal is that no US official has ever acknowledged an actual weapons system in space before.

“It is a shift in terms of traditional US policy around counterspace capabilities. And it is also dangerous from a linguistic perspective,” she cautioned.

She said the comment could potentially be escalatory, causing others to deploy similar technologies, making space more contested and competitive.

“I do think that this is a bit of a step back in terms of everything that we’re trying to achieve at the multilateral level on space security [and] diplomacy.”

The 13th edition of the Beijing Xiangshan Forum – China’s most important annual defence gathering – was held from Tuesday to Thursday. It brought together defence officials, military leaders and global experts from over 100 countries.

Experts at the forum also argued that ambiguous space terminology had fuelled rising space competition between countries.

Gao Guozhu, director of Beihang University’s Institute of Outer Space Law, said opinions varied on what constituted a space threat.

“For example, the United States considers the use of anti-satellite weapons or technologies to pose a serious threat to space systems – or, more precisely, a serious threat to the countries that own those systems,” Gao said.

“China, by contrast, views the US policy of seeking dominance in outer space as a threat. In other words, China sees this as a strategic or policy-level threat. The two sides therefore differ in what they identify as a threat.”

Gao agreed that the US deployment of space weapons was “bound to trigger a new round of the arms race in space”.

“If a war in space breaks out in the future, the era of spacefaring civilisation that humanity has pursued for more than half a century could well become a thing of the past,” he warned.

US defence and intelligence assessments have long highlighted China’s pursuit of wide-ranging counterspace capabilities, including direct-ascent anti-satellite missiles, co-orbital systems, electronic warfare and directed-energy weapons, with some capabilities already considered operational.

Since 2024, the US has also claimed that Russia is developing space-based anti-satellite capabilities – which Moscow denies. Additionally, the US alleged in both its 2025 and 2026 annual threat assessment reports that Russia was developing a new satellite designed to carry a nuclear weapon into orbit as an anti-satellite payload.

Senior Colonel Zhao Weibin, a prominent Chinese military scholar and member of the Beijing Xiangshan Forum Secretariat, said the risks of a space arms race had probably just become a reality.

“Once [the arms race] logic is activated, it’s hard to reverse. Because an on-orbit weapon will severely erode strategic mutual trust,” he warned.

“Planning countermeasures on the assumption of a worst-case scenario – may trigger an escalating spiral of action and reaction.”

In 2025, President Donald Trump unveiled the “Golden Dome for America”, a multilayered missile-defence architecture incorporating space-based interceptors (SBI) designed to intercept threats shortly after launch.

The SBI programme aims to place missile interceptors in low-Earth orbit, rather than solely using satellites to detect and track threats.

Gao said both the SBI programme and the deployment of space-based weapons had shattered the quiet global understanding on “no weapons in space”.

“What did we mean by strategic stability in the past? It meant that each side had the ability to destroy the other – a theory based on mutually assured vulnerability,” Gao said, warning that the present shift left adversaries with no diplomatic recourse.

“The problem now is that you are becoming increasingly powerful, while using that power to make me vulnerable and grind me into the ground. What am I supposed to do under those circumstances? I have no options left, and there is no longer any basis for dialogue.”

Experts are also calling for space cooperation to become part of the China-US constructive strategic stability dialogue initiated during the May Xi-Trump summit in Beijing.

Gao also highlighted the dangers posed by space weapons in case of a Taiwan conflict, pointing to commercial and military infrastructure in orbit.

“The US might use Starlink to provide services for Taiwan and we might disrupt it because we don’t think that’s OK. And the US, in order to stop China, could disrupt China’s BeiDou [satellite-based navigation] system,” he said.

Such an escalation would be very dangerous and could potentially lead to both sides attacking each other’s satellites, Gao warned.

Beijing sees Taiwan as part of China and has never renounced the use of force to achieve reunification. Most countries, including the US – Taiwan’s main international backer – do not recognise the self-ruled island as an independent state. However, Washington is opposed to any attempt to take Taiwan by force and is committed to supplying it with weapons for its defence.
