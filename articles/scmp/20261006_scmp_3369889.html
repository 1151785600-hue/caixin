How Singapore’s local cafes compete with Chinese coffee giant Luckin

SCMP | FREE | 1098 words | 2026-10-06

When Chinese coffee giant Luckin Coffee moved into the unit next to his neighbourhood cafe in Singapore, Folk Yard owner Chew Yit Shan grew nervous.

Luckin launched with an aggressive promotional deal of S$1 (78 US cents) per cup, served in just two minutes. In the first few months following its arrival, Chew saw sales at his independent cafe drop by 15 per cent.

But once the initial hype had died down and the promotions ended, Chew said regular patrons returned and coffee sales bounced back.

“We had to explain to customers that we offer something different. It’s the simple things, like knowing the baristas and coming back to the same places in the neighbourhood,” Chew said. “There’s a level of interaction and familiarity that becomes part of their routine, and that goes beyond the product itself.”

Chew, who opened Folk Yard in 2022, said that Chinese brands like Luckin worked because of their “incredible purchasing power and economies of scale”.

“I don’t think there’s a point where we can compete with them purely on price and convenience. What we can offer is individuality – something that is unique to us and can’t be replicated at the same scale,” Chew said.

Last month, Luckin announced it had hit its 100th-store milestone, just three years after making its overseas debut in the city state.

The chain now operates 103 outlets in Singapore, with up to six more reportedly planned by the end of the year. Its mobile app, which allows customers to order and pay in advance before picking up in-store, now has some 1.9 million users in the city state. Globally, the brand operates more than 36,000 stores across China, over 150 in Malaysia and more than 20 in New York.

Satish Kumar, a 40-year-old civil servant, said he became hooked after his first sip of Luckin’s coconut velvet latte two years ago.

While local coffee – affectionately known as kopi – is a cheaper alternative at S$1 to S$2 a cup, Kumar said Luckin had a different appeal.

“I got addicted after trying [it] because it tastes good to me, and I prefer lattes to kopi,” Kumar said. “They also now have a lot of different flavours and it’s so easy to order on the app.”

‘Like tech stores’

Singapore’s specialist coffee market was estimated to be worth about US$327 million last year, with Luckin capturing roughly 24 per cent of the market share. Starbucks, which entered Singapore in 1996, held about 34 per cent, according to Weihan Chen, insights lead at market intelligence consultancy Momentum Works.

Chen said the arrival of Chinese drink chains had raised the level of competition, particularly around convenience, product variety, pricing and the speed of new product roll-outs.

Yet Chen cautioned against framing Chinese chains as simply replacing local cafes, noting that consumers support very different drink formats, and these brands often compete for different occasions.

Nathanael Lim, Asia-Pacific insight manager for beverages at Euromonitor International, said Luckin’s rapid rise in Singapore reflected its ambitions beyond China.

“Unlike local brands, Luckin combines substantial financial backing with its playbook centred on digital ordering, frequent limited-edition product launches and strong app-based loyalty programmes,” Lim said, adding that Luckin’s scale gave it advantages in supply chain sourcing and operational efficiency.

Seshan Ramaswami, an associate professor of marketing education at Singapore Management University, pointed out that Luckin stores typically had limited seating, keeping overheads low while minimising staffing requirements.

App-based ordering also gives the brand a huge database to use for real-time customer analysis. Drawing from its experience in China, Luckin fine-tunes its customer relationship management to offer targeted discounts that drive repeat purchases.

“This sort of rapid expansion is not always successful, of course – so much depends on consumer loyalty as well as competitive reaction,” Ramaswami said. “But if the taste and the value perception at Luckin’s prices catch on, it could force other chains to change their product mix or even close some stores in the proximity of its outlet.”

Lawrence Loh, a professor of strategy and policy at the National University of Singapore Business School, noted that beyond menu options or marketing strategies, brands like Luckin used novel scaling models that rapidly expanded the number of physical stores to secure long-term market share, even at the expense of short-term profitability.

“Brands are often like tech stores at the shopfront, which not only reduces labour costs but adds a modern feel that appeals to customers, particularly the younger generations who are always on the move,” Loh said.

Deep-pocketed foreign players would often outbid competitors for retail space while constantly reinventing their products, he said, adding that the coffee experience they offered went beyond the menu to embrace culture, community, corporate social responsibility and the “cool” factor attached to being a patron.

“Of late, Chinese brands seem to be able to offer this totality in overseas markets even better than the incumbent brands,” Loh said.

But Denise Lum, co-owner of independent local cafe Maxi Coffee Bar, said she did not see Luckin as a competitor because it catered to a completely different consumer.

“Luckin probably attracts a distinct customer base – people who really care about convenience, price, efficiency, technology and all of that. We cannot compete in that space … All we can do is build on what we feel makes our shop special, which is the customer experience,” she said.

“For them, it’s a scale game. For us, it’s not a breadth but a depth game.”

Opening a new outlet for Maxi Coffee required careful consideration, Lum said, regardless of whether capital was readily available.

“Growth has to be intentional. It’s very much about the neighbourhood and less about just wanting to grow. It’s about identity and community building,” she said.

Folk Yard’s Chew agreed that Luckin customers appeared to want convenience and low prices, whereas his clientele were looking for something else entirely.

“Our customers are people who are looking to slow down,” he said. “They’re not just coming in for a coffee; they’re coming for the experience, the interaction and the sense of community.”

Looking ahead, Momentum Works’ Chen said Luckin would face challenges in maintaining its large network of stores.

“Opening stores quickly demonstrates that there is consumer demand, but sustaining a large network requires strong store-level economics, local management and supply chains,” Chen said.

“As they expand into more countries, the challenge becomes how much of the system developed in China can be replicated and how much needs to be localised.”
