Heading to America to bridge the gap in US-China relations

SCMP | FREE | 1017 words | 2026-09-07

Before boarding my flight to Boston late last month, I spent the better part of three days doing something I never imagined I would do in more than 30 years of covering China: scrubbing my phone. Old emails, screenshots, WeChat threads that might be read the wrong way, anything that could be misconstrued in an airport interview room – all deleted, just in case.

I was not being paranoid. I was following advice now routinely passed among Chinese travellers to the United States: carry a clean phone, or better yet, a burner. American officials, academics and business travellers headed into China have long been told the same thing. Two sets of citizens, one shared instinct – that what is on your phone could get you into trouble on the other side.

As it turned out, my entry into the US was swift and unremarkable. No locked room, no confiscated devices, none of the horror stories that have circulated among Chinese academics in recent years. The gap between what I feared and what happened was, in miniature, what I have spent 35 years writing about: the gap between perception and reality in US-China relations. It is also the reason I am here.

Beginning this month, I take up a one-year appointment as a senior visiting scholar at the Rajawali Foundation Institute for Asia at the Harvard Kennedy School. My purpose is straightforward to state and harder to fulfil: to sit down with American thought leaders across the political spectrum and ask them where they think this relationship is headed, and whether conflict is as inevitable as it now appears.

To understand why this question matters so much, a brief look back is useful. The relationship began its current downward spiral in 2018, when the first Trump administration launched its opening salvo in the trade war. At the time, the confrontation felt sudden and jarring. It was not, in fact, sudden at all. Grievances had been quietly accumulating for decades across government, industry, trade, academia, science and technology. What changed in 2018 was that those grievances finally found their outlet.

Layered on top were structural anxieties. In Washington, there is the perceived erosion of American primacy in manufacturing and superpower status. In Beijing, there is a mirror-image worry: that a declining but still-dominant America, unwilling to accept the reality of a rising China, is bent on containing its development, through tariffs, technology export controls and alliances built for that purpose.

That mutual anxiety, in turn, fed less rational forces on both sides of the Pacific: incomplete narratives, hostile rhetoric, rumour dressed as fact, all doing damage to how each public sees the other. In Washington, there is a wry observation that American politicians agree on almost nothing except the need to get tough on China. In China, public sentiment towards the US has turned sour.

The goodwill accumulated over decades of engagement has largely evaporated, replaced by mutual suspicion and precaution – of which my scrubbed phone is a small but telling example.

None of this is to dismiss the seriousness of the moment. In the past fortnight alone, US Treasury officials have floated the possibility of sanctioning Chinese banks over their dealings with Iran, a suggestion that drew a sharp pushback from Beijing.

Long Guoqiang, deputy director general of the Development Research Centre of the State Council, a leading government think tank, captured the prevailing mood in Beijing when he warned recently that the US remains “the biggest external source of uncertainty affecting China’s path to modernisation”. He argues that American containment of China will persist and intensify over the long run, notwithstanding the trade war truce.

Long’s warning is not wrong. But a truce, however temporary, is also an opening. After their Beijing summit in May, President Xi Jinping and President Donald Trump are set to meet three more times this year: Xi is expected in Washington later this month, with further encounters likely on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation and G20 leaders’ meetings in November and December.

Much of the coverage of these meetings will, understandably, focus on tangible deliverables – deals on trade, investment, technology. But Beijing and Washington might also use the moment to look at the bigger picture, along three tracks that receive far less airtime than they should.

On global challenges, can both powers still cooperate on climate change and future pandemics, even if the current US administration shows little enthusiasm for the former?

On engagement, law enforcement cooperation – against transnational crime, the booming scam-centre operations along China’s periphery, and the flow of fentanyl precursor chemicals into North America – is a shining example. Quiet cooperation on precursor chemicals continued even through the worst of the trade war, proof that narrow, mutually beneficial arrangements can survive broader hostility when both sides see a direct interest in results.

And on global regulation, can the two governments find common ground in governing artificial intelligence, space and other emerging technologies?

Right now, discourse in both capitals dwells overwhelmingly on the negative. Far less is said about where cooperation remains not just possible but necessary.

That is what I intend to spend the next year finding out. I am, by long habit of observation, a clear-eyed optimist – clear-eyed about the depth of the pessimism baked into this relationship, but unwilling to accept that Washington and Beijing are destined for conflict. From Cambridge, I plan to reach beyond the think tanks most familiar to a China watcher, including into quarters not typically friendly to Beijing’s view of the world, setting aside my instincts and assumptions as best I can.

What will I find by the time this fellowship ends? I do not know. But having spent three decades chronicling how perception so often diverges from reality in this relationship, I remain hopeful that reality, properly examined, still leaves room for two great powers to coexist – durably, and peacefully – for everyone’s sake.
