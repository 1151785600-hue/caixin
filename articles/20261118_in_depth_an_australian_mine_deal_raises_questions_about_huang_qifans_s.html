In Depth: An Australian Mine Deal Raises Questions About Huang Qifan’s Son

Caixin Global | FULLTEXT | 639 words | 2026-11-18 05:44

1. Authorities are investigating Huang Qifan, the former mayor of Chongqing, who was taken from his home on Oct. 10 and had his investigation announced on Oct. 13, a decade after retirement. His son, Huang Ranfei, was also placed under authorities' control at around the same time. The scrutiny is partly tied to a 2009 Australian mining investment by state-owned Chongqing Iron & Steel Group, intended to secure iron ore but which committed hundreds of millions of Australian dollars to a project that required far more capital and never entered production. Sources have questioned the price paid and whether improper financial benefits were involved, though it remains unclear if the mining deal is part of the formal inquiry.

2. Huang Ranfei, about 45, is Huang Qifan's son from his first marriage. He earned a finance degree from Fudan University in 2003 and a master's in computational finance from Carnegie Mellon University in 2004. He worked at Lehman Brothers' investment-banking division from 2005 in New York and Hong Kong, then at Nomura International in Hong Kong from 2008. Around 2010, he joined Morgan Stanley in Hong Kong as a vice president, later serving as executive director at Morgan Stanley Huaxin Securities from 2012 to 2014. He left foreign investment banking in 2014 for Guangdong Microholdings, then remained largely out of public view until 2021, when he became special assistant to the chairman of Shandong Weiqiao Pioneering Group.

4. The mining deal originated as Chongqing officials encouraged local companies to acquire overseas resources while iron-ore prices climbed; Huang Qifan, then executive vice mayor, led the effort. In June 2009, Chongqing Iron & Steel and two partners began talks with Chenglong Investment over Australian assets held by its Hong Kong-registered subsidiary, Asia Iron Holdings. Morgan Stanley advised Chongqing on the acquisition, and sources say Huang Ranfei, then preparing to move from Nomura to Morgan Stanley, played a key role. The investment vehicle paid A$273 million ($190 million) for a 60% stake in Asia Iron, with Chenglong retaining 40%. The initial plan called for about $2 billion in first-phase development, with operations starting by end-2013 and annual output of 10 million metric tons of magnetite concentrate.

5. Industry sources questioned the price from the outset, noting Chenglong had acquired the project in 2006 for only a few million Australian dollars. Founder Zhang Chi was under financial pressure after the 2008 crisis and eager to sell. The main asset was the Extension Hill magnetite project in Western Australia, with inferred resources of 1.8 billion tons and potential resources exceeding 5 billion tons; iron grade was 39.4%, while processed concentrate could reach 68.5%. The portfolio also included additional iron-ore rights, coal resources, and three farms. Chenglong later pledged its remaining stake to Chongqing Iron & Steel for an A$170 million loan. People familiar with Zhang described him as skilled at cultivating ties with state-owned companies, and accounts of a close relationship between him and Huang Ranfei circulated.

Developing the mine required roughly A$3 billion more for processing, rail, and port facilities; Chongqing Iron & Steel lacked the personnel and expertise to manage the project, and large loans from China Development Bank and others failed to materialize. Meanwhile, the steelmaker's relocation caused severe cost overruns and mounting debt; its listed subsidiary accumulated about 23.8 billion yuan in losses from 2011 to 2016 and entered court-supervised restructuring in 2017. China Baowu formally took control in 2021. The Australian mineral rights remained in Chongqing's state-owned asset system; three associated farms were sold for a combined A$16.35 million in 2023 and 2024, but the mine remained undeveloped.
