How Japan aims to convert ageing warships into leverage to counter China

SCMP | FREE | 1018 words | 2026-09-02

Japan’s plans to transfer ageing warships to the Philippines and Indonesia could turn older military hardware into geopolitical leverage, deepening regional security ties while helping Tokyo counterbalance Beijing’s growing influence, observers say.

Such transfers, they said, fit into Tokyo’s broader security assistance toolkit to strengthen partners’ capabilities and forge longer-term defence relationships through equipment, training, maintenance and interoperability – expanding Japan’s regional influence without demanding alignment against Beijing.

The broader effort is aimed at cultivating what Tokyo calls a “desirable security environment” by taking a more active role in shaping the region’s security architecture.

Providing retiring warships is an important element of that push. The Philippines expects to receive five Abukuma-class destroyer escorts within two to three years, while Tokyo and Jakarta are discussing the transfer of Asagiri-class destroyers, the first of which was retired in March after 38 years of service.

An analysis published on August 25 by Japan’s National Institute for Defence Studies (NIDS), the defence ministry’s main research institute, suggests Tokyo could eventually broaden the pool of equipment for such transfers.

NIDS researcher Akinori Ishihara pointed to Japan’s transfer of ageing but still operational warships to Russia during World War I after newer vessels reduced their relative importance in Japan’s future fleet.

Ishihara argued that the key consideration was not whether equipment was new or old, or active or retired, but whether Japan could transfer it and still maintain the defence capabilities it needed.

That could open the door to retiring equipment early to make it available for transfer, the NIDS analysis suggests. As Japan introduces more advanced, automated and uncrewed systems, some older platforms could become less critical to the Japan Self-Defence Forces structure before reaching the end of their planned service lives, expanding the pool of equipment available for transfer to regional partners.

Hirohito Ogi, a senior research fellow at the Institute of Geoeconomics in Tokyo, said such an approach made sense for vessels with relatively limited air-defence capabilities designed mainly for peacetime and grey-zone operations. As China’s precision-strike capabilities became increasingly sophisticated, such ships were unlikely to survive high-intensity combat, he said, arguing that Japan should retire them as early as possible and renew its capabilities.

Retiring equipment, however, is only one part of Tokyo’s expanding security aid toolkit.

A key mechanism is the Official Security Assistance framework, a grant-based military aid programme Japan launched in 2023 to provide equipment and infrastructure to partner countries. Tokyo has increased its budget from 2 billion yen (US$12.5 million) in the 2023 financial year to a record 18.1 billion yen this financial year and plans to continue expanding the number of countries covered by the programme.

Tokyo has used the programme to supply equipment ranging from patrol boats and coastal radars, to surveillance systems and drones, with maritime security being a major focus. Both the Philippines and Indonesia are recipients, reflecting Japan’s efforts to strengthen partners along strategically important waterways.

Sebastian Maslow, an associate professor at the University of Tokyo’s Institute of Social Science, said Japan had increasingly embraced arms transfers as a foreign policy tool since overhauling its export restrictions in 2014, a process that culminated in a further major easing of the rules earlier this year.

He said Japan’s main strategic objective was to strengthen defence cooperation with Indo-Pacific partners to deter China’s growing influence.

Japan’s evolving strategy was “designed to counterbalance China’s rise by preserving the status quo in the region” with arms transfers, helping Tokyo arrive at the centre of emerging bilateral and minilateral defence networks, according to Maslow.

The Philippines provides a concrete example of that strategic logic. Maslow pointed to increasingly active mainland Chinese naval operations southeast of Taiwan, particularly around the Bashi Channel and Philippine Sea, with vessels subsequently deploying deeper into the Pacific and around Japan’s islands.

Ogi said that strengthening the Philippine Navy’s ability to monitor and track those movements would “directly contribute to Japan’s security” and could also help deter a Taiwan contingency.

The warships would add to a growing range of Japanese maritime security equipment provided or offered to Manila, including coastguard vessels and TC-90 aircraft, while the two sides have also explored the potential transfer of Japan’s ageing Type 88 surface-to-ship missiles.

The value of second-hand platforms could also extend beyond the hardware itself. Tokyo is discussing training, maintenance and sustainment with Manila, potentially keeping Japan involved throughout much of the equipment’s remaining service life.

Indonesia presents a different case as Jakarta seeks to preserve its strategic autonomy, even as defence cooperation with Tokyo expands.

Misato Matsuoka, an associate professor at Teikyo University, said Indonesia’s value to Japan did not depend on Jakarta siding with Tokyo against Beijing. She described Tokyo’s approach as “influence without demanding alignment”.

Cooperation with Jakarta could show that Japan’s expanding security role did not necessarily amount to bloc-building, she said, a particularly significant point given Indonesia’s influence within Asean and the region’s broader preference for strategic autonomy.

Building on this perspective, an analysis for Singapore’s S. Rajaratnam School of International Studies by Chaula Rininta Anindya and Adhi Priamarizki described Jakarta’s ties with Japan as one of “strategic convenience” rather than alignment, while cautioning that Beijing might still view closer defence cooperation as an attempt to constrain China.

Maslow said Indonesia’s position as both a Japanese security partner and a close partner of China would test “how China will respond to new defence cooperation in the region”.

For Matsuoka, Japan’s longer-term influence could lie less in locking Indonesia into Japanese hardware than in making itself more relevant within the country’s security institutions through training, maintenance and other cooperation.

Japan’s influence, she said, could be greatest when it did not require Indonesia to become “Japan-aligned”.

According to Maslow, that reflects a broader transformation in Japan’s regional role. He said Tokyo was expanding its traditional economic role into the security sphere, moving “from building production networks to establishing defence networks”.
