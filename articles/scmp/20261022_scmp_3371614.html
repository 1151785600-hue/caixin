For Filipinos, virtual assistant trumps doctor as ‘dream job’. But what’s the reality?

SCMP | FREE | 1025 words | 2026-10-22

When Mark Ifurung left his 18-year career as an IT professional in May, he was happy to ditch his two-hour commutes from his home in Cavite province south of Manila to his office in Makati City.

So for his fresh start, the 40-year-old father of four wanted something that would allow him to work from home. “After being unemployed for five months, I decided to explore freelancing,” he said. “With my background in IT operations and application support, I realised I already have transferable skills that I can offer to clients.”

While job hunting online, he came across information on the “virtual assistant” industry and was lured by the idea of flexibility and attractive pay.

Ifurung is one of a growing number of Filipinos gravitating to virtual assistant work, a booming sector of the Philippines’ offshore labour industry that has steadily risen post-pandemic.

Virtual assistants provide administrative, technical or creative services to businesses and individuals, often overseas. Unlike traditional office workers, they typically work from home and may serve multiple clients on a freelance basis – handling tasks ranging from scheduling and customer support to social media management and IT services.

VA Masters, a virtual assistant placement agency, estimates there are more than 1 million virtual assistants in the Philippines. Filipinos make up about 38 per cent of the global market, often for clients and companies based in the US, the UK and Australia, according to the agency’s estimates.

A survey based on online job searches from May 2025 to May 2026 by remittance agency Remitly found that the role is now the top “dream job” among Filipinos, overtaking doctors.

“Virtual work in the Philippines is a fast-rising source of employment for Filipinos,” said Verna Dinah Viajar, an assistant professor at the University of the Philippines School of Labour and Industrial Relations.

Viajar said virtual assistant work had attracted Filipinos branching out from the business process outsourcing sector, after AI replaced many tasks in IT and communication such as call centres. “With adequate skills in IT communications, many [business process outsourcing] workers have shifted to VAs,” she said.

Work from home, earn abroad

Many virtual assistants in the Philippines are independent contractors who are not tied full time to a particular employer. This allows them to take on several clients at once and increase their income potential.

Erica Marie Ebrona, 29, manages seven clients from her home in Dumaguete City in central Philippines. “I work as a social media and content strategist, handling content planning, captions, strategy and analytics. I have flexible hours, so I can choose when I work as long as I meet deadlines,” she said.

Ebrona started as a virtual assistant in 2023, transitioning from human resources, and earned her first client through a referral.

While her monthly income varies, she earns at least 100,000 pesos (US$1,580) a month – over five times the minimum wage in Metro Manila. Virtual assistant work, she said, “offers flexibility and opportunities to work with international clients”.

Phu Huynh, a labour analyst for the International Labour Organization (ILO), said the strong interest in virtual assistant work “reflects the appeal of relatively accessible remote work that can connect Filipinos directly to international clients and potentially higher earnings without having to migrate abroad”.

A young, digitally connected, and English-speaking workforce made the Philippines an attractive location for outsourced labour – but did not necessarily guarantee decent working conditions, he added.

The industry’s unregulated nature leaves many workers open to abuse, according to experts. Most have no contracts, no social protection benefits, and are vulnerable to health issues due to working in different time zones. This lack of regulation could make some assistants “work like slaves in the cyberworld”, Viajar said.

Their geographical location also means they do not have redress from the head office. “Being in remote work, it is difficult to follow up work grievances, and their isolated work formats … robs them of the opportunity to exercise their rights as workers,” she said.

Most people are drawn to virtual assistant work because of “the prospect of building a better livelihood while remaining connected to their families and communities”, according to Alistair Mercado, CEO and founder of Kasama Connect, a technology platform focused on transparent agreements, payments and accountability for remote work.

But the dream should not stop there, he added. “It should include getting paid reliably, developing expertise and having a meaningful say in the terms of your work.”

To protect independent contractors, “we need to stop treating ‘be careful’ as a worker-protection system”, he said. “Professionals have responsibilities, but clients, platforms, intermediaries and government have responsibilities too.”

AI intervention

Artificial intelligence is another challenge to the industry.

“[The] work is exposed to AI because many routine tasks such as scheduling, data entry, drafting and basic customer support can increasingly be automated through AI technologies,” Huynh said. “However, ILO research suggests AI is more likely to transform many jobs than eliminate them entirely.”

Virtual assistant roles may shift towards more complex tasks requiring judgment, problem solving, managing client relationships and the ability to use AI effectively, he said. “The challenge will be ensuring workers are well equipped with these complementary skills.”

Viajar said that AI has become a necessity for virtual assistants. “AI can easily replace tasks that are repetitive but if the work still requires human cognitive abilities, AI becomes a tool for higher productivity and precision work,” she said.

Virtual assistant Dani Gregorio, 25, does not see AI fully replacing her job just yet.

“I can definitely see why people are worried. We’ve already seen how AI is changing the way different industries operate, and some roles … are being automated or reduced,” she said.

“But I also think we need to look at what AI can actually replace and what it can’t fully replicate … You can automate certain processes, but managing people, building trust, understanding human behaviour, and making decisions that consider the bigger picture require more than simply generating an answer. Emotional intelligence and accountability matter, too.”

Not everyone would have such skills – “and that’s OK”, Gregorio said. “We shouldn’t romanticise one kind of work just because it looks good on social media.”
