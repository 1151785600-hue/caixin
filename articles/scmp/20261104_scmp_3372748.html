Why some EU and Chinese firms are keen to cooperate – even as tensions simmer

SCMP | FREE | 1011 words | 2026-11-04

While trade tensions between Beijing and Brussels remain elevated, some European businesses are pointing to disruptions from the United States as a bigger challenge.

Instead, they are seeking closer cooperation with China, even as Chinese carmakers scramble to front-load exports ahead of potential future trade barriers.

At last week’s 19th Europe-China Business and Technology Cooperation Fair in the southwestern Chinese city of Chengdu, the appetite for closer business ties was evident.

Hundreds of Chinese and European companies across a wide range of sectors filled a hall for matchmaking sessions, rotating between tables to exchange ideas and seek potential partners and investors.

Some European business representatives said they were not too worried about tensions, instead seeing significant potential for cooperation in sectors such as automotive, renewable energy and agriculture.

The biggest challenge facing companies from both sides, however, was “the instability coming from America”, said Nuno Orfao, managing partner at Portuguese consulting firm Stream, pointing to uncertainty over US trade policy and its impact on business planning and investment.

“America is putting [up] a wall, which is affecting not only the relations that Europe has with them,” Orfao said. As China faced greater trade friction with the US than Europe, this created an additional incentive for Chinese and European companies to explore cooperation, he added.

Jorge Marques, CEO of Spain-headquartered digital marketplace Space Mall, echoed that view, saying higher US tariffs on the EU and other markets since last year had driven up costs for European producers in North America, forcing many to look east.

The market is in Asia. We must be ready to open deals and make really good trade with Asia – in general, with China

Jorge Marques, Space Mall

Citing wine prices as an example, Marques said European wine sold in the US was now roughly 15 to 25 per cent more expensive than three years ago, partly because of US tariffs imposed last year.

Europe must “open its mind” and recognise where actual growth lies, he noted. “The market is in Asia. What we have to face is [being] ready to open deals and make really good trade with Asia – in general, with China.”

Marques also warned that EU policies targeting China could ultimately benefit American businesses. Rising costs were discouraging European buyers from sourcing goods directly from China, leaving some to turn to Amazon instead, he said. “In the end who wins? American companies.”

Some Chinese cross-border sellers are also looking to diversify into Europe as US tariffs raise their costs.

Zhang Jinlong, a Chinese manufacturer and cross-border vendor of smart-home products, said he was considering entering the EU market and setting up service hubs after gaining a foothold in the US earlier this year.

“Tariff rates in Europe are generally much lower than in the US, where tariffs on our categories hover around 50 per cent,” he said.

The comments come as China’s widening trade surplus, which is on track to hit another record high this year, sparks widespread concern in the West. Beijing and Brussels had appeared to be on the verge of a trade war last month, before reaching a deal to cap Chinese hybrid car imports into the EU for four years.

Beijing also pledged to “continue to facilitate” export licence approvals for rare earths and permanent magnets destined for the EU, while both sides agreed to explore possible tariff cuts on certain goods.

Some business representatives argued that the answer to Chinese low-cost competition was not tariff barriers but greater cooperation in local manufacturing and technology.

European firms have to realise that competition with China is “inevitable”, particularly in the automotive sector, according to Rupert Varnai, CEO of advisory firm Cathay Partners. They need to “find an answer to that – and it is not sanctions or higher customs duties, but some kind of cooperation”, he said.

The need to find new markets has become more pressing amid Europe’s energy crisis and high inflation, which Varnai linked to the Russia-Ukraine war, the US-Israel war on Iran and recent protests in France and elsewhere.

“The solution is to open the doors and try to make big electric vehicle companies in China to invest there,” Orfao said.

Chinese carmakers and other manufacturers are increasingly building factories or forming joint ventures in Europe amid higher tariffs on imports and EU efforts to encourage local manufacturing.

BYD, for example, has said its plant in Hungary will begin mass production in the fourth quarter of this year and that it plans to build more factories in Europe.

China’s broader lead in electrification could also make its companies attractive partners as European economies seek to accelerate their green transitions.

The Electrification Competitiveness Index, released on Tuesday by climate non-profit We Mean Business Coalition, assessed 16 major economies and ranked China first for deployment, with a score of 68 per cent, reflecting strong uptake of EVs across vehicle categories and extensive charging infrastructure.

By contrast, the US faces policy reversals and grid bottlenecks, while countries including Germany, Italy, Poland and the United Kingdom have yet to translate supportive policies into comparable progress on the ground, the index showed.

For some Chinese carmakers, however, the immediate priority is to take advantage of the current window to ramp up exports before potential policy changes take effect.

A representative of Sichuan-based Chuanyun Heavy Industry Group, which is involved in the new energy vehicle business, said the recent China-EU deal had pushed back the prospect of punitive tariffs, giving exporters more time to ship vehicles to Europe.

The company, which previously focused on markets in Central Asia, was now exploring opportunities in Europe, the representative said. In the longer term, it was also considering setting up an assembly plant there to meet local rules-of-origin requirements and significantly reduce tariff costs, he added.

In September, China’s hybrid car exports to the EU tripled from a year earlier to nearly 100,000 vehicles, outpacing the 142.7 per cent year-on-year growth in China’s total passenger car exports to the bloc, customs data showed.
