Newcastle University Medicine Malaysia graduates in limbo after UK rule change

SCMP | FREE | 1179 words | 2026-08-12

When S* enrolled at Newcastle University Medicine Malaysia (NUMed Malaysia) in the Southeast Asian nation, the British-born Pakistani student believed the five-year medical degree would give him a realistic route into Britain’s National Health Service (NHS).

The pathway was never formally guaranteed, but S said NUMed had highlighted a decade-long record in which students who applied for the UK Foundation Programme (UKFP) – the supervised training route for medical graduates – had secured posts.

For international students such as S, who spent about US$134,500 on a Bachelor of Medicine and Bachelor of Surgery (MBBS) degree, the UK route was crucial because alternatives were limited.

Malaysian NUMed graduates could opt for housemanship locally, while NUMed says international graduates are not eligible for the pre-registration internship required to practise medicine in Malaysia.

Then, during S’s final year, the UK parliament changed the rules.

The Medical Training (Prioritisation) Act, which became law in March, gave priority for UKFP places to medical graduates who had completed most of their training in Britain.

The British government has said the policy aims to protect taxpayer investment in medical training, give domestically trained doctors a clearer route into the NHS and reduce reliance on overseas recruitment.

The law does not bar other applicants from applying, but changes the order in which posts are allocated.

Although S holds an MBBS awarded by Newcastle University and recognised by the General Medical Council (GMC) as a UK primary medical qualification, his five years of training in Malaysia mean he is allocated after priority applicants trained in Britain.

For students who had built their futures around the NUMed degree’s British status, the rule change has upended expectations about where it could take them after graduation.

As of July 30, Newcastle University told This Week in Asia that 68 of NUMed’s 102 eligible graduates had secured UKFP posts, leaving 34 without confirmed placements.

S remains among those without a post.

Across Britain, the UK Foundation Programme Office initially placed 738 non-priority applicants on a reserve list after allocating 10,810 priority-group individuals in March.

Reserve-list applicants are offered posts only as vacancies emerge after the priority group has been allocated, using a computer-generated rank and applicants’ preferences.

By the end of July, 204 remained on the reserve list, and a fifth and final allocation round from the reserve applicant list is slated for Wednesday.

Not guaranteed

“It [the UK pathway] was not something that was guaranteed, but this was one of the selling points which the university used. Everyone who applied from Newcastle University to UK foundation training got a UKFP position,” S said.

He acknowledged that prospective students had been told placement ultimately rested with British authorities, but said the experience of previous NUMed cohorts shaped how he and his classmates planned their careers.

“We saw four batches of our seniors, they all got it [training posts].”

Newcastle University said access to postgraduate training was never guaranteed and its published information always stated that routes including UKFP were subject to change.

The university added that the UKFP became a mainstream route for NUMed graduates from 2017, with over 600 graduates entering its training pipeline. Previously, most had undergone housemanship training in Malaysia or used overseas routes.

“The trend since 2017 has moved sharply towards UKFP: 31 per cent in 2019, 64 per cent in 2020, 75 per cent in 2021, and 83 per cent by 2025, with the rest going into Malaysian housemanship or other pathways each year,” the university said.

“That rise tracked NHS workforce pressure and a relaxing of UK immigration rules for overseas medical graduates from 2020. This is also the first cohort to graduate under the new prioritisation rules.”

The university said it wrote to students within two days of the bill’s introduction to parliament in January, tracked its progress and held briefings and town halls.

After the bill became law in March, the university confirmed to students that their eligibility to apply for the UKFP had not changed and individual support was available.

S said the change nevertheless came too late for final-year students to seek alternatives.

“They changed it mid-cycle, leaving us completely stranded,” S said.

Few alternatives

For international graduates, the alternatives are limited.

S’s family lives in a third country where he spent most of his life before medical school, but practising there would require accreditation and a medical licensing examination in a language in which he did not study medicine.

He estimated that the Australian Medical Council pathway would cost another US$7,000 to US$8,000.

Instead, he is pursuing the United States Medical Licensing Examination after completing a two-month elective at a US university while also considering research and a PhD.

“Whatever I had planned for five years has been pretty much down the drain because of whatever changes have taken place,” he said.

In a written reply to This Week in Asia, the GMC said NUMed’s MBBS remained recognised as a UK primary medical qualification despite the policy change.

“Newcastle University’s Medicine Malaysia campus is an overseas campus of a UK medical school. Its medical degree is recognised by the GMC as a UK primary medical qualification, and there have been no changes to that recognition,” it said.

The regulator has stressed, however, that deciding who receives a UKFP post is the remit of the programme office.

The UK Foundation Programme Office and Department of Health and Social Care did not respond to This Week In Asia’s requests for comment.

The change has forced current students to rethink their future plans.

Unaizah Ahmed, a fourth-year NUMed student from Pakistan living in the United Arab Emirates, said that earlier cohorts had reinforced students’ expectations that NUMed graduates could progress to UK training. Since the rule change, she and her classmates have been looking more seriously at alternatives including Australia and the US.

“I’d be lying if I said I am not concerned about my future with all these changes,” she said. “So far, my main goal is to complete my studies first, and I shall look at my options during or after I’m almost done with my final year.”

Newcastle University said it recognised that “this is a particularly stressful time” for students who could not secure UKFP posts, and was determined to support them.

Professor Geoffrey Williams, a higher education analyst and visiting fellow at Universiti Malaya, said the rule change exposed a distinction between the regulatory status of a degree awarded by an overseas British campus and its practical value for graduates seeking UK training.

NUMed’s qualification had not lost regulatory recognition, he said, but the difference lay in its practical value for graduates seeking priority access to the NHS training system.

“The loss of value is the difference between immediate priority access versus stand-in-line certificates,” he said.

“A Malaysian student who studied in the UK has immediate priority access to the UK training programme. A British student who studied in the Malaysian franchise campus as a foreign student here would stand in line too when she went home.”

*Name changed to protect interviewee’s identity
