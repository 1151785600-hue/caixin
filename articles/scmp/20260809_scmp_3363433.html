How China’s ‘magic’ rice seeds are transforming the fortunes of Nigerian farmers

SCMP | FREE | 1277 words | 2026-08-09

Just outside the Nigerian capital Abuja, where Green Agriculture West Africa Limited (Gawal) is based, locals do not refer to the company by name, instead as the place where the Chinese supply “magic” rice seeds.

Ahmed Yusuf, 42, credits the seeds with turning around his rice business for good. Before Gawal, Yusuf’s yields averaged just 2 tonnes per hectare, putting him on the verge of forsaking farming after more than a decade of toiling.

“Each year I was making losses, and I couldn’t meet the needs of my family, so I thought, ‘what was the point?’” he recalled.

Yusuf’s fortunes changed after he was introduced to Gawal’s R1 rice strain.

“With this new variety I have been able to stay in business by increasing my yield more than threefold on basically the same effort,” he said.

Yusuf’s success is part of a broader China-backed push to transform African subsistence farming into a highly productive mechanised powerhouse. But this agricultural diplomacy is about more than food security.

Beijing’s hands-off, state-backed approach is directly challenging traditional Western aid models, prompting Washington to counter with its own multimillion-dollar agribusiness interventions to maintain influence on the continent.

Gawal is among a growing number of Chinese companies finding business opportunities in African agriculture, which smallholder farmers dominate.

According to officials, the companies are deploying new techniques, using demonstration farms to accelerate local adoption and help build value chains linking the farms to markets.

Gawal is a joint venture between Yuan Longping High-Tech Agriculture, which is headquartered in Changsha in China’s central Hunan province, and state-owned Beijing-based CGCOC Group.

Founded in 2006, Gawal has spearheaded China’s agricultural diplomacy in Africa, producing new seeds and offering machinery, field expertise and processing capacity.

Its 2,000-hectare (4,900-acre) demonstration farm in Nigeria’s northwestern Kebbi state provides seeds and training to more than 5,000 smallholder rice farmers who also have access to modern technologies under a programme linked to the Nigerian government.

“Seeing is believing,” said Godfrey Ngwoke, an agricultural development programme manager with Nigeria’s agriculture ministry, of the demonstration farm.

“These demonstrations showcase the superiority of new crop varieties and techniques, helping farmers make informed decisions.”

Gawal’s R1 rice variety is among its most popular products. Introduced in 2017 for its resistance to disease and ability to grow on diverse terrain, it yields between 7.5 and 8 tonnes per hectare and became popular with local farmers like Yusuf.

Other varieties, such as Faro 69 and Faro 70, have even greater yield capabilities that range from 11 to 13 tonnes per hectare. Their widespread acceptance has helped boost Nigeria’s rice output by as much as 2 million tonnes a year, according to the company.

Nigeria now consumes an estimated 10.5 million tonnes of rice every year while producing about 7 million tonnes, according to data from the central bank.

Xu Guoxin is part of a team comprising six Chinese and two Nigerians who developed Gawal’s R1.

Holding a bachelor’s degree in agriculture and postgraduate specialisation in crop genetics and breeding, Xu came to Nigeria in 2014, encouraged by the country’s sizeable arable land and large population.

“My professional work focuses on crop variety development, seed systems, agricultural technology promotion and international agricultural cooperation,” Xu said.

“I chose to work in Nigeria because agriculture here has huge development potential and significant opportunities for impact.”

Despite Nigeria’s considerable natural resources and strong workforce, there is still a significant “need for improved technologies, quality seeds and modern farming systems”, Xu added.

This is where Gawal is asserting its presence. Aside from rice, the company supplies improved varieties of maize, millet, peanuts, sorghum and other local staples.

The project falls under a Beijing-backed programme touting “China aid for a shared future” for Africa, emphasising the benefits of shared development.

Xu said Nigeria was chosen for its “leading role in West Africa” and for being “an important hub for regional agricultural cooperation and food security initiatives”.

Beyond Nigeria, CGCOC Group is involved in agricultural cooperation initiatives in Burkina Faso, Ethiopia, Guinea and Zimbabwe.

In addition, China is currently engaged in major agricultural projects in more than a dozen countries across Africa, including what it describes as “friendship farms” in Mozambique and Tanzania.

According to rice farmer Chieke Agu, who gets his seeds from Gawal: “It is packaged as aid, but it is also showing us the Chinese way and encouraging us to use their agricultural technology.”

“We can say that Western aid does the same thing.”

The company’s demonstration farm in Abuja boasts hundreds of newly imported tractor engines for supply to Nigerian farmers and serves as a showroom for Chinese equipment and technology.

Still, Xu cited winning the support and trust of the government and local communities as Gawal’s major achievement.

“Government agencies appreciate the company’s contributions to seed development, agricultural technology transfer and farmer capacity building,” he said.

“Farmers have also responded positively because improved seeds and better cultivation practices have helped increase yields and incomes.”

In support of Chinese efforts, the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), a United Nations agency, started a programme in March to accelerate the mechanisation of African agriculture through the exchange of information in collaboration with the UN Office for South-South Cooperation in China.

The objective was to create a platform to exchange information on best practices in agricultural mechanisation among researchers, entrepreneurs, government officials and farmers from Africa and Asia, as well as other regions.

“Agriculture mechanisation is an essential pillar in the African transformation agenda,” said Meshack Malo, the FAO’s deputy regional director for Africa. “Scalable mechanisation is not an option but urgent.”

To counter China’s perceived influence in Africa’s agribusiness, the US has weighed in with its own interventions.

While Beijing favours big agricultural projects, Washington has focused on private-sector support, mostly aimed at mitigating risk and improving access to finance.

For instance, the US Department of Agriculture provides commercial credit guarantees under its GSM 102 programme to back exports of American agricultural products and the Facility Guarantee Programme for related equipment.

It also provides inputs such as special seeds and fertiliser to support crops such as maize, rice, cowpea and wheat.

China’s agricultural objectives in Africa, as articulated by its Ministry of Foreign Affairs, are to support the African Union’s Agenda 2063.

The main goal is to achieve continental food security by transforming subsistence farming into highly productive mechanised agriculture.

Nigeria appeared to endorse this approach when it announced a US$900 million deal with China in March to establish six giant poultry farms across the country.

Located on farms measuring 7,000 to 10,000 hectares, each will have the capacity to produce a million eggs daily. They will also be supported by feed mills and power stations, as well as feature maize and soybean farms to generate the feed.

Beijing’s decision to implement a zero-tariff regime for African exports means that a significant amount of product surpluses will have the opportunity to be exported to China, given its position as a major destination of African agricultural exports, according to a study led by Zhao Wenjie of China Agricultural University in Beijing.

Published in the peer-reviewed journal Sustainability in March, the study said the contrasting approaches adopted by Western countries and China had influenced project outcomes.

Western aid often comes with exacting conditions involving human rights, good governance and environmental protection.

“While these standards are important, they can sometimes be perceived as intrusive or paternalistic by African nations,” wrote Zhao and his colleagues.

“In contrast, China’s non-conditional approach provides a sense of relief to these countries, allowing for more flexible and pragmatic cooperation agreements.”
