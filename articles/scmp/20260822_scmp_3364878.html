Why China’s reusable rocket breakthrough matters for the PLA’s ‘kill chain’

SCMP | FREE | 1013 words | 2026-08-22

A pair of landmark rocket recoveries in recent weeks has brought China a step closer to mastering reusable launch technology – a breakthrough analysts said could give the People’s Liberation Army an advantage in combat by increasing the resilience of its satellite-supported “kill chain”, the process of identifying, tracking and engaging a target.

In a first for China, commercial rocket company LandSpace recovered a stainless-steel booster using deployable legs after an orbital flight earlier this week. This followed a successful launch last month by state-owned China Aerospace Science and Technology Corporation (CASC), which achieved the world’s first recovery of an orbital-class booster at sea using a net-capture system.

Defence experts said the technology would dramatically lower the cost of putting satellites into space and increase the frequency of launches. Mastering reusability will improve the PLA’s space capabilities – including communication, navigation and surveillance – much like how SpaceX supports the US military.

Malcolm Davis, a senior analyst with the Canberra-based Australian Strategic Policy Institute’s defence strategy programme, said space was the most crucial operational domain in modern warfare.

He said modern militaries relied heavily on space for everything from communications and battlespace awareness to positioning, navigation and timing using systems such as GPS.

Without those abilities, militaries could be left “effectively deaf, dumb and blind”, severely degrading their ability to communicate, understand what is happening on the battlefield and conduct operations, Davis added.

SpaceX has become a core enabler of US military space power. It provides rockets and relatively low-cost access to orbit to deploy and replenish large military satellite constellations through US Space Force projects, such as the Proliferated Warfighter Space Architecture.

LandSpace and CASC have yet to demonstrate the ability to reuse the recovered first-stage booster, but experts said reusability itself was the bottleneck.

Timothy Heath, director of the Virginia-based research firm Perceptum, said the principal advantage for the People’s Liberation Army would be the ability to replace or increase space capabilities during a conflict.

“In a conflict, the reusable rocket launch capability would allow the PLA to quickly recover from losses of space platforms or to expand space capabilities quickly,” he said.

“This could enable the PLA to maintain situational awareness, navigation and communications even as it loses satellites to adversary attacks.”

Namrata Goswami, a professor at Johns Hopkins University’s School of Advanced International Studies, said these reusable launch systems would become part of an integrated national infrastructure supporting commercial mega-constellations, military satellites, logistics and ultimately greater freedom of action in space.

She said reusable rockets, combined with replacement satellites and rapid launch operations, would be particularly important in a Taiwan contingency because the PLA increasingly relied on satellites for intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance (ISR) as well as communications, BeiDou navigation and targeting across the Indo-Pacific.

“China is moving towards near-real time situational awareness that can support the tracking and targeting of mobile US and allied forces,” Goswami said.

“If damaged ISR or communications nodes can be replenished rapidly by China, disrupting the space layer may produce only temporary degradation rather than breaking the kill chain.”

Lieutenant General Douglas Schiess, the incoming US chief of space operations, said during his July nomination hearing that “the threat China poses is real”. He urged the US Space Force to strengthen its ability in the region to counter threats such as the PLA’s rapidly expanding space operations, particularly in the Indo-Pacific.

“They are using their intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance efforts to target our forces much farther than they ever have in the past. They’ve built a kill chain to be able to see our carrier strike groups, our bombers at much farther pace, speed and distances,” Schiess said.

In July 2023, China deployed swarms of satellites to monitor the Talisman Sabre joint military exercise between the US, Australia and other partners, ABC News reported at the time.

According to the Australian news service, 300 Chinese satellites conducted more than 3,000 overflights to survey ground-based activities.

China in 2024 established the PLA Aerospace Force, placing it directly under the Central Military Commission.

“I think the US will probably recognise that they have to live with the fact that the Chinese will be able to put satellites into orbit very quickly in the same way the Americans can,” Davis said.

“And I think that where the contest happens is not so much on Earth in terms of the US destroying Chinese launch systems or China destroying US launch systems. The contest happens in space where you have to fight in space on orbit.”

Space has become a key area of US-China competition. While China is still a few years behind the US, which has now moved to tackle full reusability with the Starship programme, experts said LandSpace’s achievements would help narrow the gap and erode the American advantage in space.

“Dropping the cost to launch will open up China’s space sector and encourage a lot of innovation and new space technologies that could have benefits for its national security,” said Victoria Samson, chief director of the space security and stability programme at the Washington-based Secure World Foundation.

“This might allow China to catch up to the United States in terms of space launch.”

Analysts said that the ability to produce satellites was also critical. Goswami said reusable rockets could become consequential when combined with China’s development of mass-produced satellites and large constellations.

As of April, China had 55 satellite manufacturing facilities with a combined annual production capacity expected to reach 7,360 satellites by 2030, according to Beijing-based research firm Hello Space.

SpaceX said in a regulatory filing earlier this year that it was able to produce an average of about 70 Starlink satellites per week between December and April, equivalent to roughly 3,640 satellites a year.

“We have seen Ukraine’s ability to use SpaceX’s Starlink satellites to fight Russia has become an important source of advantage. If China can launch many military satellites quickly and cheaply, this could provide a critical edge in battle,” Heath said.
