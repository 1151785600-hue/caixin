Antwerp: a well-seasoned melting pot of Rubens, diamonds and Belgium’s only Chinatown

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2205 words | 2026-10-09

Tourists visiting the Belgian city of Antwerp are usually drawn by its reputation as the cradle of Flemish culture.

This was home to the Baroque painter Peter Paul Rubens (1577-1640), whose masterpieces decorate churches across the city, including St Charles Borromeo Church and the soaring Cathedral of Our Lady. Grand squares and marketplaces are lined with flamboyant Renaissance guild-houses and palatial merchants’ mansions dating back to a golden age, at the beginning of the 16th century, when Antwerp’s port was the undisputed capital of world trade. And then there is the Royal Museum of Fine Arts, which showcases an unparalleled collection of Low Countries old masters, from Rubens, Jan Brueghel the Elder (1568-1625) and Jan van Eyck (1390-1441) to modern artists such as René Magritte (1898-1967), Rik Wouters (1882-1916) and James Ensor (1860-1949). But Flemish heritage is just one face of this city of 568,000 people.

On closer inspection, the city reveals itself to be a colourful melting pot of cultures, cuisines and religions, those of generations of immigrants who have chosen to make their home in Antwerp.

Jews began to settle in the city after their expulsion from Spain in the late 15th century, and their descendants transformed it into a global centre of the diamond trade. Belgium’s 19th century empire resulted in immigration from the Maghreb and Central Africa, and after World War II, a wave of settlers from Hong Kong and mainland China arrived, creating what is today the only official Chinatown in the country. The latest additions to the city’s cosmopolitan character have been refugees fleeing conflict in countries such as Afghanistan, Syria, Iraq, Somalia and Ukraine.

Just 40 minutes north by train from the Belgian capital, Brussels, Antwerp’s late-19th century Central Station has a lavish dome, a sweeping marble staircase, frescoes and stained-glass windows that periodically win the building awards for “World’s Most Beautiful Station” in travel magazines.

Walk out of what locals call their “railway cathedral” and you plunge into a harmonious clash of cultures: on one side, the ornamental pagoda gate at the entrance to Van Wesenbekestraat that marks the beginning of Chinatown; on the other, Pelikaanstraat, home to a tight-knit Orthodox Jewish community and its diamond stores.

One-hundred-and-twenty boutiques full of glittering, dazzling diamonds and gold jewellery are crammed into this single square mile, though it is a wonder how they all survive when they seem to be selling the same items.

“I came to Antwerp 35 years ago, from Israel, though my family is originally from Georgia,” says Yosef Degan, standing proudly next to a replica of a gold bullion bar in his temptingly named store, Be Very Rich. “I am one of the last authentic goldsmiths here, because apart from dealing diamonds, I specialise in buying old gold, melting it down and creating my own unique jewellery designs.”

Although Yiddish is the lingua franca for the hundreds of Jewish dealers, brokers, cutters and jewellers working here, and the kippah skullcap and tzitzit trouser tassels can be seen everywhere, Degan insists that his community remains discreet.

I take his recommendation to have lunch nearby, at Hoffy’s, a kosher diner filled with noisy diamond dealers, whose owners, three Hoffman brothers, explain their Hasidic Ashkenazi culture to customers through their unique cuisine: in my case, a slow-cooked cholent of baked beef and vegetables, kugel potato pancakes, veal schnitzel, poached gefilte fish and salt-beef pastrami.

Another world awaits through the jewel-toned archway that stands at the entrance of Van Wesenbekestraat, ablaze in radiant reds and brilliant golds and proudly flanked by two sculpted stone lions.

Attracted by the city’s port, Chinese sailors and dock workers began settling in Antwerp from the early 20th century. They were followed in the late 1940s by larger numbers of mainly Cantonese-speaking immigrants from Hong Kong (particularly the New Territories) and southern China, who were escaping political turmoil and a lack of jobs, and were drawn by a post-war European revival and what was known as the “chop-suey boom”.

As with all Chinatowns, the restaurant business was the obvious line of work for most of these new immigrants, and from the 70s, the closeness to Antwerp’s railway station helped solidify Van Wesenbekestraat and the surrounding area as a genuine community.

Recognised as Belgium’s sole official Chinatown in 2001, the neighbourhood’s culinary scene has mushroomed from the early Cantonese restaurants to an eclectic kaleidoscope of Thai, Philippine, Malaysian and Nepalese eateries. A dozen bustling Asian supermarkets rub shoulders with two martial-arts schools, traditional-medicine establishments, a lion-dance school and the Fo Guang Shan Belgique Temple, located not in an ornate pagoda but in a simple Flemish house on a terraced row, painted a distinctive yellow, with a wrought-iron-framed window in the front door.

Much of the fare on offer in Chinatown is cheap and cheerful street food – hand-pulled noodles, char siu pork, wonton soup, even the classic sweet and sour chicken – but two addresses stand out, both just off the main drag of Van Wesenbekestraat.

Fong Mei opened more than 30 years ago and its classic Cantonese cuisine – more than 50 handmade varieties of dim sum alongside steamed fish with ginger and coriander, lacquered roast duck, beef-tripe soup and duck tongue – has been recognised by Belgium’s Michelin Guide. The decor is delightfully kitsch, with red lanterns, giant photos of Hong Kong, even a poster depicting a dragon boat powering through a village scene and celebrating Chinese-Belgian friendship.

The proprietor at the hole-in-the-wall Ni Shifu Sichuan Cuisine & Fondue, “Master Ni”, as the chef-owner describes himself, came to Antwerp 25 years ago, from Chengdu, and still does not speak a word of Flemish or English, but he certainly cooks up a storm in his tiny kitchen, dressed in shorts and a T-shirt, bent over his smoky wok. His dan dan noodles are wickedly spicy, the best I have eaten outside Asia, with the small dining room packed with a mix of Flemish locals, Antwerp Chinese and savvy visiting foodies. Ni Shifu has been singled out for Michelin praise for its authentic Sichuan cuisine and booking in advance is recommended.

Antwerp’s crucial bridge to the world, the docks – alongside the Scheldt river – are still among the busiest in Europe, but much of the surrounding Het Eilandje neighbourhood has been redeveloped. Its cutting-edge architecture includes the abstract pile of towering red-sandstone blocks that is the MAS maritime, history and cultural museum and Zaha Hadid’s metallic Port House, which resembles the shimmering prow of a floating ship.

Less conspicuous is the old red-brick warehouse that houses the Red Star Line Museum, a memorial to the millions of emigrants who passed through Antwerp on their way to a new life in North America. This intensely personal collection graphically illustrates the stories of some of the two million people who transited through Antwerp between 1873 and 1924, often after a long, tortuous journey from Central or Eastern Europe. Here, they endured medical examinations, disinfection and exhaustive immigration checks before finally being allowed to board a Red Star Line ship bound, in most cases, for New York. Most were fleeing poverty and persecution, little different from today’s migrants, and the anonymous tales are just as moving as those of the more famous émigrés who set sail on the Red Star Line: theoretical physicist Albert Einstein, composer Irving Berlin and future Israeli prime minister Golda Meir.

Just around the corner, in the Montevideo area – Antwerp’s quays are named after the exotic ports that produce was exported to and imported from – celebrity chef Nick Bril has just relocated his two-Michelin-star restaurant, The Jane, into a minimalist dining room, a striking departure from its original location, in a deconsecrated chapel downtown.

“Choosing Het Eilandje felt like a very logical step,” explains Bril, who has appeared on television in Belgian reality show Special Forces: Who Dares Wins, has served as a judge on Celebrity MasterChef Vlaanderen and is a renowned DJ. “It is an area where Antwerp’s history and future come together in a very natural way. The city has always drawn its strength from its openness to the world, and though our cuisine is firmly rooted in Belgium and in the products that surround us, it has always been influenced by experiences beyond our own borders.”

Gert de Mangeleer is present for every service in his kitchen at the two-Michelin-star Hertog Jan at Botanic, overseeing a tasting menu that is his own interpretation of a Japanese omakase experience.

“When I decided to open my restaurant in Antwerp, it was the perfect inspiration for my cuisine: this cosmopolitan harbour city that is an entrepôt for spices and exotic produce from around the globe,” says de Mangeleer, who gained prominence as part of the Flanders Kitchen Rebels campaign, a movement of young, talented chefs who brought a modern, rebellious attitude to traditional Belgian gastronomy.

His diners are tempted with dishes such as Japanese binchotan-grilled Wagyu beef served with locally smoked eel sauce, crispy veal sweetbreads, steamed dumplings of spinach dim sum and a sauce of Zeeland oysters.

One of Belgium’s two three-Michelin-star restaurants, Zilte, stands in the old docks area of Antwerp, but a favourite haunt of local chefs and sommeliers – evidenced by the fact that it’s open on Mondays, a traditional day off for restaurant staff – is the casual bistro Invincible. Here, animatedly supervising a brigade of cooks, Kenny Burssens interprets traditional Flemish cuisine, sautéing crunchy Brussels sprouts with bone marrow, and pairing tiny girolle mushrooms with tender chunks of milky cows’ udder, or line-caught cod with the salty sea-vegetable salicorne. The wine list includes vintages from little-known Belgian vineyards.

A little different is a meal at Samenloop, a restaurant that occupies the ground floor and cobbled courtyard of the Maagdenhuis, which was opened in 1552 as an orphanage for girls and also houses a museum and a chapel. The recently opened Samenloop is a social project for refugees: its waiters from Afghanistan and Palestine, and kitchen staff from Eritrea, the Maldives and Colombia are offered hospitality training so they can better integrate into society. One of a changing cast of celebrated chefs (for two months from November, it will be Karen Shu, an American-Filipino chef with a restaurant near Antwerp who is known for vegan and plant-based cuisine) collaborates by selecting recipes from the homelands of the refugees and adapting them into a tasting menu: crispy Nepalese pani puri surprisingly stuffed with chopped local asparagus; traditional Syrian falafel with fresh green peas; a light Ukrainian strawberry sernik cheesecake.

Another string to Antwerp’s bow is its reputation as a cutting-edge capital of European fashion. The Fashion Museum, known as MoMu, is a hotbed of creation, its studios and ateliers training the next generation of designers.

The city broke onto the international fashion scene in the 80s, with the rise of the Antwerp Six: revolutionary stylists who all graduated from the city’s Royal Academy of Fine Arts and colonised the pages of Vogue, Elle and Harper’s Bazaar.

“[Antwerp] is the perfect place to work,” one of the six, designer Walter Van Beirendonck, told Surface Magazine in 2016. “It’s not expensive, it’s quiet and it’s close to Paris, Milan and London. The art is great, the food is good, you can live well. There’s a real sense of possibility here.”

The visionary designs and eye-catching fashion shows of the likes of Dries Van Noten, Ann Demeulemeester and Van Beirendonck can now be seen at the MoMu exhibition “Antwerp Six”, which runs until January 17, 2027.

Another innovative temporary show can be found at the monumental Royal Museum of Fine Arts, with its colonnaded facade, neoclassical architecture, marble staircase and huge exhibition rooms. “KMSKA x Young Fashion Designers” (until November 8) gives free rein to 10 young graduates from the Antwerp Fashion Academy to create fashion sculptures influenced by pieces in the museum’s collection, inspiration having come from the dramatic flowing robes with cascading folds of fabric often depicted by Rubens and the revolutionary slashed canvases of Lucio Fontana (1899-1968).

A fitting way to end an exploration of eclectic, multi-ethnic Antwerp is to head for the Theaterplein square on a Saturday, the venue for the Exotic Market.

As Chinese immigrants were arriving after the end of World War II, migrants from countries such as Spain, Greece, Turkey and Morocco settled here, too, bringing the spices and ingredients of their native cuisines. They started selling their wares at the Theaterplein every Saturday, at what was first termed the Foreigners’ Market.

Teeming with locals and tourists from 8am to 4pm, this is more a multicultural, all-day party than a commercial centre, where the cava flows and food trucks serve customers sitting on temporary terraces a kaleidoscope of global cuisine: cheesy Turkish börek; plump Tibetan momo dumplings; Pakistani palak paneer; Peruvian panini; North African couscous and tagines; Vietnamese glass noodles. And to remind you that this is still Antwerp, there are also Flemish specialities, such as matjes (herrings), local oysters and bowls of karakol, a tasty soup of whelks and celery.
