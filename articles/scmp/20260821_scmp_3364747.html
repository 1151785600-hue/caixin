Hong Kong Tiananmen vigil organiser, former leaders guilty of inciting subversion

SCMP | FREE | 1009 words | 2026-08-21

The now-disbanded alliance behind Hong Kong’s annual Tiananmen Square vigil and two of its former leaders have been found guilty of inciting subversion under the national security law by advocating an end to “one-party dictatorship” in mainland China.

In a 216-page judgment delivered at West Kowloon Court on Friday, three High Court judges ruled that any attempt to overthrow or undermine the Communist Party of China’s leadership could constitute a subversion offence because the country’s constitution does not permit Western-style democracy.

They found the Hong Kong Alliance in Support of Patriotic Democratic Movements of China, its former chairman Lee Cheuk-yan and former vice-chairwoman Chow Hang-tung had incited public hatred towards the party by likening Beijing’s crackdown on pro-democracy protesters in 1989 to local authorities’ suppression of the 2019 anti-government protests.

While stressing it was not unlawful for the alliance to organise vigils to mark the crackdown, the panel said the defendants had used the annual event to attract new supporters and instigate them to take action to oust the ruling party.

The group had drummed up public support through the operation of the June 4 museum, publication of periodicals and messages posted on its website, the court ruled.

“The evidence of this case showed that even after the implementation of the national security law, [the defendants] still continued to advocate and spread the goal of ‘end one-party dictatorship’ … thereby stirring up the hostility of their target audience towards the [party] as well as eroding and undermining the public’s confidence in its leadership,” the judges said.

“They did not genuinely and honestly believe that their words and deeds were not in breach of the national security law. They just refused to pull back from the brink and were bent on embracing the alliance’s resistance line to the very end.”

Justices Alex Lee Wan-tang, Johnny Chan Jong-herng and Anna Lai Yuen-kee will hear oral mitigation arguments on August 28 before passing sentences.

A government spokesman praised the verdict as a reasoned judgment, while accusing “external forces” of making “blatant and persistent” attempts to vilify local authorities through political manoeuvres and misleading statements.

Chief Executive John Lee Ka-chiu said the alliance had long sown hatred of Beijing and the party to incite the public to subvert state power. He added that anyone who tried to “shift the goalposts” or cover up their crimes with “clever words and flattery” would not escape justice.

Police Chief Superintendent Steve Li Kwai-wah, of the force’s national security department, declined to say whether future discussions of the 1989 crackdown would constitute an offence. He said event organisers had to comply with relevant laws.

Former lawmaker Lee and Chow, a barrister, have been remanded in custody for nearly five years. A fourth defendant, former vice-chairman Albert Ho Chun-yan, has also been detained for more than four years and is awaiting sentencing after pleading guilty to the offence in January.

Lee’s wife, Elizabeth Tang Yin-ngor, said she was “very disappointed and angry” with the ruling because she felt the judges did not give sufficient consideration to the defence’s arguments and evidence.

Chow’s mother said she hoped that “flowers of all colours can blossom”.

The court heard that putting an end to “one-party dictatorship” was among the alliance’s five operational objectives, which also included vindicating the 1989 pro-democracy movement in Beijing and building a democratic China.

The trial centred on Article 1 of the Chinese constitution, which was amended in 2018 to describe the party’s leadership as the “defining feature of socialism with Chinese characteristics”.

The judges noted that the article effectively required the country to be run solely by the party as a socialist state. The role of other “democratic” parties, they said, was restricted to taking part in the “patriotic united front” under the party’s leadership.

The court found that overthrowing the country’s fundamental system could involve an extrinsic force causing the regime to “lose its existing order or status” over a lengthy period and that a system could be undermined through “sowing discord, creating division and causing others to lose confidence in the system”.

The defence contended that all the alliance’s objectives were a legitimate exercise of fundamental human rights.

Lee testified that he did not oppose party rule on the mainland but said the one-party system was incompatible with the constitution’s preamble, which provides for “multiparty cooperation and political consultation” under the party’s leadership.

Chow argued that advocating an end to one-party rule was not the same as seeking to oust the party from its leading position. She added that pursuing democratic change in society should not be considered illegal in the first place.

The judges rejected their assertions, saying the defendants did not simply seek more diverse political participation on the mainland but hoped to overthrow and undermine the party.

They said Lee, Ho and Chow had all along been hostile towards the regime and refused to make any compromise.

Following the mass arrest and prosecution of prominent mainland dissidents who advocated sweeping political reform in a 2008 manifesto known as “Charter 08”, the defendants never saw any opportunity for a peaceful dialogue with central authorities or any chance that Beijing would agree to amend the constitution again, the judges added.

Set up after the 1989 crackdown, the alliance organised annual candlelight vigils in Hong Kong’s Victoria Park to mark the event and call for accountability and democratic reforms across the border.

The event had been the only large-scale commemoration of the crackdown held on Chinese soil.

Police prohibited the vigil for the first time in 2020 on public health grounds related to the Covid-19 pandemic. The 2021 event was banned for similar reasons before the alliance disbanded in September that year.

The judges echoed prosecutors’ characterisation that the trial was not politically motivated and was not designed to punish dissidents.

Inciting subversion in Hong Kong carries a maximum sentence of 10 years’ imprisonment under the national security law.
