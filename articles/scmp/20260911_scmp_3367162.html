Fresh photos offer clearest view yet of China’s ‘WZ-X’ spy drone

SCMP | FREE | 1088 words | 2026-09-11

Fresh clues are emerging about a new type of Chinese drone being developed as a long-range intelligence and surveillance platform intended to operate deep inside contested airspace.

New photographs circulated on Chinese internet forums earlier this month that appear to show the massive drone in the air, offering much clearer views of its wing shape than earlier images. The original source of the photos and exactly where they were taken have not been independently verified.

Andreas Rupprecht, a specialist on Chinese military aviation, said the photographs showed the clearest view yet of the aircraft’s serrated trailing edge – a sawtooth-like shape along the rear of the wing characteristic of stealth design. He added that the aircraft appeared to have been flown in daylight, suggesting such flights might have reached a degree of normality.

Rupprecht said he was “100 per cent certain” the photographs showed what military aviation watchers have dubbed the “WZ-X”, citing its dimensions, proportions and other visible details. Beijing has not publicly confirmed the aircraft’s designation, purpose or specifications.

The aircraft was first publicly identified by US defence website TWZ in satellite imagery from May last year showing the Chinese air force’s Malan test facility in Xinjiang Uygur autonomous region. Its estimated wingspan of around 52 metres (170 feet) is comparable to that of a US B-2 stealth bomber.

Rupprecht said the aircraft was designed as an intercontinental high-altitude, long-endurance (Hale) intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance (ISR) drone – a platform intended to operate deep inside defended airspace and function as an advanced communications relay node.

He likened its probable role to that of the RQ-180 – a secretive US high-altitude stealth flying-wing drone believed to be designed for reconnaissance inside defended airspace – describing the Chinese aircraft as an “RQ-180 XXL”.

He stressed that the comparison was about the expected mission and likely sensor role rather than proven performance, saying it remained unclear whether the WZ-X’s larger airframe translated into greater altitude, range or endurance.

Michael Bosack, director of the Parley Policy Institute, said the WZ-X was likely intended as a strategic high-altitude reconnaissance platform.

Compared with smaller medium-altitude, long-endurance drones, large Hale aircraft could offer advantages in speed, survivability and capacity for intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance payloads, according to Bosack, a former US Air Force drone maintenance unit commander who worked on MQ-9 Reaper strike-capable drones.

“The speed comes from the larger engine and allows the aircraft to get to its target faster,” he said. “The survivability comes from its ability to fly higher than traditional air-defence systems can detect and reach.”

He added that the WZ-X’s airframe appeared to be shaped to minimise its radar signature. Its high operating altitude could protect it from many conventional air-defence threats, he said.

Bosack said a practical challenge would be shelter and maintenance, since keeping it out of satellite view would require sufficient hangar space.

But he cautioned that size and design alone would not determine whether the aircraft became operationally useful.

“Airframes like this are meaningless without the systems they carry,” Bosack said. “However, if they can achieve the right flight profile and carry the most useful ISR equipment, then they can stay operationally relevant for decades.”

“Simply put, until we know what sensors or other payloads the Chinese will put into the aircraft, we cannot really determine how useful it will be.”

Other key details remain uncertain, including the aircraft’s inlet configuration, engine count, sensor fit and whether more than one airframe has been built.

Bosack said the drone’s ability to contribute to real-time operations would depend on whether China could move large amounts of sensor data quickly and securely across the battlespace.

Without that, he said, the aircraft could be more useful for collecting intelligence for later analysis than directly supporting fast-moving combat operations.

He also considered a strike role unlikely, arguing that weapons would detract from its more useful reconnaissance mission.

Tate Nurkin, founder of OTH Intelligence Group, also sees the WZ-X mainly as an intelligence and communications platform rather than a strike aircraft, pointing to its potential for wide-area maritime surveillance, target identification and tracking, as well as data relay.

The WZ-X could fit into a broader People’s Liberation Army effort to expand and integrate specialised uncrewed reconnaissance systems. Last month, Chinese state media showed the new GJ-3A armed reconnaissance drone operating alongside WZ-10 and WZ-7 systems, with the three drone types described as providing intelligence support across the PLA.

“Everything points towards networking, joint and inter-service interoperability,” Rupprecht said, adding that the WZ-X could initially complement and eventually replace the WZ-7 and WZ-10 while performing similar missions in more demanding environments.

Open-source research has linked the Malan facility in far western China to the PLA Air Force’s 178th UAV Brigade and identified it as a major testing and training facility for unmanned aerial vehicles.

Rupprecht described Malan as the Chinese air force’s principal drone test centre, saying the WZ-X’s presence there clearly showed the programme’s importance.

Western China, which falls within the PLA’s Western Theatre Command and includes the frontier with India, could serve both as a testing area and as an early environment for evaluating the drone in scenarios involving India, according to Rupprecht.

He said operations around Taiwan were unlikely to be a priority for the WZ-X, given the island’s proximity to mainland China.

“For Taiwan you don’t need such a range and endurance – it’s almost on the doorstep,” he said.

Given the WZ-X’s likely reach, Rupprecht said the South China Sea, waters around Japan and the Korean peninsula, and potentially the eastern Pacific were other possible operational theatres for the drone.

Nurkin placed greater emphasis on the western Pacific, arguing that the aircraft appeared to be tailored for the “longer ranges, better air defences, [and] more complex maritime environment” of a broader regional conflict. He noted that while Taiwan itself was nearby, a crisis over the island could expand “north to Japan, east to Guam, and south to the Philippine Sea and South China Sea”.

In such a contested environment, a stealthy uncrewed aircraft capable of long-range operations could bolster China’s existing surveillance network.

For now, the WZ-X is known mainly through imagery and analyst assessments rather than official information. Its significance may depend less on size than on the sensors and communications systems it ultimately carries.
