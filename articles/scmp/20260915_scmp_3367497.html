Japanese delegations flock to China, but thaw hinges on Takaichi mending ties

SCMP | FREE | 1077 words | 2026-09-15

With bilateral ties in a deep freeze since late last year, a flurry of planned China visits by Japanese lawmakers and business leaders has been interpreted as an attempt to break the ice ahead of November’s Apec summit in China.

However, analysts warned that these efforts to carve out a diplomatic opening with Beijing might not achieve much unless Tokyo sends a clearer signal to mend ties from the very top.

Relations reached their lowest point in a decade following Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi’s remarks last November that a Taiwan contingency could constitute a “survival-threatening situation” that could allow Tokyo to exercise collective self-defence.

An eight-member cross-party delegation of junior lawmakers led by the Liberal Democratic Party’s Masahiro Takamura is visiting Beijing this week.

Meanwhile, Keidanren – Japan’s largest business lobby – and the Osaka-based Kansai Economic Federation are also planning China trips in the coming months.

Later this month, former Japanese foreign minister Takeshi Iwaya will lead a high-profile delegation from the Japan Association for the Promotion of International Trade, which is seeking to meet Chinese President Xi Jinping.

These efforts are not going to lead to much unless there is a clear message from the very top – that is, the prime minister or her cabinet – to make proactive efforts to mend ties

Rintaro Nishimura, senior associate at The Asia Group

Rintaro Nishimura, a senior associate at The Asia Group strategic advisory firm, noted that Beijing probably viewed progress as requiring clearer signals from Japan’s top leadership.

“These efforts are not going to lead to much unless there is a clear message from the very top – that is, the prime minister or her cabinet – to make proactive efforts to mend ties,” he said.

“Diet members and business delegations seem to be trying to create that opening, but Beijing is also drawing a clear line between the Japanese government and other actors.”

“These successive visits are largely intended to lay the groundwork and break the ice for a potential meeting between Takaichi and Xi in November,” said Wang Guangtao, deputy director of the Centre for Japanese Studies at Fudan University.

Takaichi is expected to attend the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation gathering in Shenzhen in mid-November, but whether she will secure a meeting with Xi remains uncertain.

Wang said he was sceptical about the effectiveness of the business and lawmaker visits given the depth of deterioration in China-Japan relations and the gravity of what he called Takaichi’s “erroneous remarks” on Taiwan.

Beijing sees Taiwan as part of China to be reunited by force if necessary. Most countries, including the US and Japan, do not recognise Taiwan as an independent state, but Washington is opposed to any attempt to take the self-governed island by force and is committed to supplying it with weapons.

Wang added that it was unclear whether these delegations had received a mandate from Takaichi or her cabinet, or whether the prime minister even wanted them to visit China now.

The fragile limits of parliamentary diplomacy were exposed in late August when a group of Japanese lawmakers met Lu Kang, vice-minister of the Communist Party’s International Department.

According to a statement from the department, Lu told the delegation that history and Taiwan were at the centre of tensions between the two nations, and Tokyo needed to take “concrete actions” to improve ties.

Lu added that he hoped Japanese politicians could urge their leadership to learn from history and handle the issue “honestly and faithfully in accordance with the one-China principle”.

Toru Horiuchi, a China-Japan relations expert at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, noted that while these visits helped keep communication channels open, Japanese lawmakers “have not been able to meet with anybody in the Chinese leadership, and business delegations so far have been rather small”.

He suggested watching who from the Chinese side would meet the delegation led by Iwaya – who is viewed as more China-friendly – as a potential sign of Beijing’s willingness to accept greater political contact with Japan.

“China still demands that Takaichi retract her remark on Taiwan and continues to put pressure on Japan,” Horiuchi said. “So as long as Japan does not compromise in some way, a major breakthrough is unlikely.”

During the 2014 Apec summit in Beijing, Shinzo Abe, Japanese prime minister at the time, had an ice-breaking meeting with Xi following a two-year freeze triggered by territorial disputes and Abe’s visit to the Yasukuni Shrine.

Before that meeting, former prime minister Yasuo Fukuda went to China on a “secret mission” to ease a rapprochement and deliver Abe’s request for dialogue.

But Wang said a similar breakthrough was out of reach today because the current rift was centred on Taiwan – a “red line” on which Beijing allows no compromise.

“Both sides lack intermediaries with sufficient political capital and a foundation of mutual trust,” he added.

According to observers, a deeper structural problem compounds the difficulties facing Beijing-Tokyo ties: a generation gap in Japan’s vital conduits to China.

Older politicians such as Yohei Kono – who died in June – and Toshihiro Nikai previously wielded considerable influence within the Liberal Democratic Party and maintained long-standing trust with Chinese counterparts.

The LDP’s former junior coalition partner Komeito, which positions itself as a party of peace and acted as a bridge between China and Japan, is no longer in government.

“The major problem we’re facing now is that as this cohort of older-generation ‘China-knowledgeable’ politicians collectively exits the political stage, the younger generation has not done a good job in maintaining continuity in communication channels,” said Wang of Fudan University, describing it as an “alarming gap”.

Nishimura noted that Japanese politicians today faced mounting domestic pressure that made China engagement politically perilous.

“With sentiment towards China souring in Japan, it is becoming increasingly challenging for lawmakers to visit China or engage with Chinese stakeholders without being seen as ‘too cosy’ with Beijing or under Beijing’s influence.”

He said this perception carried huge political costs, potentially damaging their standing within the LDP and alienating voters.

“The recent acceptance of business and Diet member delegations suggests that Beijing is keen to gauge how Tokyo is thinking about China engagement and to encourage these delegations to communicate back to Tokyo that it needs to make efforts to re-engage Beijing, not the other way around,” he said.
