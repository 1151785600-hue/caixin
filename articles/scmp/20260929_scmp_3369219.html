India-Europe trade corridor gets Trump’s backing, but can IMEC survive war?

SCMP | FREE | 1087 words | 2026-09-29

US President Donald Trump’s recent endorsement of a trade corridor connecting India to Europe via the Middle East has renewed focus on the three-year-old project, but analysts warn it must expand to withstand ongoing geopolitical conflicts.

The India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor (IMEC) was first unveiled at the G20 summit in 2023 as an alternative to existing routes and China’s Belt and Road Initiative.

Backed by India, the United States, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Israel and the European Union, the project envisages combining shipping lanes, railways, energy pipelines and data cables into one corridor that is still largely a work in progress.

Though some existing infrastructure can handle parts of the proposed route, key rail links, ports and other connections are still being developed.

Trump tied his support for IMEC to efforts to move Middle Eastern energy infrastructure away from vulnerable maritime routes amid the ongoing conflict with Iran.

“Iran’s terrorist attacks on commercial shipping in neighbouring countries have shown the need to shift Middle East energy infrastructure away from the Iranian checkpoints,” Trump said on September 22 at a meeting with Gulf leaders on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly.

“That’s why my administration strongly supports the India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor and other places of getting oil out, whether it’s pipelines or anything else,” he said, adding that “together we are on the verge of overcoming challenges that have plagued this region for a decade”.

The corridor represents a rare area of continuity between the second Trump administration and Joe Biden’s presidency, despite Trump’s backing having been erratic until recently, according to Alberto Rizzi, a policy fellow at the European Council on Foreign Relations.

Trump’s recent endorsement helped build political momentum for the project, Rizzi said, even if “it is currently difficult to imagine that this political approval will translate any time soon into financial support from the US”.

He said conflict in the region had also exposed a fundamental weakness in IMEC: how resilient can a trade corridor be if its key gateways rely on the very chokepoints it was meant to bypass?

The near-total closure of the Strait of Hormuz amid the Iran war was already changing the scope of the project, Rizzi said, creating an “IMEC on wheels” in which trucks crossed the Arabian deserts to transport goods to the Mediterranean.

Reliance on Omani and Emirati ports outside the strait had also grown, though this remapping had largely been spontaneous and not necessarily coordinated or interoperable, he said.

Rizzi said the crisis had added weight to the argument for turning IMEC into a network with built-in redundancies. “A single route can be disrupted easily; a network of lanes instead can deal with disruptions much better,” he said.

This weakness is clearest on IMEC’s eastern maritime leg, which connects Indian ports to UAE ports such as Jebel Ali and Khalifa before cargo travels overland through the UAE, Saudi Arabia and Jordan towards Israel and Europe.

Because UAE ports sit inside the Persian Gulf, ships arriving from India must pass through the Strait of Hormuz. At the other end, the corridor’s western gateway to the Mediterranean at Haifa in Israel is directly exposed to regional conflict.

Shanthie Mariet D’Souza, founder and president of the Mantraya Institute for Strategic Studies in Goa, said the Iran conflict had laid these weaknesses bare.

“The corridor’s design depends on ships carrying goods from India across the Arabian Sea to the UAE, and that route runs through waters where the Strait of Hormuz and the approaches to it are hostage to any escalation involving Iran,” D’Souza said.

“IMEC was conceived as a reliable alternative to the Suez route. But under the present circumstances, it can only become a corridor that swaps one chokepoint risk for another.”

The Gaza war, which erupted just weeks after IMEC’s launch, had already delayed the project amid regional tensions and logistical challenges. The Iran conflict has only added to the uncertainty.

Afaq Hussain, a non-resident senior fellow with the Project for Middle East Integration at the Atlantic Council, agreed with Rizzi that the IMEC’s geographic footprint needed to be diversified.

“It shouldn’t be a single-line corridor,” Hussain said. “Making it a network of corridors [will] be more secure and diversify risk.”

That could mean integrating ports such as Salalah in Oman, as well as routes through Egypt or Syria via Jordan, while addressing vulnerabilities at other regional chokepoints such as the Bab al-Mandeb Strait in the Red Sea, he said.

Samriddhi Vij, an associate fellow in the Middle East programme at the Observer Research Foundation in New Delhi, called the problem IMEC’s “paradox of resilience”, adding that the crisis had validated the case for diversification while making the corridor harder to operationalise.

“Its eastern maritime architecture relies on Gulf ports inside or adjacent to Hormuz, while its western gateway around Haifa remains exposed to regional conflict,” Vij said.

“Higher war-risk insurance and geopolitical uncertainty raise the corridor’s cost of capital. The crisis has therefore made IMEC simultaneously more strategically necessary and commercially harder to implement.”

One response would be to build alternative gateways. Fujairah, on the UAE’s eastern coast outside the Strait of Hormuz, is one option. On September 20 – two days before Trump’s remarks – Etihad Rail and AD Ports launched a direct freight service connecting the Port of Fujairah with the Industrial City of Abu Dhabi.

Cargo arriving at Fujairah could now travel inland via the UAE’s national rail network, turning an outside-Hormuz gateway into an increasingly practical logistics route, said foreign affairs analyst Robinder Sachdev.

“Oman provides a second, potentially more structural option,” Sachdev said, noting that a 238km (148-mile) rail project was being built to connect the Omani city of Sohar to the UAE.

But moving the IMEC’s gateways “cannot by itself solve the geography of modern warfare”, he said. “I would call it less a redrawing of IMEC than a redesign of IMEC.”

The different components of IMEC need not follow the same geography, Sachdev said: freight could follow one alignment, while energy pipelines, electricity grids and digital cables follow others.

“The crisis has not weakened the logic of IMEC – it has weakened the logic of a single-gateway IMEC,” he added. “The next stage should transform IMEC from a corridor into a network, because a line can be broken at one point; a network can reroute.”
