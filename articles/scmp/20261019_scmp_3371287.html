Hong Kong’s Apec meetings give Beijing a venue to pitch the virtues of its system

SCMP | FREE | 1070 words | 2026-10-19

As Hong Kong hosts the Apec finance ministers’ meeting on Tuesday, this two-part series examines the summit’s significance and its implications for Beijing on the global stage. In part two, Frank Chen explores how the event could help Beijing pitch its philosophies and urge cooperation in digital infrastructure financing.

China’s hosting of this year’s Apec meetings, including a finance ministers’ gathering in Hong Kong, will see a more confident Beijing seek to pitch its economic and financial governance philosophies to other countries, analysts say.

That desire – and ambition – to showcase the feats and experience of the world’s second-largest economy to member nations of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum was bolstered by perceived “inadequacies” in the ability of Western doctrines to address challenges, they added.

“China has evolved from a participant in global governance to a real architect … Beijing knows the delegates will spend three days in Hong Kong, and a month later leaders will be in Shenzhen. The message is in the setting: come see how this works, come see what we’ve built,” said Zak Dychtwald, founder and CEO of Young China Group, a Shanghai-founded market advisory consultancy.

About 500 guests, including some finance ministers from Apec’s 21 economies, are set to congregate in Hong Kong from Monday to Wednesday. Finance Minister Lan Foan will deliver a keynote address and China will also present a policy statement on enhancing cooperation in digital infrastructure financing across the Asia-Pacific region.

“When some Western governance principles are unravelling and becoming inadequate to resolve economic and financial challenges and as America retreats from its leadership role, China is proposing its visions as a solution to the complexities,” said Wang Yong, a professor at Peking University’s school of international studies. “The hope is that Chinese initiatives can become Apec consensus.”

Dychtwald said China would be deft in peddling its vision.

“Beijing will look to create economic dependencies and continue positioning itself as a reliable partner, which has been a successful policy amid continued unpredictability from America and Europe,” he said.

One indication of China’s effort to lead this year’s discussion and how well Chinese proposals are heeded and adopted will be the finance ministers’ joint statement, the most significant outcome of the gathering.

“Governments in this region are pragmatic. They’ll take the parts of China’s offer that help them and, within the constraints of maintaining a positive relationship, ignore the parts that don’t,” Dychtwald said.

Beijing has set four key themes for talks between financial officials: fiscal policies supporting livelihoods, financing for digital infrastructure, inclusive finance tailored to local conditions and exchange and cooperation among young finance professionals.

China’s finance ministry last week said these topics would address “both immediate priorities and longer-term considerations”.

“Economies across the region now face common tasks of stabilising growth, fighting risks and safeguarding livelihoods,” said a ministry spokesperson, referring to the changes brought about by artificial intelligence. “There is an urgent need to strengthen communication and coordination on macroeconomic policies and to adopt appropriate measures to support innovation.”

The spokesperson called the finance ministers’ meeting one of the most important ministerial exchanges in the Apec grouping – demonstrating the significance Beijing attaches to the Hong Kong event – as it will help “build financial-sector deliverables” for the leaders’ summit next month.

Beijing also aimed to continue to champion values of a multipolar world.

“China stands ready to work with all Apec economies to uphold multilateralism [and] safeguard stability and industrial supply chains for the region’s long-term, sustainable growth,” said the spokesperson.

The conversation that matters most this fall is the one between Washington and Beijing

Zak Dychtwald, Young China Group

In a sign of Beijing’s efforts to back multilateralism and the role of international agencies, representatives from the International Monetary Fund (IMF), Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank and Asian Development Bank would present their global and regional economic and financial outlooks to Apec financial policymakers, according to the meeting agendas.

On Saturday, China’s central bank governor Pan Gongsheng also said in Bangkok that Beijing supported the IMF in boosting the surveillance of fiscal risks in “major advanced economies” and their spillover effects.

Meanwhile, the likely absence of the US’ top finance official might make discussions smoother and give Beijing more room to boost its influence, according to Peking University’s Wang.

The South China Morning Post has reported that US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent may skip Hong Kong to focus on preparations for US President Donald Trump’s visit to Shenzhen and his talk with President Xi Jinping at next month’s Apec leaders’ summit. The two countries aim to secure a more durable trade truce, which is due to expire in January.

Dychtwald said the outlook for a third Trump-Xi summit this year might dominate discussions in Hong Kong.

“Everyone at the finance ministers’ meeting understands that the conversation that matters most this fall is the one between Washington and Beijing, and that one is happening just across the border,” he said.

Dychtwald added that Bessent’s absence could be a reminder that only one superpower was a regional partner.

That said, China’s recent slowdown in gross domestic product growth, with a 4.3 per cent year-on-year rate reported in the second quarter – lower than Beijing’s full-year target of 4.5 to 5 per cent – may draw attention to some issues afflicting the Chinese economy even as Beijing touts the outlook and applicability of its approach.

“China as the host may still face different voices from other countries on certain issues concerning China, like market access, subsidies, transparency, industrial policy, exports and debt, as well as insufficient consumption,” Wang said.

He said the country’s bulging trade surplus, with outbound shipments rising 36.3 per cent year on year to US$447.18 billion last month, might also spark debate at the gathering.

Dychtwald said other countries might seek to use Apec to gain some leverage over China.

“The idea that Beijing wants continued access to export markets, and will work to protect that access, isn’t novel,” he said. “Everyone at Apec knows it. What’s interesting is whether it becomes a bargaining chip for other participants.

“China’s export reliance isn’t going away due to the softness in domestic consumption. I’d expect the smart governments at Apec to use that leverage in the way they work with Beijing.”
