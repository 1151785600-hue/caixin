South Korea hits back at Trump’s investment claims in ‘tit-for-tat move’

SCMP | FREE | 1092 words | 2026-10-03

US President Donald Trump’s unilateral announcement of a “colossal” South Korean investment package has prompted Seoul to clarify that the deal is far from finalised, laying bare stark differences between the allies over the terms of their trade agreement, observers have said.

On Wednesday, Trump announced that South Korea would invest in the construction of a natural gas-fired power plant in Texas, eight large-scale nuclear power plants across the United States and a liquefied natural gas (LNG) project in Alaska.

Hours later, South Korean President Lee Jae Myung issued a clarification that effectively pushed back against Washington’s portrayal of the investment package as a done deal, making clear that further negotiations were still necessary.

Trump’s announcement comes just weeks before the highly competitive November midterm elections, apparently aiming to bolster the Republican Party’s economic record and support vulnerable candidates, including Alaska Senator Dan Sullivan.

It followed protracted negotiations over the implementation of Seoul’s commitment to invest US$350 billion in the US – US$150 billion in the shipbuilding sector and US$200 billion in strategic industries – in return for Washington lowering tariffs on South Korean goods.

Trump touted the three projects as a “colossal package” that marked a “major step towards securing our critical energy supply chain, ensuring American energy dominance and making our partnership with South Korea and the entire Pacific region stronger than ever before”.

He said Seoul would invest up to US$200 billion in US energy infrastructure, including US$22.3 billion for a 6-gigawatt power generation facility in Texas and US$120 billion for the eight nuclear power plants.

“And at long last, the Alaska liquefied natural gas pipeline. That’s a big one,” he added.

US Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick also said the investment in the Alaska LNG project would exceed US$50 billion, and the eight nuclear reactors would be built in Ohio, Tennessee, South Carolina and Kentucky.

Seoul’s position is that only the Texas project has been finalised, while the commercially contentious Alaska LNG project and the eight nuclear power plants remain subject to further discussions.

On Thursday, Lee reiterated that “commercial viability” must be a prerequisite for South Korean investment in the US.

“Among the investment projects in the United States announced by the US, we have agreed to begin ‘working’ on the Alaska LNG project on the condition that its commercial viability is confirmed and that it complies with South Korea’s legal procedures,” he said on social media.

Lee’s emphasis on “working” appeared intended to underscore that both sides had yet to bridge their differences over the project’s commercial feasibility and the conditions for Seoul’s participation.

A joint statement issued by the two governments appeared to support Lee’s interpretation concerning where Seoul’s investment would be made.

The statement said both sides had “agreed to commence working” on the Alaska LNG project, subject to “meeting commercial reasonableness and all applicable domestic legal requirements”.

South Korea’s Industry Minister Kim Jung-kwan said last week that the Alaska project remained under review. “We will review the Alaska LNG project under the clear principle that we will proceed only if its commercial viability is established,” Kim said.

The joint statement also said both sides “have agreed to a comprehensive cooperation framework” for constructing eight large-scale nuclear power plants.

Lee has stressed that the commercial viability of each nuclear power plant must be assessed individually, apparently contrasting with Washington’s position that the investment should be evaluated as an overall package.

His clarification comes despite reports that both countries agreed on the operating arrangements for a US$200 billion umbrella investment vehicle to govern the investment component of Seoul’s commitment.

Under the reported mechanism, revenues from individual projects would be consolidated, allowing profits from commercially successful ventures to offset losses incurred by others.

Both sides would initially share profits equally, with a 50-50 split maintained until South Korea recovered its principal investment and interest from each project. Only then would the profit-sharing ratio shift to 10 per cent for Seoul and 90 per cent for Washington.

Even after the recovery of principal and interest, however, the 50-50 arrangement would continue across the entire portfolio, including projects whose individual investments had already been recouped, Lee said.

He added that the arrangement was designed to minimise investment losses and ensure South Korea recovered its overall investment.

Observers say the arrangement suggests that both countries still need to resolve important details concerning the investment mechanism, including how commercial risks and returns will be assessed and distributed across the portfolio.

“This is a tit-for-tat move by President Lee in response to Washington’s unilateral action,” Hur Jung, a professor in the economics department at Sogang University, told This Week in Asia.

Trump’s announcement was designed to appeal to voters ahead of the midterm elections, while Lee, facing declining approval ratings and domestic criticism over his handling of negotiations with Washington, also needed to send a strong message to South Korean audiences, Hur said.

“President Lee’s remarks, however, are throwing up new obstacles in negotiations with Washington. He is doubling down on his stance as part of his negotiation tactics, but there is little possibility of the talks breaking down,” Hur said, citing the strategic importance of the two allies to each other.

Kim Jae-chun, a political-science professor at Sogang University, said Lee was “staking out his firm position” to secure South Korean interests in the negotiations, but warned that “it would be very difficult for Seoul to withstand Washington’s pressure” given that Trump’s term runs until January 2029.

The conservative opposition People Power Party (PPP), meanwhile, accused the government of deceiving the public, arguing that it had already agreed on specific investment amounts with Washington but concealed the details from the National Assembly.

It described Trump’s announcement as a “serious diplomatic disaster” for South Korea.

The PPP urged the government to disclose whether it had agreed to invest in the Alaska LNG project, release detailed plans and the criteria for determining commercial viability and seek parliamentary approval for the investment.

Lee and Trump reached the trade deal last year, under which Washington agreed to lower its so-called reciprocal tariffs on South Korean goods to 15 per cent from 25 per cent in return for Seoul’s US$350 billion investment commitment.

Both sides have since engaged in difficult negotiations over the selection and implementation of investment projects, with Seoul seeking to ensure its commitments are commercially viable and do not expose South Korean taxpayers and public finances to excessive risks.
