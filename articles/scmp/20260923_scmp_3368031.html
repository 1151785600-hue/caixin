South Korea wants free ‘AI for All’. Is society ready?

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2628 words | 2026-09-23

In Silicon Valley and beyond, the architects of artificial intelligence are increasingly warning about the risks it poses to humanity.

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei wants the industry to slow down. A former engineer at his company, who also worked at its rival OpenAI, told reporters after quitting earlier this month that he was “genuinely frightened” unchecked AI development might mean “we could all die in the immediate future”.

Even Geoffrey Hinton, the Nobel Prize-winning computer scientist known as the “godfather of AI”, has warned of the existential threat posed by the technology should it escape human control.

Concerns about AI run the gamut from autonomous weapons, mass unemployment and machine-driven cyberattacks to deepfake-fuelled misinformation, algorithmic bias and a dangerous overreliance on automation.

South Korea has seen all this – and decided now is the time to hit the accelerator.

Under President Lee Jae Myung’s “AI for All” initiative, the government is preparing to hand every citizen free access to artificial intelligence, integrating it into hospitals, schools, government offices and daily lives in a way few countries have attempted.

“The idea is to substantially lower barriers to access so that the benefits of the technology are distributed evenly rather than enjoyed by only some people,” Seoul’s Ministry of Science and ICT said in a statement to This Week in Asia.

The government, which has a 10 trillion won (US$7.3 billion) AI budget for the current financial year, has allocated an additional 250 billion won for the roll-out through 2027.

A beta version is set to launch later this month, introducing free, unlimited-access general-purpose chatbots and AI agents developed by three consortiums – led by telecoms giants SK Telecom, KT and Kakao – using hundreds of state-of-the-art Nvidia B200 graphics processing units lent to them by the government.

From next year, officials envisage the temporary trial will become a permanent, taxpayer-funded public utility, eventually connecting to government systems to allow citizens to book doctor’s appointments, go house hunting, get tax advice and the like, all through AI intermediaries.

To manage the risks involved, Seoul is relying on its landmark AI Basic Act, which came into force in January, to ensure human-in-the-loop oversight, mandatory impact assessments and clear watermarking of synthetic content.

Meanwhile, a state-funded AI Safety Institute has been tasked with serving as a technical watchdog to evaluate the pitfalls and ensure that public AI utilities remain trustworthy, secure and answerable to the citizens who use them.

“Going forward, the government plans to establish an autonomous reliability-assurance system”, the ICT ministry said in its statement, adding this would be based on the seven “AI Ethics Principles” it laid out last month: accountability, human-centredness, privacy protection and fairness among them.

South Korea hopes its “AI for All” push will help it become one of the world’s three leading AI powers, alongside the United States and China, with the government seeing the technology as an antidote to a shrinking workforce and as a way to strengthen its export-driven economy.

But the same society racing to put AI in every pocket is also confronting the consequences of what that means, as workers worry about automation eliminating jobs, police struggle to combat a surge in deepfake crimes and educators watch a generation’s reading scores slide.

Sovereignty is another concern. Of the roughly two-thirds of South Korea’s 51.6 million people who have tried or regularly use AI, 23.45 million are active users of OpenAI’s ChatGPT, 8.45 million of Google’s Gemini and 2.41 million of Anthropic’s Claude, according to government figures.

Yet access to these platforms cannot be guaranteed, as the ministry noted in its statement.

“As we saw recently, foreign AI services can have their availability limited at any time depending on decisions made by foreign governments or companies,” it said, in a possible nod to Washington’s recent attempts to block access to the newest models.

In July, Science and ICT Minister Bae Kyung-hoon unveiled plans for South Korea to develop its own cybersecurity-focused AI model by the end of this year, following the US clampdown.

Brewing with bots

At Unicup Coffee, an independent cafe vying for business against 11 low-cost chain rivals in Busan International Finance Centre, owner Bae Kyung-han, 31, credits AI with helping him beat the competition.

What began a year ago as a cost-saving experiment, with Bae teaching himself how to use generative AI to create promotional posters and menu images, has since taken over nearly every part of the business.

“It’s now running my research, proposal developments, communications and advertisements,” he said, adding that the AI system – his only office-based “colleague” – had also orchestrated a shift in focus towards bulk orders instead of walk-in customers.

Five employees handle customer orders, serving some 13,000 people a month and generating card sales that are, according to Bae, roughly double those of his competitors.

He has also built a second business around the technology, registering as a vendor under one of the government’s chosen AI consortiums to teach novices how to ask better questions of their chatbots.

“The biggest advantage of AI in the workplace is that it reduces the psychological burden of managing people,” Bae said, describing the relief of no longer having to coordinate schedules, repeat instructions or manage employees’ feelings.

“With AI, I can ask questions whenever I need to and revise plans whenever I want to. It’s just more efficient.”

Yet he is still sceptical of the government’s AI strategy, questioning whether domestically developed models – even ones that are provided for free – would actually be able to wean people off premium foreign made tools.

“People who are used to using AI would likely pay for a more expensive US-based model like ChatGPT rather than use a free domestic service,” Bae said.

That is, unless the models were specialised and purpose-built for specific problems, as opposed to another general-purpose chatbot.

“That changes the conversation,” Bae said. “That could do wonders for businesses like mine.”

Elsewhere, at a jobs fair in Seoul’s Yonsei University, technology companies crowded the floor, competing for the attention of soon-to-be graduates before they had even finished their degrees.

Kwon Il-geun, an electronic engineering student hoping to work at semiconductor giants Samsung Electronics or SK Hynix, said AI proficiency had never been more important.

“My skills in using AI tools have nearly doubled in the past year,” he said, comparing the shift to the first industrial revolution or the arrival of the personal computer. “This is just part of the changing times.”

No juniors needed?

Not everyone is sharing in the spoils, however. The number of young South Koreans aged 15 to 29 who were in work dropped by 285,000 between June 2022 and June this year, according to the central bank, which estimated that 94 per cent of that decline was in sectors highly exposed to automation, including publishing, IT services and computer programming.

And the Korea Development Institute, a government-run think tank, projected in July that AI could wipe out roughly 256,000 jobs a year within the next decade.

This has had a knock-on effect on students’ ambitions, with enrolment reportedly falling at academies teaching graphic design and coding, even as institutions preparing young people for stable public-sector careers enjoy booming interest.

At the SKY Entrance Exam Academy in Seoul, enrolment in its military and policy preparation track is up 70 per cent year on year.

Yun Ji-sang, a recent school leaver, is among those preparing to commit himself to five years of officer training and a military career of 15 years minimum.

“Some people think this is a long time to be trapped inside the military, but I just view it as a long time of having a stable job,” he said, noting that it was also one that came with a pension.

The number of young South Koreans in employment fell by 143,000 in August compared to the year before, the Chosun Daily newspaper reported on September 9, marking the 46th straight month of decline for those aged 15 to 29.

Meanwhile, the number of civil servants being recruited nationwide increased this year for the first time in five years, according to the Ministry of Personnel Management.

“I used to want to live as an entrepreneur who would travel abroad frequently and live freely,” Yun said. “But what use is that when we are constantly worrying about making a living?”

Conventional wisdom surrounding which jobs were most at risk from automation had been flipped on its head by the rise of generative AI, said Ko Hak-soo, a law professor at Seoul National University.

“When ChatGPT first emerged three or four years ago, there was widespread concern that jobs involving workers with relatively low levels of education … would be at highest risk,” he said.

“But now, there is a much stronger view that jobs requiring a great deal of human labour may actually be relatively safe while professional occupations, especially entry-level and junior workers in these fields, might gradually disappear.”

New roles were likely to emerge as AI became more integrated into workplaces, Ko said – while warning there was “little understanding of the reality of the shifting labour market”.

Kim Myuhng-joo, executive director of South Korea’s AI Safety Institute, said the disruption was already under way.

“Until just a few years ago, programmers were extremely highly valued, and their salaries were rising rapidly,” he told This Week in Asia. “But AI companies are increasingly using AI for coding … without needing as many junior-level developers.”

Kim further voiced concerns that a new cohort of workers coming of age in the “AI for All” era might never develop the skills needed to operate without the technology.

“In less than 10 years, we could see a new generation entering the workforce that is overly dependent on AI,” he said.

“Companies may expect these new employees to have the capabilities of previous generations, but they … could end up becoming people who are incapable of doing anything without AI.”

AI-fuelled abuse

South Korea’s most visceral fears surrounding AI stem not from the threat of rogue tech, however, but from the recent reality of criminal misuse.

The Nth Room case of 2020 – in which encrypted social media channels were used to produce, sell and distribute sexually exploitative material and blackmail victims – and subsequent scandals led to the country strengthening its deepfake laws in 2024.

Producing such material can now result in seven years behind bars or a 50 million won (US$37,000) fine, while possessing or knowingly viewing it can mean three years in prison or a 30 million won penalty.

Yet police still recorded 1,553 deepfake-related offences last year, accounting for over 35 per cent of all reported cybersex crimes, according to statistics presented at a recent forum hosted by Amnesty International Korea.

In the first half of this year alone, police apprehended 419 suspects for deepfake offences, 17 of whom were physically detained while awaiting trial.

The National Office of Investigation said in a statement that officials were “developing police response technologies at a pace that exceeds the speed at which technology itself is evolving”, including by expanding forensic video expertise and the use of AI-assisted detection tools.

Won Eun-ji is less convinced that technology alone will solve the problem. As director of Team Flame, which investigates digital sex crimes, she has spent much of this year infiltrating roughly 50 Telegram chat rooms alongside investigative outlet Newstapa, documenting how AI tools have swelled the ranks of anonymous perpetrators and lowered the age of victims.

“Even today, many initial leads in these investigations often come from victims themselves,” she said. “The police need to further specialise and strengthen their capabilities.”

Kim, of the AI Safety Institute, said the government needed to prepare for the unintended consequences of increasing access to AI.

“Once AI becomes embedded in society, it is difficult to remove it,” he said, describing it as “an irreversible change”.

“We need to conduct thorough impact assessments from the beginning, considering what effects the technology might have and whether it could be misused, abused or overused.”

To combat crime, Kim said he would support police undercover investigations “depending on the circumstance” alongside greater use of invisible watermarking to trace AI-generated content back to its creator or means of distribution.

“Online platforms should take more active steps to identify online crimes” and prevent abuse material from being circulated, he said, adding the children “need to receive education on these issues as part of broader civic education and AI literacy”, starting in primary school.

Generation chatbot

Yet in the classroom, an upside-down hierarchy of knowledge has begun to emerge, according to Moon Sung-ho, the 16-year-old editor-in-chief of Tokipul, an independent student-run newspaper.

“In many cases, students actually know more about AI than teachers, resulting in students using AI to complete all of their assignments while teachers don’t really know how to stop them,” he said.

Song Keun-sang was among the educators who protested last year against the government’s short-lived 1.2 trillion-won plan to roll-out AI-powered textbooks.

“Once we actually tried using AI in the classroom, we realised that it wasn’t as useful as we had expected,” said the fifth-grade teacher at a school in South Chungcheong province.

While good teaching pushes students to learn more about the world, AI short-circuits this process, according to Song.

“It simply gives you the answers,” he said. “It doesn’t have much educational value.”

Song had once thought that AI might be useful to help isolated students or those struggling to make friends, but that changed after a child in neighbouring North Chungcheong province died by suicide following conversations with a chatbot.

“I wonder whether it is really beneficial for students to be exposed to chatbots that constantly cater to them and reassure them,” he said. “They are essentially designed to tell you what you want to hear, so you will never be challenged or face disagreements.”

The effects of this may already be starting to be felt. South Korean 15-year-olds topped global reading charts in 2006, as measured by the Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development’s Programme for International Student Assessment.

Yet in 2025 they posted their lowest average reading score since the country first began taking part in the assessments in 2000, the OECD reported earlier this month.

“Other countries are starting to ban social media and other online devices for adolescents, but South Korea is moving in the exact opposite direction,” Song said. “We can already expect serious declines in academic achievement and literacy skills.”

The more pressing concern for him, however, is social rather than academic.

“Cooperation and communication between students ultimately develops the skills they will need to succeed in society later on,” he said, predicting that interacting with AI chatbots might make it “much more difficult to form relationships with other people.”

Moon has seen this happen among his own classmates and friend group, who once went to internet cafes and played football together, while also asking for help with homework assignments.

“Students these days increasingly ask AI chatbots about these questions,” he said. “It feels like it’s become more difficult to talk with people.”
