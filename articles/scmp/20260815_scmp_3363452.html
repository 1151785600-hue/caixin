China waits in wings as Mecca pact signals Gulf shift away from US security umbrella

SCMP | FREE | 1169 words | 2026-08-15

The grand security bargain between the United States and Gulf countries is widely perceived to be fraying, creating what analysts see as an opening for Beijing to expand its presence in the region.

For decades, Saudi Arabia and other Gulf states relied on Washington’s defensive shield, while it in turn relied on them for access, energy and influence.

Riyadh’s decision to sign a mutual defence agreement with Turkey and Pakistan is the clearest sign yet of that shift.

Signed this week in Mecca, the pact declares that an armed attack on one will be treated as an attack on all three, in an echo of Nato’s Article 5.

But the significance of the Mecca pact goes beyond a new regional security arrangement. Three middle powers – a Gulf energy giant, a Nato member and nuclear-armed South Asian state – are taking steps to reduce their reliance on US-dominated security architecture and build their own collective defence framework.

Such independent alliances among middle powers have been rare since the early Cold War, making the pact a potential model for other countries.

It could also complicate matters for countries such as India and prompt them to reassess their own security partnerships.

“For Riyadh, this agreement bolsters the country’s leverage as it both copes with an emboldened Iran and confronts the limits of US deterrence in the region,” Allison Minor from the Atlantic Council think tank wrote in a recent analysis.

She described the pact as “further evidence that Riyadh is abandoning its quest for an Article 5-like security guarantee from Washington.”

Rich Outzen, a former military and civilian adviser at the US State Department who is now with the Atlantic Council, argued that the agreement marked “an inflection point in the emerging multipolar order in the Middle East and South Asia.”

The pact also brings together countries with very different strategic relationships with China.

John Spencer from the Madison Policy Forum, a New York think tank, said Pakistan was “dependent on China with a military heavily equipped by Beijing”.

Highlighting Pakistan’s fragile economy and reliance on international bailouts, he added: “This is the last country a Nato member should be entering into an Article 5-style mutual defence commitment with.”

For China, however, the new arrangement could create an opening.

As Imran Khurshid from the US-based Middle East Forum wrote in an analysis published last month: “China is using Pakistan for weapons promotion as a gateway to enter the region’s defence landscape.

“Moreover, if the potential ‘Islamic Nato’ that many of these countries, including Pakistan and Turkey, discuss takes shape, China might believe it has an augmented market.”

Khurshid added that China had “aggressively advertised its weapon platforms and builds narratives around its defence systems in the region”, including claims about the success of the JF-17 fighter jet and other Chinese weapons during last year’s India-Pakistan conflict.

That military relationship could become more significant as Saudi Arabia deepens its ties with Pakistan.

According to data from the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute, more than 80 per cent of Pakistan’s arms imports between 2021 and 2024 came from China.

Under the Saudi-Pakistan mutual defence pact signed last year, Pakistan sent JF-17 fighter jets and other Chinese-made systems to Saudi Arabia, giving them greater visibility and operational experience.

Khurshid said such deals created lasting partnerships because they “typically involve long-term commitments related to maintenance, spare parts, training and logistical support,” embedding Chinese systems more deeply into the recipient’s military structure.

Ali Chishti, from the New Pakistan Foundation, an Islamabad-based think tank, wrote recently that China was “ecstatic” about the Mecca pact and pointed to Saudi Foreign Minister Prince Faisal bin Farhan’s recent visit to Beijing as evidence of Riyadh “deepening ties beyond the West”.

However, Michael Kugelman of the Atlantic Council, pointed out that the three signatories “have gone to great lengths not to cast this as a Nato-style alliance”, adding that it suggested they would want to “avoid the optics of enthusiastically pooling their resources to purchase Chinese arms”.

“Such transactions would therefore be done quietly and also not at great volume ... Turkey may be able to provide to Pakistan and Saudi Arabia what China could, and at a better price,” he said.

The combination of China’s close relationship with Pakistan and its broader strategic ambitions in the Middle East could therefore give Beijing an indirect route into the Gulf’s defence architecture.

The three-way pact also complicates India’s strategic calculus. New Delhi has moved closer to Washington in recent years, but ties have soured sharply during Donald Trump’s second term.

Meanwhile, the US has moved closer to Pakistan, which is trying to help mediate a deal to end the war with Iran.

Any future Indian military conflict with Pakistan could be framed by Islamabad as an attack that triggers the pact’s collective defence clause – a possibility that may push India and Israel closer together.

India’s former foreign secretary Kanwal Sibal described the situation as “on the face of it, a very negative development for Indian interests”.

The country needed to “explore more options with the United Arab Emirates, Israel, Greece and Cyprus. And Afghanistan”, he added. “We should consult Iran. All this is a fallout from the disastrous US aggression against Iran.”

The Mecca pact followed by months of Iranian missile and drone strikes on Gulf energy infrastructure and sustained Houthi attacks.

Despite the extensive US military presence in the region, American forces were unable to intercept every attack.

The US-Israel war against Iran has also led to the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, a vital route for oil and gas supplies, further reinforcing concerns in Gulf capitals about relying exclusively on Washington for security.

Those concerns have been compounded by reports the war has used up significant stocks of key US weapons.

Around 65 per cent of Patriot air defence interceptors and a substantial share of Terminal High Altitude Area Defence interceptors have reportedly been used up, according to multiple reports citing senior officials, as well as assessments by the Centre for Strategic and International Studies.

Stocks of long-range precision weapons have also been heavily depleted, including virtually all available ATACMS and Precision Strike Missiles, while Tomahawk cruise missile inventories have fallen by nearly half.

Restocking will take years, partly because of global supply chain constraints, and the Pentagon has already been forced to delay weapons deliveries to allies and partners.

For Gulf states, the lesson may be less about abandoning the US than about reducing their dependence on it.

Rebeccah Heinrichs of the Hudson Institute pointed to the close ties between Islamabad and Beijing, saying: “It is not in US interests for the Pakistanis to be aligning closer with Saudi and Turkey and our approach towards Iran should keep that in mind.”

But Kugelman said that the new pact, with its “emphasis on burden sharing and implied objective of deterring Iran, should go down well in Trump’s Washington”.
