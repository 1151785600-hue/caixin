China's commercial health insurers are building their own framework to cover innovative drugs, addressing the gap between government policies that expand access to new medicines and insurers' need to control costs and maintain profitability. On Nov. 10, the Insurance Association of China (IAC) released the industry's first unified recommended drug list for commercial medical insurance, covering 58 drugs and medical devices, along with three sets of model policy clauses.

These documents are reference texts, not mandates. However, insurers filing new products are expected to adopt them or submit a feasibility report explaining any deviations, making them a de facto standard. The model clauses standardize 24 common concepts, including "deductible" and "reasonable and necessary," as well as reimbursement standards, exclusions, and claims procedures.

An industry insider warned that uniform clauses could narrow room for product differentiation and innovation, pointing to auto insurance, which remains highly homogeneous under standardized clauses. The insider noted that medical insurance, involving various medical institutions, drug types, and compensation conditions, needs to preserve room for innovation.

The list responds to a gap between the National Healthcare Security Administration (NHSA) and insurers. Last year, the NHSA published its first innovative drug catalogue for commercial health insurance with 19 drugs, but coverage wasn't mandatory and some drugs pressured insurance operations, so many insurers only included some. Commercial insurance lacks the scale of the government-run basic system, which negotiates prices using its large insured pool; no industrywide drug list existed until this November, despite a 2021 proposal.

To build the list, more than 90 medical and pharmaceutical experts were assembled, and 22 were randomly chosen to conduct onsite reviews based on clinical need, innovativeness, effectiveness, and safety. Concurrently, 54 insurers scored candidates on factors like market demand, claims experience, drug and drugmaker reputations, and cooperation willingness. The IAC combined both sets of scores to create a preliminary list, which was discussed and voted on to finalize.

The list covers common and rare tumors (lung, liver, blood cancers, neuroblastoma) and advanced therapies like CAR-T cell treatment. Nearly 60% of items aren't covered by basic medical insurance. Zhao Heng, founder of Latitude Health, said the list focuses on high-value products with large market demand, high clinical value, and heavy patient burden.

Two lists follow different logics. The NHSA catalogue excludes indications already covered by basic insurance, while the IAC list weighs commercial policyholders' actual needs. Even covered drugs leave patients paying about 30% of costs out of pocket, making commercial coverage attractive. Nine of the 58 items overlap with the NHSA catalogue, but differences matter more; the IAC list covers only drugs bought outside hospitals, as commercial insurance mainly reimburses inpatient costs. Ten of the NHSA's 19 drugs (e.g., Alzheimer's, Gaucher disease) aren't on the IAC list, potentially suited for disease-specific or supplementary products.

Listing a drug solves payment but doesn't guarantee patient access. Hospital pharmacy management, hospital operations, and health authority assessments shape drug use. Zhu Junsheng, an insurance researcher, said if innovative drugs add to hospitals' operational burden and lack reasonable clinical support, even good insurance products may not translate into treatment. He calls for closer coordination among financial regulators, medical insurance authorities, and health authorities to bridge the "last mile" between coverage and patient access.
