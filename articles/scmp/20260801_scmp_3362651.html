Hong Kong has bounced back since the bleak days of the pandemic

SCMP | FREE | 1072 words | 2026-08-01

Absence, it is said, makes the heart grow fonder. This week marks the fourth anniversary of my departure from Hong Kong, a city I came to love during 28 years as a resident. It was not easy to leave. Hong Kong had become my home. But it was, for me and for many others back then, the right time to make the move.

My reasons were complex, although the wearying pandemic restrictions and dramatic legal and political reforms played their part. The city had changed so much.

Returning to my native England, though, was a leap in the dark. Us British expats used to say the UK was a nice place for a holiday but we wouldn’t want to live there.

In the four years since my departure, Hong Kong has bounced back from the bleak pandemic years. The city I left is a distant memory.

The airport, when I left, on August 3, 2022, was deserted and a mask was clamped to my face. The requirement to wear masks in public would not be lifted for months. Travel restrictions had left Hong Kong isolated from the rest of the world.

Now, the tourists have returned, along with talent and investment. Hong Kong is aiming to become a hub of hubs, a centre for pretty much everything from finance to culture, legal services and sport.

There is a breathless schedule of conferences, exhibitions, concerts and sporting contests, as the city establishes itself as a host of “mega-events”. I would have enjoyed attending this week’s football festival.

As the push for status and connectivity continues, Hong Kong is formulating its first five-year plan to align with national strategy and sees itself as key to the rise of the Greater Bay Area.

There has been a good deal of talking-up Hong Kong, in answer to the government’s call to “tell good stories” about the city. But there is no denying it has come a long way since the pandemic. Like many former residents looking on from afar, I have been delighted to see its progress.

My time in Hong Kong spanned a momentous and often turbulent period in the city’s history and I was privileged to cover it for the South China Morning Post.

The pomp and ceremony of the handover from Britain to China in 1997 was followed by landmark constitutional court cases, as the city struggled to get to grips with the novel “one country, two systems” concept.

It was an exciting time. The newly enacted Basic Law provided comprehensive protection for human rights and people were quick to try to assert them, seeking favourable judgments from the city’s independent judiciary.

Politics was dominated by the Basic Law’s requirement that there be gradual and orderly progress towards the “ultimate goal” of universal suffrage, a destination that still seems a long way away.

The months of often-violent anti-government protests in 2019 proved to be a watershed. Beijing’s response, in the form of a sweeping national security law in 2020, along with a new system ensuring only “patriots” would govern, marked the beginning of a new reality for Hong Kong.

As I said in my first column on returning to the UK, I have many wonderful memories of the city but they mostly belong to a different era. Britain beckoned in 2022, just as it did for the thousands of Hong Kong residents who moved there under a new visa scheme for holders of British National (Overseas) passports. But the UK was never going to be a panacea.

A headline in The Times the week before I arrived read: “Britain slides into crisis.” There have been many more crises since then.

I returned to a country led by Boris Johnson. He was quickly replaced by Liz Truss. But she only lasted 45 days. Then it was Rishi Sunak’s turn. Keir Starmer took over at the helm in 2024 after winning a landslide in the general election. Now, he has been ousted and succeeded by Andy Burnham. This dizzying merry-go-round does not help long-term planning, but is a reflection of the British people’s frustration with their politicians and deep-rooted desire for change.

Burnham has made a promising start, putting a little money back into people’s pockets. The popularity of his Labour Party has risen.

The honeymoon period will not last long. Britain has a multitude of seemingly insoluble problems. A slumbering economy, cost of living crisis and struggling public services will not evaporate just because there is a new prime minister.

But for all of the depictions of Britain as “broken”, my experience on returning after so long away has been a positive one. I love the tranquillity of my home in the English countryside and, after so many years in rented Hong Kong flats, it is a joy to have a house and a garden. The cost of living is high – but not as high as for an expat lifestyle in Hong Kong. My interactions with the National Health Service have, so far, been smooth. And – touch wood – crime has not been a problem. But the taxes are punishing and they are likely to rise.

I enjoy the freedom to say whatever I like about the British government and the ability to regularly vote in elections, choosing from a wide range of candidates. I just wish Count Binface was standing in my constituency!

This is just my experience. Others will have different stories to tell. There has been much debate in recent years about whether Hong Kong or Britain is the better place to live. My answer, an obvious one, remains the same as four years ago. One is not superior to the other. They both have much to offer. Preferences depend on personal circumstances.

Just because you leave Hong Kong, it does not mean you stop caring. As the city continues to integrate with mainland China, it should not lose sight of the distinctions which were intended to make it special.

These include a high degree of autonomy, protection of human rights and independent courts trusted to deliver judgments in accordance with the law. Controversial court cases should be welcomed.

The sun might have set on a period in Hong Kong’s history which I lived through and loved. But I continue to wish the city well and hope it will embrace diversity, compassion and tolerance amid the drive to grow and prosper.
