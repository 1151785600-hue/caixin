Openness must not be sacrificed in US science and tech policy on China

SCMP | FREE | 1109 words | 2026-09-10

When President Xi Jinping arrives in Washington for his planned September 24 visit – his first state-level visit to Washington in more than a decade – artificial intelligence is expected to be near the top of the agenda. Both administrations are preparing for their first formal dialogue on AI.

Washington has framed much of that conversation around the need for tighter controls on sensitive technologies. That framing is the problem.

For much of the debate over US science and technology policy towards China, Washington has been stuck on a false choice between engagement (scientific exchange, international students and collaborative research) and security (export controls, research restrictions and visa limits). The real choice is between indiscriminate restriction and disciplined risk management.

The security concerns are genuine. Some advanced technologies have clear military or intelligence applications. Universities must take seriously undisclosed affiliations, conflicts of commitment and improper technology transfer. Federal research security policy already requires extensive disclosure of appointments, foreign support, affiliations and potential conflicts of interest and commitment. Advanced computing, semiconductors, quantum science, biotechnology and AI all raise legitimate national security questions, largely because so much of the work is dual-use[end optional trim.

But acknowledging those risks does not require treating every interaction with China as equally dangerous. The challenge is to protect what truly matters without damaging the openness that has long been one of America’s greatest scientific strengths.

Smart openness starts from a simple premise: technologies are not equally sensitive and not every Chinese institution carries the same risk. Military-relevant quantum sensing is not cooperation in basic mathematics or astrophysics. A researcher who conceals ties to a defence laboratory has little in common with a Chinese graduate student studying theoretical chemistry.

Policymakers should ask not only what a measure prevents China from obtaining, but what it costs the US in lost talent, weaker collaboration, slower innovation and incentives for other countries to reduce their dependence on American technology. That calculation is too often missing.

Policy too often blurs those distinctions. Once national security becomes the primary lens for nearly every scientific interaction with China, agencies and universities turn deeply risk-averse. Restrictions spread, compliance burdens grow and institutions abandon perfectly legal activities because they look politically risky.

The result can be self-defeating: measures meant to protect American technological leadership can erode elements of the research system that produce it. Research Caroline Wagner and I conducted for the Quincy Institute has argued that a more sustainable approach would distinguish genuine security risks from ordinary international scientific collaboration, rather than treat broad categories of China-related activity as inherently suspect.

American pre-eminence in science was built in substantial part on imported knowledge and talent. The National Science Board reports that foreign-born workers accounted for 43 per cent of the US science and engineering workforce at the doctorate level in 2021; among foreign-born science and engineering workers, China is one of the two leading countries of origin.

Chinese students and researchers have made tangible contributions to American science and technology. China has also gained substantially from its access to the US research ecosystem, and concerns about technology transfer are well founded. Policy has to hold both facts at once.

Washington should begin by defining critical technologies more narrowly and updating those definitions regularly. Controls should focus on identifiable capabilities with plausible national security consequences, not on sprawling categories like “AI” or “biotechnology.” A “small yard, high fence” approach works only if the yard stays small.

The government should also build a genuinely tiered framework for international research collaboration. The most sensitive technologies, and institutions closely tied to China’s military and security apparatus, warrant heightened scrutiny or outright prohibition. Dual-use research should proceed under disclosure rules, access controls and institutional review. Fundamental research outside those categories should remain open.

Washington should move away from nationality-based screening as well. Visa and research-access decisions should turn on affiliations, research fields, access to controlled information and disclosure records – not on citizenship or country of birth. A Chinese passport is not evidence of a security threat.

Universities receiving substantial federal funding should maintain serious research-security capabilities, conduct due diligence on sensitive collaborations and enforce disclosure requirements. In return, universities need clearer and more consistent federal rules. Institutions should not be punished after the fact because a government assessment shifted.

Washington also needs a firmer distinction between disclosure violations and espionage. Researchers who hide material foreign affiliations or funding should face consequences. But a disclosure failure is not by itself proof of illicit technology transfer.

The United States should treat talent attraction as a national security asset. Foreign students who earn advanced American degrees in important scientific and engineering fields deserve clearer pathways to stay, subject to appropriate screening. It makes little strategic sense to educate exceptional people, then push them to build their careers elsewhere.

The importance of this talent pool is particularly evident in advanced science and engineering: more than half of doctorate-level computer and mathematical scientists and engineers working in the US were born abroad. In the competition for scientific talent, the ability to attract and retain such researchers is one of America’s most important structural advantages.

Finally, major restrictions should face a competitiveness test. Policymakers should ask not only what a measure prevents China from obtaining, but what it costs the US in lost talent, weaker collaboration, slower innovation and incentives for other countries to reduce their dependence on American technology. That calculation is too often missing.

The competition runs deeper than technology. It is a contest over where the world’s best scientists, engineers and entrepreneurs choose to build their careers, and openness remains a decisive American advantage. Access to China’s talent pool has real strategic value.

The US does not have to choose between security and openness, though it does have to get better at both. A good place to start is by re-energising the US-China Science and Technology Cooperation Agreement, revised and extended for five years in December 2024. The amended agreement already comes close to the model we need: it limits government-to-government cooperation to basic research while adding stronger safeguards concerning national security, intellectual property, transparency, data reciprocity, and researcher safety and security.

Rather than retreat from scientific engagement, Washington should build on it, drawing sharper lines around genuinely sensitive technologies while preserving room for collaboration where the risks are manageable.

The measure of a successful China policy is not how many doors America can close. It is whether the country can protect what needs protecting without sacrificing the openness, international talent and collaboration that have long been among its greatest sources of strength.
