US economist Barry Eichengreen on reality of de-dollarisation and next currency reshuffle

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 3705 words | 2026-08-25

Barry Eichengreen is a leading American economist and one of the world’s foremost experts on international monetary systems. He is a professor of economics and political science at the University of California, Berkeley, where he has taught since 1987. He served as a senior policy adviser at the International Monetary Fund in 1997-98.

Here, he discusses why today’s “de-dollarisation” talk is different from past episodes, how close the world is to a reshuffling of the international monetary system, and what it will really take for the yuan to close the gap.

This interview first appeared in SCMP Plus. For other interviews in the Open Questions series, click here.

Since last year, discussions about “de-dollarisation” have resurfaced with renewed intensity. In your view, how much of this reflects a genuine structural shift? And what, if anything, makes the current moment different from previous episodes of dollar scepticism?

My view is that a genuine structural shift is finally getting under way against the backdrop of some long-standing concerns about the US dollar – for example, concerns about the high level of public debt in the United States and whether the growth of the debt is sustainable or not.

If there are chronic debt problems, will they have negative implications for the stability of the dollar and the value of foreign central banks’ holdings of US Treasury securities? Those concerns are long-standing, and people have been pointing to them when asking whether the era of dollar dominance may be coming to a close – they have been pointing to them for a long time.

What is new and different, however, are two sets of questions that we did not use to ask about the United States and the dollar, but everyone is asking now.

First, the strength of US political institutions. The dollar’s stability and confidence in the greenback rest fundamentally on the rule of law, separation of powers, the independence of the central bank and the control of corruption.

People are beginning to ask whether those institutional arrangements are more fragile than previously assumed. This has begun to undermine the feeling of security among foreign central banks, governments and other investors holding financial portfolios heavy in dollars.

Second, new questions are being asked about US geopolitical alliances. We know from history that central banks, governments and others prefer to hold and use the currencies of their alliance partners, because those partners are viewed as reliable stewards of their reserve holdings. If one is in an alliance with the United States, moreover, holding and using US dollars is a show of good faith in the country.

However, US President Donald Trump has raised questions about the continued commitment of the United States to Nato and to its other security arrangements. That has led the Europeans and others to ask: Do we really want to be so reliant on the US and the dollar in this geopolitically uncertain world, or should we look to alternatives, including relying more on our own currency – the euro, in the European case?

So, I think questions about both US alliances and the strength of US institutions are new and different, and they imply that the prospects for the dollar may be different going forward as well.

It will take years, maybe even decades, for China to close that gap with the United States and the dollar – unless the US shoots itself in the foot

Some argue that the US dollar-centred monetary order that emerged after Bretton Woods may be nearing its end, giving way to a more multipolar system. Do you share that view? And what role do you expect the yuan to play in whatever comes next?

I have long shared that view. In 2011 – prematurely, perhaps – I published a book called Exorbitant Privilege about the rise and fall of the dollar.

My forecast then was that we would be moving gradually towards a more multipolar monetary and financial system, less centred on the dollar – a more multipolar monetary system that better matched our more multipolar global economy. We have moved in that direction as predicted, but more slowly than I expected.

The persistence of dollar dominance is very strong because so many banks, firms and governments use dollars, which creates network effects that lock in the use of the dollar.

So, I’ve been surprised in the last 15 years that we haven’t moved towards that multipolar system more rapidly. But that movement may now accelerate.

I think China’s currency will, over time, come to play a more consequential global role. But the important point to emphasise is that the yuan is starting out very, very far behind the dollar.

Hence it will take years, maybe even decades, for China to close that gap with the United States and the dollar – unless the US shoots itself in the foot and confidence in the dollar is abruptly lost.

We have seen progress in the yuan’s international use, from broader participation in China’s payment systems to growing panda bond issuance. Do you believe China is now in a particularly important window for advancing yuan internationalisation? How would you characterise the recent progress?

The US-Iran war is affording China additional opportunities to increase the use of its currency in global oil and gas trade, and to do more settlements with the energy exporters from the Middle East in its own currency.

And now, those energy exporters are likely happy to take yuan in payment. They can use those yuan to buy the services of Chinese construction companies and Chinese exports of solar panels, batteries, electronics and other advanced technologies.

The war in the [Persian] Gulf has, I think, accelerated the tendency for other central banks and governments to look for alternatives to the dollar, and in this way created a window for China.

Starting from ground zero 15 years ago, China has made impressive progress. But you have to put that in context, where the dollar still accounts for nearly 60 per cent of the foreign exchange reserves of central banks around the world, and the yuan accounts for 2 per cent.

And one sees the same thing along other dimensions, such as turnover in global foreign exchange markets, where the dollar is 10 times as important as the yuan.

I think China has been making rapid progress, for example, in transactions through China’s Cross-border Interbank Payment System (CIPS). Those transactions had been growing at a 30 or 40 per cent annual rate – really fast, starting from basically nothing 10 years ago.

But that growth slowed sharply in 2025. This slowdown suggests that there still are important obstacles to the yuan’s internationalisation. So I would say: impressive progress, but still, the dollar remains in a different league than China’s currency.

China has developed the requisite financial and technological infrastructure. China is, in a way, ahead of the United States in terms of the digital financial revolution. But it has to create confidence among bankers that the markets and platforms on which the yuan can be traded constitute a safe, secure and reliable way to make payments. Creating confidence in a new, novel payments platform takes time. More time will be required.

The US correspondent banking system clears many more transactions per day than CIPS does. And it will take, as I said before, probably decades before that gap is closed.

Looking ahead, what do you see as the next steps for yuan internationalisation? Do you believe Beijing ultimately envisions the yuan on par with the US dollar, or as one of several major currencies in a more multipolar world?

China needs to continue to enhance the liquidity of its financial markets. If people are going to make payments using the yuan, they are going to have to hold balances in the currency, so they have the money ready to make those payments.

The kinds of securities that foreigners can hold in China are limited by regulation and capital controls. The availability of financial derivatives to hedge the risk of exchange rate changes – those markets are limited in China.

So China needs to move slowly but surely to liberalise access to those financial markets, increase the liquidity of its markets, and – where markets barely exist, as in the case of derivatives – to build them out.

I want to be clear here: China should move slowly but surely in this direction. It should not move quickly and abruptly, since doing the latter could create financial risks.

Back in 2015, China began to move rapidly to relax its capital controls. Money flowed out of the country, the stock market crashed and the authorities reversed course. This episode was a reminder that slow but steady wins the race, and that moving abruptly is not a good idea.

Regarding how Beijing envisions the path, the answer is not entirely under Beijing’s control. Whether the dollar remains the leading international currency – and for how long – depends first and foremost on Washington.

What Beijing can do is think about what the best Chinese policy is under different scenarios: under the scenario where the dollar remains sound and stable, and banks, governments, and others around the world feel comfortable in holding and using it; or, if there is a crisis of confidence in the dollar caused by the United States itself, how quickly China can ramp up its alternative, namely, own-currency settlement.

Beijing should be doing scenario planning, rather than having a blueprint for what they think will happen next.

In the event of a crisis of confidence in the US dollar, are widely discussed alternatives such as the euro and the yuan truly ready to step in as viable substitutes?

None of the alternatives, including the euro and the yuan, are ready to substitute for the dollar. What they can do is supplement the dollar on the margin. But they lack the scale as international currencies, and they have shortcomings.

In the case of China, it is the lack of access to domestic financial markets and the limited liquidity of those markets. In the case of Europe, it is the absence of a stock of safe and liquid assets for international investors to hold and use. Consequently, neither the euro nor the yuan can replace or substitute for the dollar any time soon.

While on some dimensions, like the use of currencies as reserves by central banks, the dollar has lost some of its global market share, the euro has gained none of that market share. The yuan has gained only about one-quarter of what the dollar has lost. The other three-quarters have been gained by non-traditional reserve currencies: the South Korean won, the Australian dollar, the Canadian dollar, the Danish krone, the Norwegian krone.

So far, you and I have been talking about the currencies of big economies. Traditionally, there has been an association between the widespread international use of a currency and the size of the issuing economy. Big economies engage in more foreign trade and foreign investment, so it makes sense that their currencies would be the dominant units in international transactions.

But we now live in a digital world where it is easier to buy, sell, hold and trade the Australian dollar, the Swedish krona, and so forth. These non-traditional reserve currencies are the currencies of small, open, well-managed, inflation-targeting countries. They are becoming more attractive in international use. They too lack the scale to replace the dollar, but they can help to supplement it on the margin.

What role do you see Hong Kong playing in the next phase of yuan internationalisation? As Beijing builds up Shanghai’s offshore financial capabilities, do you see the two cities as increasingly competitive?

Hong Kong in the next phase will play the same role that it has played in the previous phase – as a kind of sandbox, or pilot scheme, for yuan use, where Chinese authorities experiment with and observe the effects of different financial innovations.

This will continue to be the case. If you believe having markets and financial derivatives is important for the use of China’s currency, those markets will be developed, in part, in Hong Kong. And if they work smoothly there, they will be brought onshore.

Hong Kong now has a stablecoin ordinance and has issued two stablecoin licences to a couple of big banks. We don’t know whether stablecoins will become important going forward. I, for one, doubt that they will. But Hong Kong can be a place where banks and others can experiment with the payments technology provided by stablecoins.

And if that turns out to be important, it’ll be easier for China to catch up, because Hong Kong will already have provided evidence about how that new payments technology works, how it should be regulated, and so forth.

As for the competition between Hong Kong and Shanghai, economists generally think that competition is good. Competition forces banks, regulators and others to step up their game to avoid losing market share. So that is what Hong Kong will have to do.

Hong Kong has anchored its currency to the US dollar for more than four decades. If the world becomes more multipolar over time, do you believe the arrangement will still make sense? How do you see Hong Kong’s role evolving in a less US dollar-centric world?

The dollar peg has served Hong Kong well. It has had more advantages than disadvantages because the global economy has been dollar-dominated. Hong Kong links directly into that because markets for financial instruments denominated in US dollars are so big. This enables Hong Kong banks to act as global banks, because they have a big global market out there that they can service.

If people grow more sceptical about the US dollar and move away from it, the global system will become less dollar-centric. At that point, the advantages to Hong Kong of being linked to the US dollar obviously become less.

Hong Kong authorities ought to quietly engage in the same kind of scenario planning for different outcomes, depending on what kind of policies – stable or unstable – are followed in the US, so they can respond appropriately. But I would emphasise that they should do their scenario planning quietly.

As stablecoins and central bank digital currencies gain traction around the world, do you see them as game-changers that could disrupt the current monetary hierarchy, or will they simply reinforce the existing status quo?

If you look at the long sweep of history, you see that financial and payments technology is always changing. We moved from doing transactions using lumps of gold and silver, to tokens and coins, to bank money and bills of exchange, and so forth.

We are in another one of those periods where financial and payments technology is changing rapidly. An important new technology is blockchain, or distributed ledger technology. I am convinced that distributed ledger technology will be used to make cross-border payments going forward.

The question is: what kind of units run on those new payment rails? Will they be stablecoins on the one hand, or central bank digital currencies and tokenised commercial bank deposits on the other hand? And depending on the answer to that question, in what currency will they be denominated? In US dollars, or something else?

The US has taken a bet on stablecoins. If privately issued stablecoins become the dominant unit that runs on those payment rails, it is likely to cement the international role of the dollar, because 99 per cent of stablecoins are linked to the US dollar. And again, the US has a big head start in going down that road.

On the other hand, both the euro area and China are looking at central bank digital currencies and tokenised commercial bank deposits. If that turns out to be what runs on these new payment rails, it won’t be the dollar but the euro and the yuan that dominate in the future.

So you have to ask, will we be relying more on private digital money, which is what the US is aiming for, or publicly issued and regulated digital money, which is what China and Europe are developing?

The arrow of history points towards public provision of the public good of stable money. In the past, we tended to have lots of private issuers of banknotes and coins. Gradually, governments have taken over that function as a public utility. In effect, they have monopolised it. So if I have to bet on one outcome or another, I would bet on central bank digital currencies and tokenised bank deposits.

Just as a diverse ecosystem is good for planet Earth, a multipolar monetary system would be good for the global economy

Gold has outlasted every major currency in history. As central banks buy gold at the fastest pace in decades, are we witnessing a return of the world’s oldest monetary asset? What role do you see it playing in the future international monetary system?

I do not think gold will play a big role in the future. Central banks that inherited very dollar-heavy reserve portfolios in the past want to diversify. One direction they can diversify towards is by using some of their dollars to buy gold. We’ve seen a little bit of that on the margin.

If you are a country like Russia that is subject to sanctions, then buying gold is a way of protecting your reserves from being frozen by foreign authorities. You can buy gold, bring it home and vault it in St Petersburg or Moscow. We’ve seen examples of that kind of behaviour in the last decade and a half.

But it is important to note, number one: all the gold purchases that we’ve seen in the last 15 years have been by emerging markets that did not inherit a lot of gold from the past. Advanced countries that inherited gold from the Bretton Woods period and the earlier gold standard period have not added to their reserves.

And number two, it is important to note that most of the increase in the share of gold in central bank reserves has been due to the run-up in the price, and not active purchases.

We may see a little bit more of that behaviour, because every global investor wants a commodity play in their portfolio. Holding a little bit of gold is a way for central banks to acquire that exposure and a bit of additional diversification.

Many speak of a “multipolar” monetary system. In practice, how would such a system function? Would it inevitably require greater coordination among major economies, and is meaningful monetary cooperation still possible at a time of growing geopolitical fragmentation?

In practice, a multipolar system would be one in which several currencies play consequential international roles – in which several currencies are used in cross-border payments and are regarded as stable stores of value by international investors. Multiple central banks would act as international lenders and liquidity providers of last resort.

Just as a diverse ecosystem is good for planet Earth, a multipolar monetary system would be good for the global economy. If there were a shortage of liquidity, and one central bank fell down on the job – for instance, if the Federal Reserve were dominated by isolationist governors – then central banks in China and Europe could step up. When foreign central banks needed liquidity, they would turn to Beijing and Frankfurt, as well as New York and Washington.

However, we are in a world now where coordination is difficult to achieve, partly because the current US administration makes it difficult to coordinate – or to use another word, cooperate – smoothly with other governments.

The best we can do is hope that the central banks and governments responsible for these different currencies follow sound and stable policies, so that the exchange rates between their currencies remain relatively stable.

If they each understand that they have some responsibility for the stability and liquidity of the global system, they can act individually to help to stabilise the system.

If the issuers of the currencies that are internationally used all follow sound and stable policies, that would be better than our current dollar-dominated system.

Note, however, that my previous statement had a lot of sentences that began with the word “if”.

Consider, for example, the Federal Reserve and the dollar currency swap lines that it provides to other countries. By and large, it only provides them to friendly governments – countries that are geopolitically aligned with the United States – rather than providing them more widely.

On the other hand, the People’s Bank of China swap lines are very diverse. It provides them to politically aligned countries in Asia, but it also provides them to the Bank of England, the European Central Bank and others. I worry about whether the US model of only providing liquidity to the country’s friends is compatible with global stability.

Looking back through history, are there any precedents for a multipolar monetary system, or are we entering an entirely unprecedented paradigm?

One that is relevant to us today is the gold standard period before 1913, when currencies were linked to gold, but gold was not really used for international transactions. Gold is very heavy and risky to ship internationally, so currencies were used for payments.

The British pound sterling, the French franc, and the German mark – all three of them were widely used for cross-border transactions in that system before 1913. The system worked smoothly because the policies of the issuing central banks and governments were sound and stable.

A very different precedent would be the 1920s and 30s, when both the US dollar and the British pound sterling were used internationally. In the 1920s, the two currencies were equal in importance. But neither the issuing central bank nor government followed sound and stable policies. Both the US and the UK had crises of one sort or another in the early 1930s. There was flight from the two international reserve currencies. As a result, that system worked very poorly.

We have seen multipolar systems before. Whether they work well or poorly depends on the stability of the underlying policies.
