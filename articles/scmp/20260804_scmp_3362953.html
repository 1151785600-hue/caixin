Arrest of ‘fake Filipino’ sharpens concerns over security risks in key sectors

SCMP | FREE | 1101 words | 2026-08-04

The detention of a Chinese national accused of posing as a Filipino for more than two decades has sharpened concerns in the Philippines that identity fraud could be used to gain access to sectors vital to national security.

Immigration authorities arrested Lawrence Ke Sy, president, largest incorporator and majority shareholder of Maxipro Development Corp., on June 27, although the arrest was only disclosed to the public on Monday.

Maxipro is a contractor involved in projects for the National Grid Corp. of the Philippines (NGCP), the privately owned company responsible for operating, maintaining and developing the country’s state-owned power grid.

Authorities did not disclose how Sy was captured but presented documents they said were fraudulently obtained to support his alleged claim to Philippine citizenship, including a Philippine passport, birth certificate, marriage certificate and records from the National Bureau of Investigation and the Land Transportation Office.

Investigators also uncovered an Alien Certificate of Registration Identity Card issued to Sy in 2009 that identified him as a Chinese national born in Fujian province.

“He was immediately taken into custody. He is now facing a case for undesirability for allegedly misrepresenting himself as a Filipino. Cases involving foreigners who use Philippine documents to pretend or misrepresent themselves as Filipinos are considered violations of immigration laws,” Bureau of Immigration spokeswoman Dana Sandoval was quoted by news outlet Bilyonaryo as saying.

According to the Bureau of Immigration, citing Securities and Exchange Commission records, Maxipro’s 2026 General Information Sheet showed Sy owned 88.8 per cent of the company’s shares, down from 98.8 per cent in 2025.

Maxipro’s website says it has completed 11 projects for NGCP and is undertaking six more.

Identity fraud as security threat

Experts say the operation leading to Sy’s arrest underscores a shift in how Philippine authorities approach national security, with identity fraud increasingly seen as a possible route into sensitive sectors.

Edmund Tayao, a law professor at San Beda Graduate School of Law in Manila and president of the Political Economic Elemental Researchers and Strategists think tank, said the concept was no longer limited to threats directly targeting the government or the Filipino people.

It now also encompassed less visible activities that could undermine the country’s critical infrastructure, key institutions and overall resilience, he said.

Danielito Jimenez, a doctor of civil law and founder of the advocacy platform The Pinoy Street Lawyer Official, said passport fraud – including the falsification of birth certificates – was increasingly being examined through a national security lens when it was used to conceal a person’s true identity or facilitate access to critical sectors.

“Clearly, when a fake Filipino identity is used to enter strategic industries like power, telecommunications, or other critical infrastructure, it should be a national security concern. Also, it can be used to circumvent constitutional limits on foreign ownership,” Jimenez told This Week in Asia.

Justin Kyle Malarayap, a visiting assistant professor at Arellano University in Manila, described authorities’ approach as a “strategic rebalance”.

Since the early 2000s, Manila had already linked passport fraud to national security concerns because foreign terrorists and terrorism financiers often came to the Philippines carrying tampered or falsified documents, Malarayap said.

“Therefore, leveraging lessons learned since the early 2000s, the tightening of security against falsified documentation may appear aggressive and coordinated to foreign observers, but it should not be viewed as targeting any particular nationality,” he said.

Recent high-profile cases illustrated why authorities had adopted a broader national security approach, Jimenez said.

He cited the case of Alice Guo that exposed how a questionable Filipino identity could be used to gain political power and infiltrate local government. Guo, also known as Guo Hua Ping, is a Chinese national who entered the Philippines in 2003 and later served as mayor of Bamban, Tarlac.

She came under scrutiny in 2024 after authorities linked her to a Philippine offshore gaming operator allegedly involved in fraud, human trafficking and illegal detention, then fled the country during a Senate investigation before being arrested in Indonesia.

In November last year, a court found her guilty of human trafficking and sentenced her to life imprisonment.

Another high-profile case involves mining tycoon Joseph Sy, who is accused of fraudulently obtaining Philippine citizenship and compromising national security through his honorary role in the Philippine Coast Guard.

Joseph Sy, who also uses the Chinese name Chen Zhong Zhen, was detained at Ninoy Aquino International Airport after investigators said his fingerprints matched those of a Chinese citizen previously issued a long-term visa.

The immigration bureau said Joseph Sy remained in its lawful custody while deportation proceedings and an investigation into his citizenship status were pending.

The Chinese embassy has not publicly commented on the cases involving Guo or Lawrence Ke Sy, but it previously expressed concern over what it described as the increasing number of arrests of Chinese nationals in the Philippines, urging authorities to handle such cases fairly and in accordance with Philippine law.

The military has called for cases involving foreign nationals engaged in critical infrastructure projects to be thoroughly investigated.

Retired Rear Admiral Roy Vincent Trinidad, a spokesman for the Armed Forces of the Philippines, said authorities should determine whether foreign nationals involved in essential government infrastructure projects posed national security risks.

“Critical government infrastructure is called critical because it is essential for our survival as a people and as a nation,” Trinidad said, warning there could be significant security risks if hostile foreign powers controlled such infrastructure.

But analysts say Manila’s challenge is tightening scrutiny over sensitive sectors without treating foreign participation as a threat.

Jimenez said stronger national security measures need not come at the expense of foreign investment, arguing that the government could pursue both objectives simultaneously.

Authorities should focus on enforcing existing laws, verifying the true ownership of companies and imposing stricter screening on businesses operating in sectors critical to national security, he added.

Malarayap urged the government to address domestic vulnerabilities, saying that proper oversight should be coupled with ownership rules favouring Filipino investors, especially in sectors where companies operating in the Philippines had significant foreign ownership.

Jimenez called for stricter measures on passports, birth records and corporate ownership, especially in sectors critical to national security.

One possible measure, Jimenez said, was a law allowing the government to cancel or nullify fraudulently obtained birth certificates to enable authorities to resolve such cases more quickly instead of relying on lengthy court proceedings.

“Enforcement must, however, remain fair and evidence-based to avoid unfairly targeting legitimate foreign investors, naturalised Filipinos, or Chinese-Filipino communities. The goal should be protecting the country’s interests without undermining the rule of law or investor confidence,” Jimenez said.
