US scientist Elsa Reichmanis defends global collaboration amid scrutiny of science exchange

SCMP | FREE | 1142 words | 2026-08-01

American materials scientist Elsa Reichmanis, whose pioneering work helped lay part of the foundation for modern semiconductor manufacturing, said scientific collaboration should transcend rivalry after receiving one of China’s highest honours for foreign experts, describing science as “a universal language” that benefits all of society.

Reichmanis, a professor of chemical engineering in Lehigh University’s department of chemical and biomolecular engineering and the director of its Institute for Functional Materials and Devices, was one of nine recipients of the China International Science and Technology Cooperation Award in early July.

The award recognises exceptional contributions to scientific exchanges and collaboration with Chinese researchers.

Reichmanis is best known for her groundbreaking work on chemically amplified photoresists – light-sensitive materials used to etch microscopic circuits onto silicon wafers.

Her chemistry made it commercially viable to pack tens of millions of transistors – or more – onto a single silicon chip, laying the groundwork for the nanoscale semiconductor industry.

“I was shocked,” said the 72-year-old Reichmanis, an accomplished US chemistry and materials scientist, in an interview with the South China Morning Post.

China held the award ceremony on July 8 at the Great Hall of the People in Beijing. Recipients came from eight countries: the US, France, Germany, the Netherlands, Russia, Australia, Belgium and Portugal, representing marine engineering, artificial intelligence (AI), photovoltaics and oncology, among other fields.

The award, established by the State Council – China’s cabinet – in 1994, honours those who collaborate with Chinese partners on significant scientific achievements, introduce advanced technologies, cultivate domestic scientific talent and promote international scientific exchange and cooperation.

This year’s award carries added significance as it comes at a fragile moment for US-China scientific exchange, which faces growing scrutiny amid intensifying technology competition and tightening research restrictions.

On the day of the award ceremony, the US National Science Foundation said in an open letter that it would bar its funded researchers from collaborating with restricted parties, particularly Chinese universities and technology firms, an escalation after years of mounting scrutiny of research ties with China.

Reichmanis said fundamental scientific research should remain distinct from technological competition.

In a sense, the development of technology can be independent from the science

Elsa Reichmanis

“Science is a universal language,” she said. “Scientific collaboration and communication, and having diversity of interactions … provides global society with a knowledge base that can benefit everyone.

“In a sense, the development of technology can be independent from the science”, she added, saying the discipline could be used to build pathways and understanding across borders to help further innovation globally.

Reichmanis’ own relationship with China developed gradually, beginning with early exchanges between the two countries’ chemical societies before expanding to collaborations with Chinese institutions.

A little over two decades ago, she began taking part in joint activities between the American Chemical Society (ACS) and the Chinese Chemical Society, helping organise academic exchanges and build ties with Chinese researchers.

Organisational exchanges gradually developed into a research partnership with Shanghai-based Donghua University. Since 2013, she has worked with the university’s State Key Laboratory of Advanced Fiber Materials to jointly train doctoral students, advance research collaboration and cultivate talent.

Reichmanis said decades working with China had given her a first-hand view of the country’s rise in research capabilities.

When she first visited Chinese laboratories in the early 2000s, state-of-the-art scientific instruments had begun to appear, she said, though some were still relatively underutilised.

But since then, she had observed sustained investment in research infrastructure, growing numbers of scientists and students, as well as steadily improving research quality.

As one of the executive editors of the ACS journal Chemistry of Materials, Reichmanis said she had seen a sharp increase in submissions from Chinese institutions.

“The number of manuscripts being accepted is increasing,” she said, calling it “a testament to the quality of the research that is being done”, adding that the research taking place in China was globally competitive.

Despite her long collaboration with Chinese institutions and early focus on chemically amplified photoresists, Reichmanis dismissed suggestions that she had helped China develop photoresist technology.

China has pushed to develop domestic photoresists to cut its reliance on foreign suppliers. It has made progress in entry-level materials, with a self-sufficiency rate of more than 20 per cent in older G-line and I-line photoresists used for power chips and LEDs, though the high-end market is still dominated by Japanese and US companies.

After Reichmanis received the science cooperation award, voices emerged online linking her pioneering work on chemically amplified photoresists to China’s drive to develop substitutes and localise chipmaking technology.

[Scientific] communication and networking … are going to continue. And it’s really at the essence of making the world a better place

Elsa Reichmanis

Reichmanis said that since her research in the field had been published openly in peer-reviewed journals decades ago, it now served as part of the broader scientific discoveries available worldwide.

“The research I do … is not application-specific. It’s in the realm of fundamental science,” she said, adding that “the research that I do now is very far removed from my early research”.

According to the Lehigh University website, Reichmanis’ work focuses on studying how to control materials at the sub-nanometre scale in “transistor devices, photovoltaics and high-capacity energy storage”.

An Australian emigrant who moved to the US, Reichmanis started her industry career at the AT&T Bell Laboratories, now known as Nokia Bell Labs, following a three-year postdoctoral fellowship at Syracuse University, where she earned her PhD in organic chemistry at age 22 in 1975.

At Bell Labs, Reichmanis and her team helped solve a critical bottleneck in modern semiconductor manufacturing by developing new “chemically amplified resists” for deep ultraviolet lithography, allowing chipmakers to print extremely small and precise circuit patterns that conventional optical photoresists could not achieve.

Her chemistry work made it commercially viable to pack tens of millions of transistors – or more – onto a single silicon chip, laying the groundwork for the nanoscale semiconductor industry.

Reichmanis credited the achievement to Bell Labs’ collaborative culture, emphasising that the breakthrough had been a team effort.

“I’m a very small piece of that really large effort,” she said, adding that advances in chipmaking had depended on contributions from chemists, physicists, engineers, materials scientists and equipment designers working together.

That collaborative philosophy at Bell Labs, she said, had shaped her entire career.

“Just about everything I’ve done in my career has been the result of very valuable collaborations.”

When asked about whether growing US-China tensions would threaten future scientific cooperation, Reichmanis remained optimistic.

“[Scientific] communication and networking … are going to continue,” she said, adding that technology would facilitate continued interactions with global support for multidisciplinary, fundamental research to underpin everything that scientists do.

“And it’s really at the essence of making the world a better place.”
