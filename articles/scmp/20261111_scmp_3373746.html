China’s tech funding divide faces test as fears of an AI bubble rattle markets

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1845 words | 2026-11-11

For Jason Wang, a media operator based in Zhengzhou in central China’s Henan province, switching his long-held investment strategy of buying gold to tech-themed funds was a bittersweet decision.

The 28-year-old retail investor, who has no idea about tech company fundamentals, started buying tech funds – focused on China’s semiconductors, robotics and similar sectors – at the end of last year amid a bull run that lasted until the middle of this year.

Watching returns on his semiconductor funds surge to a peak of 170 per cent before plunging to just over 10 per cent recently has been an emotional roller coaster.

“It’s easy to imagine how painful it must be for people who bought near the peak,” he said, adding that he only stopped buying the dip recently when some stocks kept falling with no sign of rebounding. “I was naive and had never been tested by market swings.”

Meanwhile, industry insiders and professional investors, who had been flocking to artificial intelligence, semiconductors and robotics companies over the past few years – a trend that added to those firms’ ever-growing valuations – are sharing the same concern over market turbulence with retail investors like Wang.

A founder at a Shanghai-based tech company focusing on AI-powered video gaming, who declined to be named, said that it was “absolutely terrifying to wake up and see all stock markets plunge”.

Amid a global bloodbath in tech stocks exacerbated by concerns over the future of the US AI infrastructure buildout following a “blue wave” of Democratic Party victories in the midterm elections, listed tech firms in Shanghai and Hong Kong have also been battered by a wave of selling in recent months.

Shanghai’s Star 50 Index, which tracks 50 leading companies on China’s Nasdaq-style technology board, had plunged nearly 50 per cent from its late-June peak by November 3. And Hong Kong’s Hang Seng Tech Index fell 11.7 per cent in October alone, bringing its losses for the year to nearly 32 per cent as of October 30.

The Shanghai start-up founder said that he worried the volatility would deal a blow to public companies and deter others from going public in the next few years.

The technology industries in the US and China – the two leading powers of the AI era – had both enjoyed the recent frenzy of high valuations and, in the downturn, are facing the same fears of an AI bubble.

However, the US tech sector had withstood boom-bust cycles before, including the dotcom bubble in the late 1990s and 2000s, and the 2008 global financial crisis, while the Chinese tech industry is too young to have experienced those.

The question at present is how the current storm will play out to reshape funding dynamics across the Chinese landscape.

Analysts said China’s technology industry faced a growing test of its ability to sustain investment amid intensifying competition with the US, as recent developments threatened to widen the funding gap between its national champions and smaller players.

Chelsey Tam, senior equity analyst at Morningstar, said current market conditions would make it more difficult for local tech firms to raise capital, either through initial public offerings (IPOs) or private investments. Nevertheless, she said it still made sense for companies to tap channels such as equity or debt markets as long as they were “fairly or still overvalued after the share price decline”.

The ability to raise capital has become increasingly uneven, with investors favouring established technology giants over smaller, cash-strapped start-ups.

“We do see a crowding-out effect … as capital providers prefer backing AI initiatives housed inside cash-generative incumbents over stand-alone, loss-making start-ups,” said Ivan Su, Morningstar’s director of equity research.

A Beijing-based investor at a state-backed venture capital company said his firm had already stopped backing consumer-facing AI agent start-ups, focusing instead on business applications that could deliver measurable productivity benefits with a clear revenue path.

The investor, who requested anonymity due to the sensitivity of the topic, said the current sell-off was exacerbated by heavy institutional holdings in previously surging computing and semiconductor stocks, with losses now spreading across the technology supply chain.

The rout was weighing on some start-ups’ funding, according to Lin Jie, managing partner at Fuzhou-based Pioteer Investment.

Early-stage, highly valuable and cash-burning hard-tech companies would also be under apparent pressure, with some companies’ funding cycles extended from three months to up to a year.

Our assessment of the companies remains unchanged, but we will adjust the pace of our investments

Lin Jie, managing partner at Pioteer Investment

Furthermore, investors should get used to “value discounting” as the venture market would now benchmark against the stock market, according to Lin.

Still, Lin argued, the current market sell-off was not a replay of the last dotcom bubble but more likely a “healthy correction” following a sharp rally in technology stocks, particularly in computing infrastructure, optical components, semiconductors and memory chips.

“Our assessment of the companies remains unchanged, but we will adjust the pace of our investments,” he said, adding that long-term trends such as AI infrastructure expansion and semiconductor self-sufficiency remained intact.

For the US market, anxiety over returns came against the backdrop of an unprecedented infrastructure build-out worldwide, exacerbated by fears that the sweeping midterm election victory by the Democrats could derail its AI data centre construction campaign, which some estimated to be as high as US$10 trillion over the next five years.

By comparison, capital expenditure on AI infrastructure among 13 major Chinese companies is projected to surge 103 per cent this year to 932 billion yuan (US$139.6 billion), before exceeding 1.2 trillion yuan in 2027, according to a September study by research firm Rhodium Group.

The firm noted that China’s AI industry faces different financial risks from those in the US, reflecting its greater reliance on equity financing and bank loans to fund AI infrastructure, compared with American technology companies’ increasing use of bond markets.

In the Chinese public equity market, among the hardest hit were optical component suppliers Eoptolink Technology, Suzhou TFC Communication and Zhongji Innolight, whose shares fell around 55 per cent, 53 per cent and 44 per cent, respectively, between October 8 and November 9. Fears of slower AI growth overshadowed the upbeat earnings.

Another example was Chinese AI chip developer Biren Technology’s October fundraising. It raised about HK$4 billion (US$510 million) through a share placement priced nearly one-third below that of a similar placement in July.

In China’s embodied AI sector, financing fell to 33.3 billion yuan across 185 deals in the third quarter, from a quarterly average of 46.8 billion yuan in the first half, according to data compiled by start-up database platform ITJuzi.com and cited by local outlet Yicai.

“Financing channels for smaller projects that lack the ability to generate sustainable cash flow are narrowing, making it increasingly hard for them to survive independently,” Miao Tianyi, managing partner at Puzhuo Capital, was quoted as saying in the Yicai report this week.

Miao expected mergers and acquisitions to become increasingly important as smaller players struggled to secure fresh capital.

Start-ups face another obstacle to securing capital: a slowdown in IPOs, a crucial exit route for venture capital and private equity investors.

China’s three mainland stock exchanges in Shanghai, Shenzhen and Beijing accepted 242 IPO applications in the first half of 2026, compared with just six from July to early November, according to a report by Yicai on Wednesday.

In Hong Kong, about one-in-four planned IPOs failed to reach completion so far this year, the highest proportion since the 2008 global financial crisis, according to data compiled by Bloomberg. The unfinished offerings included deals that were postponed, withdrawn or allowed to lapse.

The funding squeeze came as Beijing sought to steer more capital towards strategically important technologies, while cracking down on indiscriminate investment, highlighting the challenge of supporting smaller innovators without encouraging wasteful spending.

At a November 3 press conference, Ren Zhongbao, deputy director of the National Development and Reform Commission’s Department of Innovation and High-Tech Development, warned that some local governments were crowding into the same emerging industries, calling for stronger national coordination and investment based on regional strengths.

Yet policymakers remain committed to expanding financing support for emerging industries and, by comparison, the rout has done little to deter investment in China’s leading AI players.

DeepSeek, a leading Chinese rival to US frontier AI labs, has raised about 100 billion yuan in its most recent funding round from investors including chipmaker GigaDevice, according to a Reuters report on October 22, citing unidentified people. Local media reports suggested state-backed investors contributed a significant share.

DeepSeek has not publicly confirmed the transaction. The Hangzhou-based company did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

Amid the market turmoil, some of China’s national champions have sought to reassure investors about their prospects, pointing to robust earnings and sustained demand for AI-related products. They also pledged to buy back shares to shore up confidence.

Chinese AI chipmaker Cambricon Technologies said strong demand for computing power would support its long-term growth despite short-term share-price volatility.

ChangXin Technology, the nation’s No 1 memory chipmaker, forecast continued revenue growth in the fourth quarter, while semiconductor equipment supplier Advanced Micro-Fabrication Equipment proposed a share buy-back worth up to 500 million yuan.

According to a tally by Chinese media outlet Financial News, companies listed on Shanghai’s Star Market had disclosed 183 new share buy-back and shareholding-increase plans as of November 2, with a combined maximum value of 24.27 billion yuan.

On Friday, after a US tech sell-off that sent the Nasdaq 100 index down 6.4 per cent – the biggest drop in six years – Beijing called for more efforts to stabilise the stock market in a State Council executive meeting chaired by Premier Li Qiang.

“The different financing channels imply the two sides are constrained by different factors – China by government excess and the US by market pushback,” said Charles Chang, Greater China country lead for corporates at S&P Global Ratings.

Lu Ting, chief China economist at Nomura, said in a note on Friday that unlike the fragmented and politically contentious power grid landscape in the US, China’s domestic data centre expansion was guided by centralised state coordination.

“China is advancing a massive, state-orchestrated push to build a nationwide network of AI data centres, spearheaded by the 15th five-year blueprint,” he said, adding that the “blue wave” in the US election could overall benefit China’s AI economy.

Michael Zhang, a Shanghai-based investor who has traded mainland Chinese stocks for more than two decades, said his portfolio had swung from a 50 per cent gain earlier this year to a 20 per cent loss.

“I lost 5 per cent today alone,” he said. “With 1.2 million yuan in capital, that’s 60,000 yuan gone in a single day.”

But as a seasoned retail investor, Zhang said he remained cautiously optimistic, because he had confidence that government intervention would come.
