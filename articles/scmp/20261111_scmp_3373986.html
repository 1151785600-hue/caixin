Will Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang’s affair become another ‘spy balloon’ saga for US-China ties?

SCMP | FREE | 1112 words | 2026-11-11

China hawks in Washington could weaponise allegations that Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang was targeted by Chinese intelligence, despite denials from Beijing, Chinese experts warn.

They said controversy would give US hardliners fresh ammunition to push for tougher curbs on AI chip exports to China.

One of them compared the potential fallout to the 2023 “spy balloon” incident, when the US shot down in its airspace what Beijing said was an off-course weather balloon, putting relations on ice for months.

Huang admitted over the weekend to having an extramarital affair after the New York Times reported that a Chinese businesswoman with whom he had close ties might have been working for Beijing’s intelligence services. Beijing has rejected the espionage claims as “groundless fabrication designed to smear China”.

The report identified the woman as Alice Huang, or Huang Le, chief executive of a Singapore-based data centre company.

More restrictions on chip exports?

The revelations come amid an already aggressive US campaign to restrict China’s access to cutting-edge chips. Washington has already barred direct exports of the most advanced American AI chips to China, including Nvidia’s flagship Blackwell and Rubin graphics processing units or GPUs.

Analysts in China say the controversy will only intensify the US regulatory clampdown, even if no formal espionage charges are filed.

Zhu Feng, dean of Nanjing University’s school of international studies, expects heightened AI decoupling and an “increased crackdown” on China’s hi-tech sectors.

“What’s important now is that committees in both the Senate and the House [of Representatives] will have hearings and investigations on this. So my view on this matter is simple: it will become another balloon event in China-US relations,” he said.

“The key is not the investigation result; the process of the investigation itself will bring on another anti-China wave,” Zhu said, adding that congressional hawks would seize the moment to demand wider export controls and harsher restrictions against Chinese AI platforms.

Ties hinge on US response

Xin Qiang, a professor and deputy director of the Centre for American Studies at Fudan University, said regardless of whether the allegations led to formal charges, the controversy would still weigh on China-US ties, even though the impact might not be too severe.

“It won’t come to the degree of derailing China-US relations,” he said, pointing to the balloon incident as precedent for how tensions might ease once the media frenzy subsided.

However, he warned that the fallout could hit Nvidia’s chip exports to China directly. The US was likely to tighten oversight even for less advanced processors, he said, enforcing stricter end-user checks and tracking of chips routed through third countries.

“The two countries’ efforts to cooperate on AI governance, which was mentioned during the Xi–Trump summit, will also become more difficult,” Xin added, referring to the Chinese president’s state visit to Washington in September.

Sun Chenghao, a fellow with Tsinghua University’s Centre for International Security and Strategy, also said the controversy was unlikely to trigger a diplomatic crisis comparable to the 2023 balloon incident, as there was no public evidence establishing Chinese government involvement.

However, it could intensify scrutiny of semiconductor exports and disrupt trade talks, he warned. “If the relevant restrictions are written into law, Trump’s room to adjust chip licences in negotiations with China will narrow,” Sun said.

The greatest impact would be on industrial cooperation, he added, with tighter checks on chip purchases, cloud computing and joint research.

“This is a test of whether China and the United States can maintain strategic stability amid intensifying technological competition.”

The Huang scandal broke just days before US President Donald Trump and Chinese leader Xi Jinping are expected to meet on the sidelines of next week’s Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen, southern China.

Huang was part of the US delegation during Trump’s May state visit to Beijing. Four months later, the tech billionaire and his wife, Lori, sat alongside Xi at the head table during a White House state dinner hosted by Trump.

On Monday, Trump defended the Nvidia chief as “an honourable guy” who had done an “amazing job for our country”.

Trump said he asked Huang directly whether he knew Alice Huang was a foreign spy. “He said ‘I had no idea that she was a spy’ and I really believe him,” Trump told reporters.

Trump has said he is open to bringing Huang with him to Shenzhen.

Huang has long lobbied against stricter controls on AI chip exports to China, arguing that blanket bans could force Beijing to fast-track domestic alternatives such as Huawei Technologies.

Zhu said: “What happens next will depend on how the Trump administration responds, what kind of scrutiny the relevant committees in the US House of Representatives and Senate undertake, and what legislation they might pass.”

Three House Republican committee chairs have asked Huang to attend a closed-door joint briefing on November 18 regarding Nvidia’s “security protocols against foreign adversary espionage”, though it remains unclear whether he will appear.

Is it racial bias?

Prominent neurobiologist and Peking University professor Rao Yi said the uproar exposed double standards rooted in racial bias against ethnic Chinese in the United States.

In a social media post on Tuesday, Rao said that while white tycoons rarely faced questions over national allegiance in similar scandals, Huang’s personal situation had quickly been elevated into a national security crisis.

“Is an affair with a Chinese person a national security issue? Why wasn’t [Australian-born US media mogul Rupert] Murdoch’s case treated the same way? Is it because the person involved was white, so no national security problem would arise, and his media would not be ‘coloured’ as Chinese media?” Rao posted, referring to Murdoch’s 1999 marriage to China-born Wendi Deng. The pair divorced in 2013.

“For an ethnic Chinese person, however, intelligence agencies monitor their private life, US government sources leak it to the press, and investigations immediately follow,” Rao wrote.

Silicon Valley precedents

American technology executives have previously faced public scrutiny over extramarital affairs and workplace relationships, with revelations involving Microsoft co-founder Bill Gates and Amazon founder Jeff Bezos attracting widespread media attention. But those scandals never amounted to much more than tabloid fodder.

Gates, whose 27-year marriage to Melinda French Gates ended in 2021, acknowledged three extramarital affairs during testimony before the US House Oversight Committee in June this year as part of its investigation into convicted sex offender Jeffrey Epstein.

Bezos’ relationship with TV presenter Lauren Sanchez emerged during his 2019 divorce from MacKenzie Scott. He and Sanchez married in June last year.
