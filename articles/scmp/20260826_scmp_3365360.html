Why US visa pause could have ‘real consequences’ for Asian applicants

SCMP | FREE | 1315 words | 2026-08-26

Asian workers and families already facing years-long waits for US visas could be pushed deeper into uncertainty by a global pause on immigrant visa appointments, as President Donald Trump’s administration tightens legal immigration through new screening and higher costs, experts warn.

The US State Department on Tuesday said it had launched a global training initiative for consular officers at all US embassies and consulates, and that immigrant visa appointments would be adjusted to accommodate it.

The department did not specify the scope or duration of the training, beyond saying it was intended to help officers screen out applicants deemed likely to become dependent on US public benefits and ensure they were evaluated “comprehensively and consistently”.

Immigrant visa applicants with scheduled interviews at US embassies and consulates have begun receiving notices that their appointments are being rescheduled, with new dates to follow, according to reports.

The disruption has left applicants uncertain not only about when they will be interviewed, but also whether documents tied to existing appointment dates – including police clearances, medical checks and financial records – will need updating.

For Asian applicants, experts say, the impact could be especially acute in countries with large visa pipelines and already crowded consular calendars.

“It’s not clear if this is being handled embassy by embassy or centrally. The Asian countries with the largest number of pending applications will likely be hit the hardest,” said Russell A. Stamets, a partner at the New Delhi-based law firm Circle of Counsels.

Srinivasan Balakrishnan, director of strategic engagements and partnerships at the Indic Researchers Forum, said the immediate hit for Asian applicants was cancellation notices and open-ended waits, as no restart date was given.

“In high-volume places like India, the Philippines and Vietnam, where family queues already stretch years, this is unlikely to be a few days’ blip. It risks months of cascading delays as slots get rebooked amid heavier screening. India, with its massive skilled and family pipeline, stands especially exposed and will feel the backlog first,” he said.

The appointment pause announcement came a day after the Department of Homeland Security proposed a new US$103,265 fee for H-1B petitions subject to the annual cap – a move that could sharply increase the cost of hiring skilled foreign workers and is expected to hit Chinese and Indian professionals particularly hard.

Immigrant visas cover permanent migration, including family reunification and some employment-based categories, while H-1B visas are temporary skilled-worker visas.

Experts said the US moves suggested employment-based applicants could face particular scrutiny, even as family cases risked being swept into broader appointment delays.

“It’s possible that employment-based applicants will be treated differently than simple family reunification applicants. Taken together with the renewed push to add at least US$100,000 per visa for employment-based visas, there may be further implications for this type of applicant,” Stamets said.

Balakrishnan warned employment-based applicants would face “real consequences”, including postponed interviews, stalled careers, frozen job offers and prolonged uncertainty for highly skilled talent America still needs.

Washington’s move implied that family reunification cases from the Philippines, Vietnam, China and especially India would be among the hardest hit because those categories already dominated immigrant visa caseloads and waiting lists, Balakrishnan said.

Hoang Lan Anh, an Australia-based academic who has studied Vietnamese overseas migration, said US-bound Vietnamese applicants were concentrated mainly in student, skilled or employment-based visas and family sponsorship.

“Because these applicants – particularly student and skilled-visa applicants – tend to come from middle- and upper-class backgrounds, I wouldn’t expect this pause to trigger major economic shocks for the applicant pool itself, in the way a similar disruption might affect lower-income labour migrants elsewhere,” Anh said.

But the sudden halt could still significantly disrupt individual plans for study, work or family reunification, she said. “These are not abstract inconveniences, but plans people have built their lives around, sometimes over years.”

The consequences could be especially acute for family-sponsorship visas, which Anh said were often the outcome of years or even decades of planning, saving and coordination across generations.

“When processing queues are suddenly frozen, the immediate fallout is less about a single missed deadline than about the collapse of a whole scaffold of interdependent decisions built around an expected timeline,” Anh said.

“Families lose not just time, but also the ability to plan with any confidence at all.”

Those planning costs could quickly become financial ones if the pause lasts long enough for immigration documents to expire.

A Philippines-based immigration lawyer and lecturer, who requested anonymity because of the sensitivity of the issue, said the costs could be especially burdensome for family-based applicants because many documents required for US immigration cases were valid only for limited periods.

“Many US immigration requirements are supported by documents that are valid for only six months or other limited periods,” the lawyer said. “As a result, the reopening itself does not provide relief from document validity requirements, and affected applicants may be required to obtain and submit updated documentation at their own expense.”

Stamets agreed, saying: “Since the entire thrust of the administration’s immigration policy is to make immigration more difficult and expensive except for a favoured few classes and categories of people, I would anticipate that any time-sensitive documentation will need to be updated.”

The larger concern, Balakrishnan warned, was that the pause could be followed by a “permanently slower, more subjective process that could reshape legal immigration from Asia for years”.

Administration versus judiciary

The training is explicitly tied to public-charge screening, a US immigration law assessment of whether an applicant is likely to depend on certain government benefits.

That rationale has also placed the appointment pause in the middle of a wider legal and political fight over the Trump administration’s immigration agenda.

“A pause like this rarely translates into a short, contained disruption. Consular sections typically batch and schedule interviews weeks or months in advance, so even a brief suspension forces a backlog that takes far longer to clear than the pause,” said Amritraj Kaushal, an advocate in India’s Supreme Court.

Vivek Mishra, deputy director of strategic studies at the Observer Research Foundation, said the latest move appeared to be part of a showdown between the administration and the US judiciary.

A federal judge on Friday struck down a ban on visas for immigrants from 75 countries, finding it was contrary to law and outside the statutory authority of US Secretary of State Marco Rubio.

The State Department had paused the issuance of immigrant visas from almost 40 per cent of the world’s nations in a move that it said would prevent the entry of people who would “take welfare and public benefits”.

The Philippines-based lawyer said the shift from a country-specific suspension to a worldwide training pause “appears to be an attempt to sidestep” the federal court ruling that barred nationality-based discrimination.

The State Department would still have to comply with the court’s ruling, but a legal block on the 75-country policy might not prevent consular officers from applying stricter scrutiny to otherwise eligible applicants, the lawyer said.

Mishra called the visa pause a “doubling down” by the US administration. “They are saying we are going ahead with a broader mandate this time. They are giving the alibi that consulates need training, which might be true.”

He predicted the US would issue new guidance and standard operating procedures to its embassies, resulting in more stringent visa checks.

“If they see what they consider as a red flag, it will reduce your chances of a visa,” he said.

Even if appointments resume, experts say the larger concern is that applicants could return to a system in which officers have wider discretion to reject cases under public charge and other screening rules.
