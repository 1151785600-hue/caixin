Big Apple vs Pearl of the Orient? How higher taxes in New York may open door for Hong Kong

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2279 words | 2026-11-04

On November 4 last year, Zohran Mamdani won the New York mayoral election on a promise to make the city more affordable, with expanded social programmes funded in part by raising taxes on the city’s wealthiest residents and most profitable corporations.

Nearly a year since his upset victory, he has secured one major piece of that agenda: a surcharge on high-end second homes. He also continues to push for higher corporate and income taxes.

The proposals have intensified criticism of Mamdani, a self-described democratic socialist, with opponents arguing that his approach to taxing the wealthy amounts to “socialism in action”.

Speaking at the Milken Institute Global Conference in May, Citadel founder Ken Griffin warned that Mamdani was sending a clear message that New York “does not welcome success”, raising the prospect of moving parts of his firm’s operations out of the city.

Halfway across the globe, Hong Kong is charting an entirely different course. The Asian financial hub is rolling out fresh tax breaks and other red-carpet incentives to lure high-net-worth individuals (HNWIs), aiming to solidify its position as a premier global wealth management centre.

This year’s Global Financial Leaders’ Investment Summit, kicking off on Monday and bringing dozens of Wall Street and European heavyweights to Hong Kong, gives the city a high-profile platform to make its case.

Analysts and overseas scholars said New York’s tax push gave Hong Kong “a timely opening” to lure wealthy families reassessing where to base themselves, along with the capital they control.

Hong Kong could become “an attractive destination” for wealthy families considering a move away from the US, they added, although it remained uncertain how much tax policy alone influences relocation decisions.

Wall Street voices opposition

Since taking office in January, Mamdani has pushed state and city lawmakers to consider an array of proposals targeting the wealthy. His most significant win so far is the “pied-a-terre” levy on second homes, which took effect on July 1.

The annual levy applies to non-primary residences worth US$5 million or more, with graduated rates ranging from 0.8 to 1.3 per cent of a property’s value. Separate rates apply to condominiums and co-ops valued at US$1 million or more until July 2028.

Mamdani thrust the policy into the spotlight when he filmed a video outside the US$238 million Manhattan penthouse of Griffin, citing the property as an example of high-end second homes targeted by the levy.

Griffin later described the video as “creepy and weird”, saying Mamdani’s actions brought back the “trauma” of his time in Chicago, where he clashed with local leaders over crime and public policy before relocating Citadel’s global corporate headquarters to Miami in 2022. He added that Citadel would expand job creation in Miami rather than Manhattan as a result.

Beyond real estate, the mayor has proposed increasing the city’s income tax rate by 2 percentage points for residents earning more than US$1 million annually, which would lift the top rate to roughly 5.9 per cent from 3.9 per cent. He has also called on New York state to raise its top corporate tax rate to 11.5 per cent from 7.25 per cent.

While state governor Kathy Hochul rejected both tax increases this year, Mamdani’s allies are preparing another push in 2027, with Gustavo Gordillo, co-chair of New York’s Democratic Socialists of America chapter, maintaining that “we have lots of work left to do”.

Other Wall Street figures have also voiced opposition. After meeting Mamdani in May, JPMorgan Chase CEO Jamie Dimon said higher taxes on the rich risked undermining the city’s position as a business hub.

Dimon said New York must compete with cities including Shanghai, Hong Kong, Singapore and Nashville, warning that people “vote with their feet”.

Larry Ikard, Yuvarra

New York already collects more tax per resident than any other US state. State and local governments took in more than US$12,000 per resident in the 2024 financial year, 71 per cent above the national average, according to the Citizens Budget Commission, a New York fiscal watchdog.

New York City residents also face the country’s highest combined top income tax rate, at about 14.8 per cent, compared with 13.3 per cent in second-placed California.

Combined with high living costs and uneven public services, those fiscal burdens threatened New York’s ability to attract and retain high-income residents, the watchdog warned.

The wealth migration debate

While New York city officials described the proposed tax measures as “a fair way” to address inequality and raise revenue for essential services, some financial industry executives and analysts warned the moves could push affluent residents to lower-tax jurisdictions, taking capital with them.

Larry Ikard, CEO of Yuvarra, an independent lender serving HNWIs, said New York’s evolving tax landscape was prompting wealthy residents to rethink where they live “to a huge extent”.

“You do not have to pass a tax to move people. You just have to convince them it’s coming, or could come,” he said, adding that wealthy families treated calls to “tax the rich” as a signal to start planning ahead.

Kevin Chen Kaifeng, chief economist at Horizon Financial and a member of the Economic Club of Miami, said an exodus was already under way, with senior bankers and lawyers increasingly relocating to Florida.

Large financial firms such as JPMorgan Chase were no longer expanding headcount in New York, he said.

The bank’s New York City workforce has fallen to about 24,000 from 30,000 a decade ago, while its Texas headcount has increased to 32,000 from 26,000, Dimon said in his April shareholder letter.

Goldman Sachs was also moving hundreds of employees out of New York, asking staff to relocate to Dallas or Salt Lake City, international media reported in March last year.

Emanuele Colonnelli, a professor of finance and entrepreneurship at the University of Chicago Booth School of Business, said higher tax rates might not trigger immediate relocations, but they inevitably influence “long-term decisions” around retirement, business sales and the timing of moves after children finish school.

Long-term data points to a decline in New York’s share of high earners. The proportion of US households reporting annual income of more than US$1 million that lived in New York was 9 per cent in 2023, down from 12.7 per cent in 2010 – the steepest decline among US states, according to the Citizens Budget Commission.

The latest Internal Revenue Service data, covering tax returns filed in 2022 and 2023, showed New York lost a net US$9.9 billion in adjusted gross income to other states. Florida gained a net US$20.6 billion.

Florida has no state income tax or estate tax and offers a lower cost of living than New York. The number of tax filers there reporting annual income above US$1 million nearly quadrupled between 2010 and 2023, according to the commission.

Ikard said destinations in Latin America and Europe, including Panama, Costa Rica and Portugal, have also steadily attracted American retirees by offering a high quality of life, relatively low living costs and healthcare systems increasingly geared towards US expatriates.

What can Hong Kong offer?

Hong Kong is stepping up efforts to attract global wealth, pitching its low-tax regime, international connectivity and emerging financial sectors to affluent families.

Salaries tax is capped at a standard rate of 15 per cent on the first HK$5 million (US$637,000) of net income and 16 per cent above that level, while corporate profits are taxed at a headline rate of 16.5 per cent. The city largely taxes only locally sourced income and levies no capital gains, dividend or estate taxes.

In June, the government introduced a bill to widen tax concessions for privately offered funds and family-owned investment vehicles. The proposal would also extend the zero-tax treatment of qualifying carried interest from private equity to hedge funds and other investment strategies.

Colonnelli said Hong Kong “combines relatively low local taxes with the financial sophistication and international connections of a major business centre”. Families, he added, “do not necessarily have to choose between a tax-friendly location and being at the centre of business activities”.

The city ranked third globally in the latest Global Financial Centres Index, behind only New York and London, and placed ahead of London in business environment, human capital and financial sector development.

Boston Consulting Group reported in May that Hong Kong had narrowly overtaken Switzerland as the world’s largest cross-border wealth management hub, overseeing US$2.9 trillion in assets.

Hong Kong was also the preferred retirement destination among wealthy expatriates, chosen by 67 per cent of HNWIs polled in a survey by British wealth management and financial advisory firm St James’s Place covering 450 affluent residents in the city.

The city government is building on that foundation. Its first five-year plan places wealth management ambitions in the opening chapter as part of its “four centres and one hub” strategy, alongside efforts to expand the offshore yuan business and commodities trading.

Access to mainland China added to the appeal, analysts said.

Initial public offerings in Hong Kong raised more than HK$388 billion in the first three quarters of the year, more than double the amount recorded a year earlier, KPMG said. The consultancy attributed the growth to A+H listings, where mainland China-listed firms secure a Hong Kong listing, and specialist technology companies.

Hong Kong’s proximity and connections to mainland China and other Asian markets “can make it easier to build business relationships and pursue investment opportunities there”, Colonnelli said.

The city is also positioning itself as a digital assets hub. It has licensed cryptocurrency exchanges since 2023 and granted its first two stablecoin issuer licences in April.

The government has issued digital green bonds three times since 2023, while regulators have approved tokenised investment products. The Hong Kong Monetary Authority plans to test tokenising HK$1.3 trillion of Exchange Fund Bills by year-end.

Regulatory clarity is one of the city’s biggest draws, according to Sascha Goetz, CEO of RootstockCollective, which supports the bitcoin-linked Rootstock blockchain.

“In the US, it’s a lot of back and forth,” he said, citing the US Senate’s failure in September to advance a crypto market-structure bill. “For a family office planning 10 years ahead, that uncertainty is a real cost.”

What might hold wealthy families back?

Despite Hong Kong’s efforts to attract global wealth, analysts said tax rates were only part of the equation. Wealthy families must also consider the durability of tax policies, the predictability of regulations and whether a city could meet their needs beyond business.

A key structural constraint for American nationals is the limited relief available under US tax law.

American citizens and green-card holders are generally taxed on their worldwide income regardless of where they live. Relocating to Hong Kong could reduce their exposure to New York state and city taxes, which together carry a top marginal rate of about 14.8 per cent, but it would not eliminate federal income tax rates of up to 37 per cent.

New York also retains advantages as a global financial centre. Nasdaq was ranked first among worldwide exchanges by initial public offering funds raised in the first nine months of 2026, ahead of Hong Kong, driven by the listings of SpaceX and SK Hynix, KPMG said.

It also topped the Global Financial Centres Index, ranking five points ahead of Hong Kong.

Beyond tax and market access, “long-term legal and regulatory predictability, and the evolution of US-China relations” remained a key consideration for global capital, Colonnelli said.

Since the national security law took effect in 2020, followed by local security legislation in 2024, debate over Hong Kong’s autonomy has intensified. The government has said the laws restored stability and rejected criticism from Washington and other Western nations.

A February survey by the American Chamber of Commerce in Hong Kong found that 94 per cent of respondents were confident in the city’s rule of law – up from 83 per cent a year earlier – although 59 per cent cited geopolitical tensions as a primary operating hurdle.

Beijing’s tighter oversight of wealth held abroad is another consideration for families with business interests on both sides of the Pacific. China’s tax authority urged residents in January to declare overseas income earned from 2022 to 2024, and began taxing offshore trusts at 20 per cent in July.

Broader trade dynamics also cloud the horizon. A September summit in Washington between President Xi Jinping and US President Donald Trump produced a two-month extension of the trade truce through January 10, leaving deeper structural disputes unresolved.

“The concern is not just what is permitted today, but whether that will remain possible over the next decade,” Colonnelli said.

“A known tax bill can be planned for, but uncertainty about the rules governing a family’s wealth is harder to manage.”

For many families, however, practical lifestyle considerations outweigh macroeconomic ones. “The biggest considerations are probably very human ones: schools, housing costs, time zones and distance from family,” Ikard said.

Hong Kong remained the world’s least affordable housing market for a 16th year, according to international public policy research organisation Demographia, while 71 per cent of American Chamber of Commerce in Hong Kong members cited living costs as the top obstacle to hiring foreign talent.

Time zone differences present another challenge. Hong Kong is 12 to 13 hours ahead of New York, making it “not a practical choice for most executives”, Chen said.

“Hong Kong’s appeal to Americans has to go beyond the tax bill,” Ikard said.
