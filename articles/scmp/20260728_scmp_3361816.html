Southeast Asia must embrace reforms for ‘China plus one’ to succeed

SCMP | FREE | 1125 words | 2026-07-28

In recent years, Southeast Asian governments have viewed the “China plus one” strategy as their best chance to escape the middle-income trap. As global manufacturers looked to diversify away from China because of rising costs, trade tensions and supply-chain vulnerabilities, Indonesia, Thailand and Malaysia seemed perfectly placed to benefit.

The promise was simple and compelling: new factories would arrive, more formal jobs would be created, wages would rise in tandem with higher productivity and decades of disappointing growth would give way to economic upgrades. This story is now colliding with a harsher reality. The anticipated wave of transformative foreign investments has been smaller than hoped, while a flood of cheap Chinese goods is hurting local industries. Wage growth remains weak, factory closures are spreading and political frustration is rising.

The source of the problem is not China. It is that domestic politics and institutional weaknesses in much of Southeast Asia have prevented countries from turning external opportunity into industrial progress at home.

Thailand shows the gap between headline success and underlying weakness most clearly. Its Board of Investment recorded a decade high in applications for electric vehicles, electronics and digital projects. Yet average manufacturing wages have been flat since mid-2023. Traditional vehicle production fell nearly 20 per cent last year even as the transition to EVs disrupted an entire network of suppliers built around the internal combustion engine. More than half the workforce remains in low-productivity informal jobs. Despite attracting new investments, Thailand’s growth forecasts have been repeatedly revised downwards to just 1.8–2 per cent.

Indonesia’s situation is more dire. The country possesses the scale, resources and young population that should make it a major winner from supply-chain diversification. Instead, business confidence has collapsed. Major business groups report being pressured to buy “patriotic bonds” and face land seizures justified by vague environmental claims. A new financial-sector law has weakened central bank independence.

More worryingly, Indonesia’s middle class has shrunk by 9.5 million people in six years even as official growth hovered around 5 per cent. Nickel downstreaming was promoted as industrial upgrading, yet the net benefit to the wider economy looks questionable once large profit outflows and environmental costs are considered. The digital economy shows promise, but it remains too small to lift the broader economy without a strong manufacturing base.

Malaysia has been more resilient, particularly in semiconductors and data centres tied to global AI demand. Its stronger institutions give it better tools to respond to external shocks. Even here, however, traditional manufacturing faces severe cost pressures, and political risks are increasing ahead of elections.

Domestic policy failures

Three interconnected domestic failures explain why the China plus one opportunity is failing to deliver for Southeast Asia’s main economies.

First, political short-termism has repeatedly overridden institutional reform. In Indonesia, compromises with vested interests degraded anti-corruption institutions while the politics of populism made it difficult for elites to focus their attention on key structural reforms. In Thailand, two decades of zero-sum conflict between popularly elected parties and the royalist-military establishment diverted resources away from much-needed investment in education, skills and industrial upgrades.

Second, Southeast Asian countries often pursued industrial strategies that reduced pressure on local firms to upgrade. Indonesia emphasised resource nationalism and commodity downstreaming. Thailand protected large domestic conglomerates that dominated the services sector. Both approaches allowed high returns behind protective walls rather than forced firms to compete globally.

At the same time, multinational corporations that outsourced part of their production to these countries kept their high-value functions such as research, design and complex engineering in their home countries, leaving Southeast Asian operations in lower-value assembly roles. This model produces impressive foreign direct investment statistics in the short term, but little wage growth or technological diffusion.

Third, governments have been slow to manage the other side of China’s economic pressures: its export surge since the Covid-19 pandemic. Weak domestic demand in China combined with Western tariffs has pushed excess capacity into regional markets. Ceramics factories in northern Thailand have closed in large numbers. Indonesian textile workers have lost jobs. Malaysian retailers and logistics operators complain of being undercut by e-commerce platforms and opaque “zero-dollar transport” arrangements that exploit regulatory loopholes.

What must change

If Southeast Asia is to turn the current difficult environment into an opportunity, a few concrete shifts are required.

First, governments must restore or build credible, independent economic institutions. This means protecting central bank independence, rebuilding professional economic teams and ending the practice of using state power to extract resources from the private sector. Without policy predictability and institutional trust, even generous investment incentives will fail to produce sustained capital formation.

Second, industrial policy needs to shift from protection and resource nationalism towards forcing competitiveness. This involves reducing the barriers that allow inefficient firms to survive, aligning vocational training with the skills demanded by modern manufacturing and services and actively supporting the formalisation of large swathes of the workforce. Informal employment above 50 per cent, as seen in Thailand, is incompatible with productivity growth.

Third, infrastructure and logistics must be treated as urgent national priorities rather than secondary concerns. Thailand’s relatively weak ranking in global logistics performance already undermines its attractiveness for high-value supply chains. Improving ports, roads, digital connectivity and trade facilitation would raise returns on existing investment and make relocation more attractive.

Fourth, countries need a more realistic approach to managing import competition from China. This does not mean blanket protectionism that hurts consumers and invites retaliation. But it does mean stronger enforcement against illegal imports, better use of trade remedies where dumping can be proven and support for domestic firms to upgrade quality and move into higher-value segments. At the same time, governments should press for greater reciprocity, including duty-free access for labour-intensive exports from lower-income members of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations into the Chinese market.

Finally, political incentives must change. Leaders should be rewarded for delivering productivity growth and formal employment rather than for short-term spending or populist measures. This requires a national consensus that escaping the middle-income trap requires sustained effort across administrations.

The window created by supply-chain diversification remains open but is narrowing. Countries that subordinate their economic strategies to short-term political gains will find themselves squeezed from both sides – losing the relocation benefits they had hoped for while watching their industries lose competitiveness. Those willing to make the critical reforms still have a realistic chance to turn pressure into progress. The choice is increasingly stark.

Anna Fong is a Master of Business Administration candidate at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology. Donald Low is Senior Lecturer and Professor of Practice in Public Policy at the same university.
