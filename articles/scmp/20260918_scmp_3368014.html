Why the ICC is cracking down on ‘fake news’ as Duterte trial looms

SCMP | FREE | 1024 words | 2026-09-18

As Rodrigo Duterte’s crimes-against-humanity trial looms, an international tribunal faces the challenge of managing a sprawling prosecution case around the health of an 81-year-old former president of the Philippines, on top of shielding the proceedings from a highly charged media environment.

Those pressures came into focus at an International Criminal Court (ICC) status conference on Wednesday, when Presiding Judge Joanna Korner urged prosecutors to trim their witness list and evidence ahead of the November 30 trial opening while delivering a blunt warning against lawyers using the media in ways that could fuel “fake news”.

Legal experts say such warnings are not unusual in international criminal cases, but the breadth of Korner’s remarks reflects the particular difficulty facing the court as its proceedings are scrutinised, interpreted and sometimes distorted across the Philippines’ political and social media landscape.

Duterte was arrested in the Philippines and surrendered to the ICC in The Hague in March 2025. In April this year, the ICC Pre-Trial Chamber confirmed all three counts of crimes against humanity against him – involving murder and attempted murder allegedly committed as part of his war on drugs between November 2011 and March 2019 – and committed him to trial.

He appeared in person at the pretrial conference for the first time, looking thinner but walking unassisted and without a cane. News agencies said he waved to the audience and smiled slightly but did not speak.

Blunt reminder

During the case conference, Korner bluntly told defence and prosecution lawyers: “We see no reason why counsel should feel it necessary to make any comment to the media. The appropriate forum for counsel to raise matters is in the courtroom. Trials are not conducted by the court of public opinion, but by this court during the course of the proceedings.”

She criticised in particular Duterte’s former counsel Nicholas Kaufman, who she said had made “wholly inappropriate” personal comments in at least three media interviews about decisions by the Pre-Trial Chamber and Appeals Chamber despite “a reminder to him to remember that he had a professional duty to be respectful and courteous to the chamber”.

Korner noted that “this case has attracted, not surprisingly, a substantial amount of media interest in the Philippines in particular” – in mainstream and social media – “as to have provoked suppliers of what is undoubtedly fake news and obviously fake news”.

“The first thing we wish to emphasise is the need for the media to report to the public accurately and fairly what is said in court and above all to avoid speculation,” Korner said.

Two legal experts on the ICC told This Week in Asia that Korner’s reminder was not unique to Duterte’s case, although the former president’s rise to power and continuing political influence have been closely intertwined with social media.

For British legal expert Tom Smith, who has been closely following Duterte’s case and other ICC proceedings, the judge’s warning “is not unprecedented”.

“What is striking here is the breadth and bluntness of Judge Korner’s warning, and her explicit recognition of the Philippine disinformation environment,” said Smith, an associate professor in international relations at the University of Portsmouth and academic director of the Royal Air Force College.

“This was not simply concern about an indiscreet lawyer. Kaufman’s interviews were feeding an enormous political and social media ecosystem in which incomplete or inaccurate claims about the ICC could be turned into supposed facts within hours.”

Smith stressed that the court had not imposed a formal gag order on the media, but warned of “a danger for the ICC”.

“If the lawyers stop speaking and the court does not communicate the proceedings clearly and quickly, the resulting information vacuum will be filled by the Duterte political network,” Smith said.

“The ICC cannot defeat disinformation simply by asking everyone to remain silent. It must explain its own decisions far more effectively to Filipinos.” .

The ICC had issued warnings in previous cases, said Ruben Carranza, a senior expert at the International Centre for Transitional Justice, which advises victims in cases before war crimes tribunals such as the ICC, including on reparations.

Carranza recalled that in the case of former Kenyan president Uhuru Kenyatta, his indicted co-accused were warned by ICC judges “to not issue statements to the media or to the public that would tend to incite more political violence in Kenya”.

‘Enormous’ amount of evidence

Korner also told prosecutors to “think long and hard” about trimming some 15,000 exhibits – including documents and videos – and their witness list covering 57 separate incidents.

Lead prosecutor Julian Nicholls said prosecutors had already begun cutting both, after initially estimating they would need between 175 and 200 hours just to present witnesses.

Duterte’s lead counsel Peter Haynes called the volume of evidence “enormous” and raised concerns about duplicate and incomplete records.

Legal experts said such large volumes of material were not unusual in crimes against humanity cases, where prosecutors must prove not only individual killings but also a broader attack on civilians and the accused’s connection to it.

Carranza indicated that Haynes was familiar with handling an enormous amount of evidence, as Haynes had handled the case of the convicted Congolese politician Jean-Pierre Bemba Gombo on appeal where he had to review “over 5,700 pages of evidence and [testimonies of] more than 70 witnesses”. Bemba was acquitted in 2018.

“This case is also unusually documentary because Duterte was president and the Philippine police and government generated extensive records,” Smith said. “Even the prosecution acknowledged that there is more material than in other cases its lawyers have handled.”

The defence, however, had “legitimate concerns [about] … duplicate records, individual pages being treated as separate exhibits and incomplete documents”, Smith added.

“The raw number tells us very little about the strength of the case. Sixty-two thousand disclosed items are not the same as 62,000 pieces of compelling evidence.”

Smith said the prosecution’s estimated courtroom hours also excluded cross-examination and procedural arguments, meaning the trial could run substantially longer once proceedings began.

His “cautious” estimate was: “Two to four years from the opening of the trial is more realistic, particularly if health disputes and appeals continue.”
