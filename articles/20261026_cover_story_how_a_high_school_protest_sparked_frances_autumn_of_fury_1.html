Cover Story: How a High School Protest Sparked France’s Autumn of Fury

Caixin Global | FULLTEXT | 674 words | 2026-10-26 02:58

1. On Oct. 17, masked rioters set the 1883 neo-Renaissance town hall in Limoges ablaze, collapsing its clock tower, in one of the most striking scenes of France's worst street violence in years, with car burnings and looting from Paris to Marseille and solidarity clashes in Milan and Brussels. Mayor Guillaume Guérin lamented that "the country is in chaos," while President Emmanuel Macron expressed "anger and shame," asking how French people could treat France this way.

2. The protests began in late September at Saint-Exupéry High School in Créteil, a Paris suburb, where pupils complained about teacher shortages and squalid facilities—cockroach- and rat-infested buildings, toilets without running water, leaking classrooms and collapsing ceilings. Education Ministry figures show nearly 10% of scheduled teaching hours in public secondary schools were lost in the 2024-25 academic year, about 7.5% due to funding shortages for substitute teachers.

3. The movement spread to hundreds of schools and dozens of universities, with two Marseille high schools set on fire on Oct. 1. It merged with broader opposition to the government's proposed 2027 budget, and by Oct. 17 workers, farmers, pensioners and Yellow Vest veterans had joined. The government estimated 550,000 protesters; the CGT put participation at 800,000. At least 3,000 shops were affected, 2,397 people arrested, and about 1,000 police officers and firefighters injured, including a police officer killed in Nantes and a protester who died of a heart attack.

4. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez offered his resignation, which Macron rejected, but apologized for police losing control. The unrest exposed a dilemma that has dogged Macron's presidency: how to repair strained public finances without provoking voters who see spending cuts as another blow to living standards. With Macron due to leave office in May 2027, the confrontation is strengthening the far left and far right.

5. The unrest sharpened the succession contest. Far-right National Rally leader Marine Le Pen promised to restore authority, security and sovereignty, while radical-left France Unbowed leader Jean-Luc Mélenchon blamed police tactics. An Odoxa poll put Le Pen's first-round support at 37-38% versus Mélenchon's 18-18.5%; in a hypothetical runoff she would win 68-32, though against centrist Édouard Philippe her edge narrows to 53-47.

7. Fiscal pressures are mounting: the deficit reached 5.1% of GDP in 2025, well above the EU's 3% ceiling; public debt hit 119% of GDP; and debt interest payments rose 11.2% to €64.7 billion. Ten-year government bond yields passed 5% on Oct. 21, the highest since 2002, while the economy grew only 0.8% in 2025.

9. Experts argue the crisis reflects a fraying social contract. Sciences Po's Bertrand Badie said the welfare system had become selective, leaving many groups "marginalized, neglected and humiliated," while schools and hospitals suffered neglect. Paul Smith of Nottingham cited Macron's failure to engage with unions and intermediary bodies, and the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences' Tian Dewen said the government relied on temporary fixes. An Ipsos poll found 85% believe France is in decline and 98% are dissatisfied.
