Discover India’s ‘last village’: a hub of Balti culture on the Pakistan border

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2321 words | 2026-07-31

An inky darkness blankets the road as I step out of the car. I blink to get my bearings, the silhouettes of trees slowly forming beneath a startling canopy of stars.

“Sorry to make you walk in the dark, but this is a shortcut,” says Rashidullah Khan, our companion and guide for the evening. He leads the way up through the trees, our phone torches illuminating pebbled, muddy slopes.

After 10 minutes, we reach a narrow lane flanked by stone walls punctuated with wooden doors. A sculpture of a large bird perches above one doorway, an unassuming entrance to what a sign identifies as the Yabgo Palace Turtuk. Inside the central courtyard of the wooden building, intricately carved upper balconies appear in the candlelight, plush bolster cushions and low tables clustered below: the setting for dinner with Turtuk’s king.

I am in the “last village” of northern India, a few kilometres from the Pakistan border. Living in a corner of the Ladakh desert – India’s northernmost region, known for its stark and arresting landscape – the villagers of Turtuk, Thang, Tyakshi and Chalunka consider themselves part of Baltistan, but flipped nationality overnight, after the 1971 Indo-Pakistani war. Families were split as the now Indian villages were separated from the larger Balti population on the other side of the border.

Until 2010, this sensitive area was closed to outsiders. Now, through tourism, residents are gently asserting their Balti identity, eager to share their heritage with the wider world.

Turtuk is deliciously lush, rich with apple and apricot orchards and green buckwheat fields, cut through by the icy blue Shyok River. At around 3,000 metres (9,843 feet), it sits at a relatively low elevation within Ladakh, but during the first few days anywhere in the area, the altitude makes activity strenuous.

My journey begins in Leh (around 3,500 metres/11,483 feet), Ladakh’s capital, where the air is palpably thin and, initially, a heavy head and breathlessness are common after even gentle walking. To prevent altitude sickness, visitors must acclimatise here for a couple of days before travelling onwards. Non-residents require a permit to travel beyond Leh – Inner Line Permits for Indians and Protected Area Permits for foreigners – which can be applied for in advance, or obtained through Leh travel agents, the process taking between 24 and 48 hours.

I spend my first day resting, hydrating and gazing at the purple mountains from the window of my room at The Pal Hotel. A brief stroll to Leh market reveals that the crowds weaving around and through the souvenir shops and cafes serving cakes and steaming momos – Tibetan dumplings filled with meat or vegetables – are larger than usual, in anticipation of the visiting Dalai Lama’s public address the following day.

Leh is more than a pit stop in which to, literally, catch your breath. It’s an introduction to the region’s history and culture, the Buddhist leanings visible in fluttering prayer flags and a landscape dotted with monasteries. The world’s highest peace pagoda, the white-domed Shanti Stupa, spans two circular levels filled with reliefs depicting the life of the Buddha and offers panoramic views of Leh and the surrounding mountains.

Twenty kilometres (12 miles) away, the stupas, paintings and statues of the 15th century Thiksey Monastery, one of the largest in the area, amount to a treasure trove of Buddhist art. Some visitors circle and prostrate themselves before a 15-metre-high (49-feet-high) Maitreya Buddha, a bodhisattva believed to be a future Buddha; others sit in quiet contemplation.

Juxtaposed against these peaceful symbols are stark reminders of conflict. Ladakh remains heavily militarised, having been the site of clashes between India and Pakistan or China. Leh’s Hall of Fame museum and memorial has displays on the 1999 Kargil war; Ladakhi culture; soldiers’ personal memorabilia; and the challenges of high-altitude warfare, as well as life in general.

From Leh, the drive to Turtuk winds through constantly changing landscapes – rugged peaks marbled with purple, gold and rust stripes; sandy deserts; the lush, boulder-strewn river plains sliced through by the Shyok; and the dizzying Khardung La pass, at around 5,400 metres (17,717 feet), before finally descending into the Shyok Valley.

“The Himalayan region of Baltistan, famously known as Gilgit-Baltistan, was on the Silk Route,” says Turtuk’s king, Yabgo Mohammad Khan Kacho, at dinner. “Some of the world’s highest peaks – Karakoram one and two [K1 and K2] – and prominent glaciers like Baltoro and Siachen are here [on the Pakistani side of the border].” Pakistani Baltistan is often known as “Little Tibet” due to its alpine scenery.

His title is unofficial but Kacho is a direct descendant of the Yabgo dynasty, Baltistan’s rulers for 2,000 years.

Wearing an elegant cream shuqa – a traditional men’s woollen overcoat – embroidered with flowers, and an ivory and gold turban, his soft yet clear voice floats gently through the courtyard, as he recounts Baltistan’s tumultuous history with invaders, including Genghis Khan (in the 13th century) and Alexander the Great (in the 4th century; although historians doubt either made it this far). We talk over a traditional Balti meal: buckwheat crepes with tangy yogurt and spinach dip; baleh, a buckwheat noodle, thyme and vegetable soup; and meat dumplings with a punchy chilli oil.

“We still eat many of our traditional dishes,” says Kacho, although his son says he prefers a less carbohydrate-heavy, more protein-rich diet, to better suit his gym regimen.

The 14th century wooden palace doubles as Kacho’s residence and a museum. Weapons, maps and artefacts showcase Balti history while Buddhist and Islamic art reflect the region’s mixed heritage.

Like most of his “subjects”, Kacho was separated from family in 1971.

“My sisters and cousins are in Pakistan. My riyasat [princely state] is there,” he says.

This division becomes tangible in nearby Thang, where Indian visitors (Thang is off-limits to non-Indians) can view the Line of Control between their country and Pakistan. Guide Rukiya Bano directs my gaze through binoculars, pointing out which mountains and bunkers are Indian and which are Pakistani. Her finger points to fields across the river: “That is Pakistan.” It is just 2km (1.2 miles) away yet feels much more distant for the residents of these four villages, as getting a visa to visit relatives is challenging.

Thang is home to botanist Mohammed Ali Bari, known as Goba Ali, whose colourful house overflows into a flourishing garden. Ali was five years old when he was separated from his parents.

“During the 1971 war, many feared the consequences if our village was captured. So, the women and most children went to hide a little further away. My grandfather, brother and I stayed,” says Ali, offering me grapes and dried apricots. When the war was over, the border was redrawn in haste, and those in hiding on that fateful December night were stranded on the wrong side of the new divide.

Ali speaks gratefully of the Indian Army for having raised him and his brother, a childhood with no formal education and a tent in a soldiers’ camp for a home. “They would dress me in red and take me to a spot where my parents could see me through binoculars from across the border and know that I was all right.”

In 2014, he was granted permission to visit his family in Pakistan, reuniting with them for 10 months. Photos from that trip hang in a small museum in his home and include one of Ali resting his head on his mother’s shoulder, household items and work tools in the background representing Balti culture. This separation from their larger community has made it difficult for many of the villagers to piece together their heritage. It took Rashidullah Khan seven years and several conversations with family in Pakistan to replicate the design of a traditional palace in his 12-room boutique hotel, Virsa Baltistan, in Turtuk.

“Virsa is the dream of a boy who grew up in this war-torn area, with very little money. This was intended to be more than a hotel, but a representation of Balti culture and identity,” says Khan.

The structure, with intricately carved windows and balconies, was created by 200 local woodcarvers, blacksmiths and stonemasons using riverbed mud, timber and hand-carved wood. Guest experiences include visits to traditional weavers, potters and metalworkers, and a five-course Balti tasting menu prepared by local women.

Under apricot trees, Zainab didi (“older sister”, a term used to address elder women in India) makes buckwheat crepes on a griddle while Khan plates each course: kisirnarang-thur, crepes cut into thin strips and a dollop of tangy yogurt dip placed on a leaf; praku, a thumb-pinched pasta cloaked in walnut sauce; and phading, a dessert of boiled apricots, apricot kernels and basil leaves shaped into a sticky caramelised square.

“These ladies think that my plating and portions are stingy,” laughs Khan. “I explain a tasting menu to them, but they believe food should be served generously and eaten with the hands to enjoy it.”

Finding hospitality professionals in this remote area is challenging and Khan has trained former mechanics and clerks, valuing their willingness to learn and cultural knowledge over industry experience.

“Involving the community is an integral part of Virsa,” he says.

Evenings bring music and dance to the hotel’s orchard – drummers and woodwind players accompany performers re-enacting Balti legends with swirling swords and athletic leaps.

Music plays an integral role in another Balti tradition: polo.

This free-spirited, rougher version of the game is accompanied by live instruments, the horses trained to musical cues.

Unfortunately, no game is scheduled for when I’m visiting, but while passing a pitch – sandy, uneven ground, with fading lines demarcating the goals – it’s easy to imagine how boisterous it gets when two teams of six players jostle on such a narrow strip, the first to score 10 goals being the winners. The prize is meat, sometimes a goat or sheep. Although a trophy is a recent addition, celebratory “goat parties” are still popular.

The four villages have eight teams, competing in tournaments for a few weeks starting at Navroze (the Persian New Year, in late March, which also signals the arrival of spring) and later, in July or August.

“We also hope to take the game beyond Turtuk, and represent Baltistan in national tournaments,” says Nassir Hussain, a player on Khan’s Virsa team.

Ordinarily, visitors en route to the border have stopped in Turtuk only to eat lunch at its riverside cafes. But with more guest houses, homestays and camping facilities now available, villagers are encouraging tourists to stay for longer, to fully experience what the area has to offer.

A steep but short hike to the Turtuk Monastery is rewarded with sweeping views over mountains, river, fields and a cascading cemetery.

Within the village, a 140-year-old home has been converted into the Balti Heritage Museum, with a display of utensils and traditional clothes: well-worn, rugged, functional-looking items made from animal skin and fur, for warmth; loose trousers of coarse cloth; fur-lined coats and tunics in cream, beige and rust hues. Among the fancier robes are shuqa in cream accented with bright embroidered flowers.

The house was built using traditional construction techniques – interlocked wooden and stone structures that serve as pillars, reinforced timber lacings and a half-timbering method – to protect against earthquakes and provide insulation in the extreme cold of winter. The pillars and doorframes are intricately carved with Buddhist and Bon (an ancient spiritual tradition that predates Buddhism and Islam in the region) motifs: left-facing swastikas, or yungdrung; florals; and knots of eternal loops, weaving back into each other.

Nearby is a centuries-old nangchung, meaning “cold house”, one of eight in the area. These bunkers were built in such a way that cool mountain air could circulate through gaps between the stones. Before electricity and fridges arrived in Turtuk, these served as communal stores for perishable food.

Elsewhere, the Bunker of Memories recalls the region’s history as a war zone. Used by the Ashoor family (the nearby Balti Heritage Museum is their ancestral home) during the Kargil war, the sturdy underground stone shelter displays military detritus, several pieces repurposed as utilitarian objects: jerrycans as water carriers and thaps – traditional stoves; ammunition boxes as safes; and rocket shells as butter churners.

It’s a sobering space, and a reminder of the resilience shown by Baltis in challenging conditions.

Later, while sipping on apricot juice with nutty kernels bobbing on top at The Balti Kitchen restaurant, Abdul Rashid Khan, Rashidullah’s father, recalls the dust and rubble during the 1999 war. “It was scary, and several people retreated from the village. I chose to stay home. When it was over, the calm and silence were disorienting,” says the jovial, soft-spoken man, who now runs a speciality store selling jams, nuts, dried apricots, oils and other local products.

“Before 1971, there was a smaller population, and few schools. Now there are more schools and girls are educated.”

A balancing of gender roles is also evident in the visibility of women in tourist businesses, noticeably at a cafe managed by the Women’s Empowerment and Welfare Association and at The Balti Kitchen, which is run by Khan’s sister and set on an open wooden platform amid organic flower and vegetable gardens. The menu lists specialities such as tsapon, home-baked bread toasted in white butter, onion and spices; squu, a potato, barley, pea and Himalayan herb stew; and kambo kushu, apple-flavoured cookies topped with mulberries and chocolate syrup.

Outside tourism, there is an ambition among the villagers to showcase their identity beyond Turtuk, whether through representing Baltistan in national polo tournaments or taking part in Khan’s plans to launch a Balti design label with local artisans. This aspiration represents preservation and renewal for a community whose identity has been defined by borders, and is now eager to move beyond those restrictions.
