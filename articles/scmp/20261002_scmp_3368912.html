How Hong Kong’s old businesses are adapting to new times

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2118 words | 2026-10-02

In the second of a two-part series on Hong Kong’s economic transformation, Ambrose Li and Wynna Wong profile three established enterprises that have rejuvenated their brands and reinvented their businesses. Read part one here.

Late into the night, Hong Kong football fans in their twenties, thirties and forties sat in Lin Heung Lau restaurant on Wellington Street watching World Cup matches over dim sum rather than pints at a sports bar.

The historic restaurant, traditionally associated with elderly morning-tea regulars, extended its operating hours during the World Cup in the summer, drawing on the expertise of chairman Rocky Wong Hei-yan, a nightlife industry veteran behind once-bustling Lan Kwai Fong venues such as Club No 9, Club Beijing and Magnum.

“On that occasion, we were not calculating money only,” he said. “We were looking at brand-building and opening another channel through which we could find customers.”

The century-old Lin Heung Lau brand has been rejuvenated with new marketing and business strategies since 2024, when its management was revamped amid a decline in the tradition of people going out for dim sum.

The restaurant has hosted a series of “dim sum raves”, taking social media by storm with its tea-infused cocktails and electronic music paired with steaming towers of pork buns and shrimp dumplings.

Lin Heung Lau’s football and rave nights illustrate one solution to a question confronting established Hong Kong businesses: how can they follow changing customer habits and find new sources of revenue without discarding the identity that made them valuable?

Interviews with family-business scholars and companies ranging from a traditional dried-seafood retailer to a long-established transport operator suggest that transformation does not follow a single formula.

It can involve creating new experiences, rebuilding unglamorous back-end systems, adopting artificial intelligence (AI) or allowing a younger generation to experiment.

Winnie Peng Qian, director of the Roger King Centre for Asian Family Business and Family Office at Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, cautioned against assuming that every established company needed to pursue rapid or radical innovation.

“People always think you need to innovate, need to react fast,” she said.

“But you see a lot of century-old family businesses in Japan. They’re very small, fewer than 10 people, just a store doing the same thing.”

Such companies could instead innovate gradually, she said, while adapting their traditions to remain relevant to the environment they faced.

Nightlife meets yum cha

Wong saw an opportunity in Lin Heung Lau when it closed during the Covid-19 pandemic, believing its brand had untapped potential despite the difficult environment facing Hong Kong’s catering industry.

Wong, the restaurant’s chairman and primary investor, was granted the perpetual right to use the Lin Heung Lau brand in Hong Kong rather than ownership of the underlying intellectual property.

He had known the restaurant for more than 25 years, having regularly visited for morning tea after finishing work at his clubs.

“I was very familiar with the brand and knew its strengths and weaknesses,” he said.

“It was not simply a matter of me taking over and benefiting from a powerful brand. It had already closed. I was setting out with it again and that was a major challenge.”

Despite the obvious differences between running nightclubs and operating a traditional Chinese dim sum restaurant frequented in the morning, Wong said both businesses ultimately depended on the same thing: management.

“If you manage people properly and they naturally follow the organisation’s instructions, you can succeed in even selling salted crispy peanuts,” he said.

“But if management is poor and everything becomes too personalised, it will destroy the entire management system.”

Since taking over the operation, Wong has sought to preserve Lin Heung Lau’s traditional yum cha atmosphere and handmade dim sum while reaching beyond the predominantly elderly clientele historically associated with such restaurants.

“We are not taking a single direction and targeting only the silver-haired market,” he said.

“We are adding new elements to attract different customers – middle-aged people, young people and even children.”

Wong did not provide figures for the investment, sales or footfall generated by the football screenings but said they were profitable and attracted a largely younger crowd.

He argued that their greater value was promotional, estimating that the equivalent publicity would have cost millions of dollars to reproduce through conventional advertising.

Lin Heung Lau had opened or substantially redeveloped five outlets in just over two years, Wong said.

Its latest restaurant occupies about 13,000 sq ft at the airport, while other locations include Mong Kok and Tsim Sha Tsui.

About 95 per cent of the menu is shared among its restaurants, but each has distinctive dishes or design features.

Wong said operators could no longer simply open their doors and assume customers would arrive.

“You have to catch the trend,” he said. “A business has to change. If it does not change, customers will not come.”

More affordable ready-to-eat abalone

At On Kee Dry Seafood, founded in the 1930s, transformation has been less visible to customers but no less extensive.

The family business has made traditionally expensive dried gourmet seafood such as abalone more affordable by creating mouth-sized snacks that proved popular with younger shoppers at the city’s signature food fair.

As preparing and cooking items like abalone, sea cucumber and duck’s feet can take a couple of days, the group has diversified into ready-to-eat offerings such as frozen and pre-cooked seafood, as well as ready-to-consume soups and desserts.

“Our ready-to-eat products have proved to be very popular because they cater to the busy lives of Hongkongers who have a preference for convenience,” said Henry Poon, 27, the fourth generation of the family business.

“About two years ago, we developed an individually wrapped abalone snack that could be consumed right away, designed for when people are feeling peckish.”

The company was still relying heavily on handwritten notes and fax machines as recently as five years ago.

Its six retail stores also handled about 1,000 online orders a day, forcing employees to pack e-commerce purchases on crowded shop floors while serving customers.

“After all, we are a very traditional company,” Poon said.

“Even five years ago, we weren’t a particularly digital company, relying heavily on handwritten notes and using fax.”

Over the past two years, Poon has led a digital transformation, describing it as a “timely catch-up” with the market amid the growing popularity of e-commerce.

An enterprise resource planning system consolidates orders placed through On Kee’s website and third-party platforms, streamlining stocking, packing and delivery.

The company can process about 1,800 online orders a day, an 80 per cent increase from before.

On Kee also introduced a point-of-sale system capable of handling more complex promotions, such as a 10 per cent discount on a second item and 20 per cent off a third.

AI has reduced the time needed to calculate how much stock should be taken to exhibitions – important events for the business – from several days to minutes.

“We’d typically look at the trend from the past three to four times we took part, as well as our previous orders,” Poon said.

“The press of a button generates you a very good forecast table.”

The transition remains unfinished. Poon estimated that it could take another three to four years for all the new systems to operate smoothly.

‘Standing still means falling behind’

At Kwoon Chung Bus Holdings, one of Hong Kong’s largest franchised and non-franchised bus operators, the changes in its business and strategies have been sweeping.

Founded in 1948 as a car-cleaning service by the late entrepreneur Wong Kwoon-chung, the firm, which earned 82 per cent more year on year in net profit in 2025-26, at HK$268.4 million, is aiming high – literally.

It made a breakthrough in the 1990s, taking over bus operations on Lantau Island and coach companies that catered to hotel and tour group operators.

A landmark change took place in 2023, when third-generation family members Timothy and James Wong pushed through a new venture, KC Smart Mobility, by investing in electric vertical take-off and landing aircraft (eVTOL), alongside autonomous vehicles, drones and ride-hailing services.

In August, the company worked with mainland Chinese autonomous aerial vehicle manufacturer EHang and tech hub Cyberport to bring an eVTOL to Hong Kong, culminating in the city’s first successful demonstration flight of an unmanned heavy-duty aircraft.

“These are medium- to long-term investments that will not be profitable soon,” said Newton Ng, business development director of KC Smart Mobility.

“Standing still means falling behind. If we remain the same as we are today, there will not be expansion.”

The company plans to buy 30 eVTOLs from EHang for more than HK$70 million, although the aircraft remain at the testing stage. Charging can take more than an hour for a flight lasting about 20 minutes, while Hong Kong does not yet have regulations for commercial operations.

Ng said acting early could help the company capture market share.

“If government policies are also directed towards newer modes of transport, the earlier we act, the sooner we can possibly capture a larger market share,” he added.

Kwoon Chung has already encountered changing customer behaviour in its traditional passenger-transport operation.

Its existing limousine customers previously had to telephone the company to reserve vehicles, but increasingly wanted to arrange their journeys online.

The operator responded by partnering with ride-hailing company Uber to launch Uber Elite, a premium service combining Kwoon Chung’s vehicles, full-time chauffeurs and local operating experience with the US-based firm’s widely used booking platform and customer reach.

The service, Uber’s first of its kind outside the United States, offers pre-booked rides in Mercedes-Benz vehicles for groups of up to six passengers, targeting C-suite executives and business travellers.

Customers can book between two hours and 90 days in advance. Ng said demand during the first month was strong enough that the company could not always accommodate requests made at shorter notice.

“It’s a changing consumption pattern,” he said. “If we do not develop a model that caters to the trend, some customers will of course drift away over time.

“We’ll have to follow the habits of the younger generation and have a presence where they book their commutes.”

Space for the next generation

Kwoon Chung’s decision to place its experimental businesses in a subsidiary headed by third-generation members reflects one way family companies can pursue transformation without immediately disrupting their core operations.

Jeremy Cheng Chi-yeung, an adjunct associate professor at the Chinese University of Hong Kong’s business school, said such companies could initially be slower to adopt the latest trends but often committed substantial resources once they decided to proceed.

One model, he said, was to allow the rising generation to establish a smaller agile unit, the kind created by Kwoon Chung.

“The rising generation will build their own team, a small unit, which is meant to be agile, and they can lead faster changes,” Cheng said.

“Once they are successful with that particular experiment, they tend to cross-fertilise that in different units in the business itself.”

Victoria Wang, a professor at the same institution, said slower adoption did not necessarily mean a family business lacked the ability to survive.

“They change slowly to go faster,” she said. “Tradition is legacy, purpose and identity. They very much want to make sure their identity continues.”

Transformation, however, does not always require radical innovation.

“If a tradition remains relevant, it is valuable,” Peng of Hong Kong University of Science and Technology said. “But if the family is static, very traditional and refuses to change, of course it cannot survive in the long term.”

For Lin Heung Lau, this has meant continuing to serve handmade dim sum while challenging assumptions about who should eat it and when.

At On Kee, the dried seafood remains, but the handwritten orders and fax machines are disappearing.

For Kwoon Chung, the coaches continue operating while the next generation explores modes of transport extending from smartphones to the skies.

Peng said the family businesses that endured tended to share two deeper characteristics – a long-term outlook and a sense of responsibility towards society.

Their experience of surviving economic and political upheavals helped them look beyond immediate difficulties, while a broader purpose gave future generations a reason to continue beyond preserving their own wealth.

“Sometimes you need to stick to who you are and your family values,” Peng said. “The first thing that stands out is a long-term perspective. The other is altruism. You need to care about society.”
