Singapore is open to chemical castration, but what are ethical, health concerns?

SCMP | FREE | 1028 words | 2026-09-15

Singapore’s openness to chemical castration for sex offenders has cast light on the ethical questions surrounding the treatment, with the discussion centred on whether it would fill any gap left by existing legislation.

Experts point to how safeguards would be set regarding who receives the treatment, the duration of the process and if resulting medical consequences would be proportional to the offence.

The debate comes after Senior Minister and Home Affairs Minister K. Shanmugam’s written parliamentary reply last week, where he said Singapore was open to considering chemical castration if there was clear evidence that the measure was effective in lowering crime rates.

But Shanmugam stressed that evidence around the method remained inconclusive, pointing out that the country already had “heavy penalties” in place for such crimes.

Chemical castration, also referred to as pharmacological anti-androgen treatments, involves the administration of drugs to reduce libido, compulsive sexual fantasies and capacity of sexual arousal, and is usually reversible.

Singapore recently strengthened its framework for dealing with high-risk sexual and violent offenders.

Rapists can be jailed up to 20 years, fined and caned. If the victim is aged under 14, the offender will be imprisoned for at least eight years and face a minimum of 12 strokes of the cane. Molestation is punishable with a prison term of up to three years, a fine and caning.

The Sentence for Enhanced Public Protection, which took effect on July 31, allows courts to impose minimum detention periods of between five and 20 years on serious violent or sexual offenders assessed as posing a substantial risk of reoffending. Before release, offenders undergo an updated psychiatric assessment and can remain detained if they are still considered a public threat.

Sexual drive and reoffending

The existing framework already fulfilled the objectives of punishment and keeping convicted sex offenders away from society, said Chen Siyuan, a law professor at the Singapore Management University.

Any push for chemical castration would then have to show additional benefits delivered, particularly for those deemed most likely to reoffend.

“If the objective is to target the likeliest reoffenders, then one still has to balance it against proportionality, such as whether the punishment has undue permanent effects,” Chen said.

He called for stronger medical evidence and research into whether the treatment actually served that purpose.

Reported sexual offences are on the rise in Singapore – police figures showed 261 rape cases in the first half of this year, compared with 230 during the same period last year. Molestation cases rose to 762 from 739.

But experts cautioned against assuming that suppressing sexual drive would necessarily stem reoffending.

Mervyn Cheong, an associate law professor from the National University of Singapore, said the penalty had to be balanced carefully with the existing framework. “It raises real operational and policy questions of when it should be deployed instead of, or in addition to, present custodial measures,” he said.

“Chemical castration does not map cleanly onto the four traditional pillars of deterrence, retribution, prevention and rehabilitation.”

Cheong described the treatment as “controversial” due to its punitive nature as it continued to burden offenders physiologically even after release from prison.

Medical professionals have similarly warned that although the effects of anti-androgen treatment are generally reversible, they can be substantial. Studies have linked such treatments to conditions including osteoporosis, depression and cardiovascular issues.

Tan Kok Kuan, medical director at Dr Tan Medical Centre and a specialist in men’s health, said recovery after treatment could also depend on an offender’s age and how long the drugs had been administered.

“If the patient is of advanced age when the treatment is stopped, fertility or bone strength may not return to 100 per cent,” he said.

Cheong added that policymakers would also have to consider less easily measurable consequences, including whether compulsory treatment could generate resentment or grievance among offenders and undermine rehabilitation.

For criminal lawyer Zachary Lim of IRB Law, these concerns mean any move to implement the treatment should have a far narrower threshold than simply applying chemical castration to broad categories of sexual offenders.

If introduced, he said, its use should be discretionary, preceded by medical assessment and confined to the most serious cases.

“A statutory threshold drawn any wider would be difficult to defend,” Lim said.

Other Asian countries have moved to implement the penalty, although experts note that studies on its effectiveness have been limited.

South Korea in 2011 approved its use on convicted paedophiles for a maximum period of 15 years, and later included sex offenders such as attempted rapists and offenders whose sexual crimes led to injury or the death of minors.

In 2020, Indonesia’s then president Joko Widodo signed a regulation that required offenders who inflict or threaten sexual violence against those under the age of 18 to undergo chemical castration for a maximum of two years.

In 2023, Thailand provided the treatment as an option to sex offenders in return for an early release from prison. Offenders are first evaluated by a psychiatrist and an internal medical specialist.

Human rights activists have criticised such laws for being improperly coercive, voicing concerns that inmates may be forced to choose between staying in prison and undergoing treatment. Medical professionals have also expressed concerns about side effects, such as osteoporosis, and the ability of offenders to make informed decisions.

US-based advocacy group Human Rights Watch has denounced chemical castration as a “cruel and degrading form” of corporal punishment.

Public attitudes in Singapore, meanwhile, are considerably less hesitant.

Singaporean content creator Athalie Yi, 33, said she believed medically supervised treatment should be considered if it lowered the danger posed by repeat offenders.

“I think it’s fair to also consider whether a medically supervised treatment could help reduce the risk of reoffending, especially once someone returns to society,” she said.

“The comfort and consideration afforded to convicted sexual offenders should never carry more weight than the safety and assurance we owe victims and potential victims.”

But Lim acknowledges the risk of the treatment’s wider appeal to society overtaking evidence of what it can actually achieve.

“A measure of this kind carries a symbolic weight well beyond its practical reach, and public expectations of what it can deliver should be managed carefully from the outset.”
