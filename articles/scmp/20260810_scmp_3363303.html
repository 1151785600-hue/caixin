Climate crisis leaves Malaysia with hot-headed youth

SCMP | FREE | 1029 words | 2026-08-10

Young Malaysians are reporting worsening illnesses, heat-related health problems and disruptions to basic services as climate change intensifies, raising questions over whether the country’s adaptation policies can protect future generations.

Unicef Malaysia’s National Youth Climate Change Survey 2025, released in July, found that climate change was no longer only an environmental challenge but an immediate public health and economic issue.

The survey gathered responses from 1,420 young people aged 15 to 30 across Malaysia between June and October 2025.

Experts warned that policymakers must invest not only in reducing emissions but also in adapting to more frequent heatwaves, floods and other climate-related shocks, or risk deeper disruption to children’s health, education and Malaysia’s future workforce.

Unicef said the survey’s findings showed climate change was affecting young people’s health and well-being, with respondents reporting significant concerns: one in four, or 25.9 per cent, said existing illnesses had worsened, while 19.7 per cent reported skin conditions, 18 per cent cited heat-related illnesses and 14.3 per cent respiratory problems.

More than half said climate-related disasters had affected their access to essential services, including information, education and safe spaces.

Dr Jemilah Mahmood, executive director of the Sunway Centre for Planetary Health, said the Unicef findings showed the issue had evolved from an environmental concern into a public health challenge.

“We are already seeing the classic climate and health picture – direct injury, infectious disease, exacerbation of chronic conditions and psychological burden, all occurring simultaneously in a population aged 15 to 30, at what should be their healthiest years,” she said.

Experts say the findings underscore Malaysia’s need to emphasise adaptation plans, arguing that climate resilience should act as an investment in the country’s future workforce.

“It is no longer an either-or conversation. Malaysia must continue reducing emissions while significantly strengthening adaptation,” said Renard Siew, climate adviser at the Malaysia-based Centre for Governance and Political Studies.

Siew said children and young people were among the most vulnerable to climate change, and the biggest gap remained implementation for adaptation across all sectors.

The cost of inaction would exceed the costs of adaptation, he said, adding that more frequent heat stress, disease outbreaks and climate-related disruptions would affect productivity, increase healthcare expenditure and widen social inequalities.

“Over time, this would weaken our workforce competitiveness and economic resilience,” he said.

Public health experts warn that young children are among the most vulnerable to extreme heat because their bodies regulate temperatures less effectively, leading them to become dehydrated more quickly than adults.

“Their body’s heat-coping mechanism is more easily overwhelmed in these situations, such as getting dehydrated faster and the body’s cooling mechanism being overwhelmed more easily,” Dr Venu Gopalan, a Kuala Lumpur-based private practitioner, told This Week in Asia.

Immediate health risks of extreme heat, he said, included heat cramps, fainting, heat exhaustion and heatstrokes that could even cause death.

Gopalan warned that in the long term, repeated extreme heat exposure could lead to deteriorating kidney function and worsening heart and lung diseases, while increasing the risk of stroke caused by dehydration and electrolyte imbalances.

While Malaysia had big ambitions, climate, health and education often operated independently, Siew said.

“We need climate risk assessments embedded in schools, stronger heat-health action plans, better air quality management, and greater investment in climate-resilient public infrastructure where young people learn and live,” he said.

The Malaysian Meteorological Department has repeatedly issued hot weather alerts this year, as temperatures hit between 35 degrees Celsius (95 degrees Fahrenheit) and 40 degrees between March and May in the northern states and some parts of Sabah.

On Monday, data from the department showed that Kuala Krai in Kelantan registered the country’s highest temperature, at 35.7 degrees, followed by Temerloh in Pahang at 35.3 degrees.

The heat was not without consequences, as the Ministry of Health recorded a total of 73 heat-related illnesses from January to July this year.

According to national news agency Bernama, Public Health Medicine Specialist Dr Hazlina Yahaya said the cases involved 59 reports of heat exhaustion, six exertional heatstrokes, four heatstrokes, two cases of post-partum hyperpyrexia (high fever following childbirth), and two heat cramps.

Four deaths have been reported: two adults and two children aged two and four.

Progress but with gaps

In a statement on July 31, the World Meteorological Organization warned that the extreme heat phenomenon El Nino would continue to intensify steadily, dominating global climate patterns between August and October this year.

The agency said this would increase the likelihood of above-normal temperatures, driving significant shifts in rainfall patterns. “The developing El Nino is expected to shape weather patterns across many parts of the world in the months ahead.”

Gopalan said Malaysia’s current public health measures to address heat-related risks were inadequate, arguing that warnings alone were insufficient, especially for those who could not avoid prolonged exposure, such as outdoor workers, the homeless, and students.

“Early signs of heat stress should be recognised early by the community to enable rapid preventive measures to be taken,” he said. “Simply telling people to stay out of the sun is not practical for large segments of the community.”

Jemilah said while Malaysia had taken important steps – including identifying public health as a priority sector under the National Adaptation Plan, introducing heatwave health action plans and advancing climate legislation – implementation was still far behind.

According to Jemilah, the biggest challenge is ensuring national climate ambitions are translated and coordinated across health, education and local government, particularly as more than half of the young people affected by climate-related disasters reported lacking access to at least one essential service.

“The health aspect is lagging behind the exposure,” Jemilah said.

Siew warned that such gaps reflected a broader weakness in Malaysia’s climate adaptation effort where health, education and environmental policies were still often developed in silos despite their overlapping risks from extreme weather.

Climate adaptation, he added, should not be confined to environmental ministries.

“Even if we achieve our climate targets, the impacts of climate change are already here because children and young people are among the most vulnerable,” he said. “Investing in climate resilience today is ultimately an investment in our future human capital.”
