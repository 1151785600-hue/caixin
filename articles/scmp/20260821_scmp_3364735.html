Beijing calls US tariff report ‘false narrative’ as named hub Mexico plans new China curbs

SCMP | FREE | 1018 words | 2026-08-21

China rejected a White House report on Thursday that accused its exporters of routing goods through Mexico and other countries to avoid US tariffs, hours after word emerged that Mexico was preparing to restrict Chinese products further.

He Yadong, a spokesman for China’s Ministry of Commerce, told a regular press briefing in Beijing that the report “ignored facts and distorted reality by treating normal international trade and investment as fraud”.

He called it a “typical exercise in unilateralism and protectionism” intended to “suppress and block Chinese products”, and accused Washington of “blaming external factors for its own economic problems”.

“The high differentiated tariffs imposed by the United States are the real cause threatening the security of global supply chains,” He said.

The spokesman demanded an immediate end to what he called irresponsible accusations and said China would keep developing commercial cooperation with all parties on the basis of equality and mutual benefit.

The report puts the annual value of the trade anywhere between US$40 billion and US$303 billion.

Its estimates of 450,000 American jobs and up to US$150 billion in lost output are presented as model-based illustrations, although the text concedes that the shift in US import sourcing after 2018 does not prove all displaced Chinese trade was illegally diverted.

Beijing’s protest comes as the country the report names as the biggest conduit has said nothing in its own defence, while the largest foreign investors in Mexico press the government to act on the accusation rather than dispute it.

The Office of Trade and Manufacturing Policy, the White House unit run by trade adviser Peter Navarro, released the 25-page document a week ago under the title “The Great Transshipment Scam”.

It named more than 40 countries it said carried elevated risk of illegal transshipment, the practice of moving goods through an intermediate country so they enter the US under a different origin and a lower duty.

Mexico sits in the top tier of risk, alongside Canada, the European Union, India, Israel, Japan, South Korea and Taiwan, a group where the report says the risk is embedded within broad and otherwise legitimate trade flows.

Chinese exporters conceal the true origin of their goods by carrying out sewing, labelling, packaging or inspection work in third countries, the document alleges, naming Mexico, Canada and several in Southeast Asia among the places used to benefit from more favourable terms with the US.

Mexico is ranked among three leading hubs worldwide, along with India and Vietnam, through which the US Commerce Department traced a combined US$67 billion in Chinese goods into the American market last year.

The publication added to friction over semiconductors, artificial intelligence and rare earths ahead of Chinese President Xi Jinping’s planned state visit to Washington on September 24.

The trip will be the first meeting between Xi and US President Donald Trump on American soil since Trump returned to office.

Mexico under pressure to widen tariffs on Chinese goods

Meanwhile, Mexico plans to restrict selected Chinese goods and raise duties already in place, Bloomberg reported on Thursday.

Mexican officials have discussed the idea with Washington and have yet to settle on how far to go, two people familiar with the talks told the South China Morning Post, speaking on condition of anonymity because they were not authorised to discuss the matter publicly.

Neither knew whether the measure would come by decree or through Congress.

Steelmakers, textile and apparel producers and heavy vehicle manufacturers have pushed for the measures, arguing that dumping is damaging domestic output and employment.

Washington has asked Mexico City to adopt tariffs mirroring those it applies to Chinese steel and aluminium under Section 232, with the aim of building a common tariff policy for North America.

The request comes as the review of the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA) is under way, after Washington declined an automatic extension.

Mexico already taxes many of those goods under a package Congress approved in December.

The measure took effect on January 1 and applies duties of between 5 and 50 per cent to 1,463 products from countries without trade agreements with Mexico, among them China, India, South Korea, Indonesia, Brazil and Turkey.

Seventeen sectors are covered, including textiles, footwear, steel, aluminium, plastics, cars, auto parts and household appliances.

Economy Secretary Marcelo Ebrard said at the time that the package was designed to protect 350,000 Mexican jobs and would add about 0.2 per cent to inflation.

President Claudia Sheinbaum framed the measure as country-neutral when it was approved. The duties applied to states with which Mexico had no trade agreement and are not aimed at restricting trade between nations, she said. Ebrard said Mexico was not taxing goods because of where they were made.

A Section 232 mirror would name China in the text of the measure and set the US rate as its reference point, ending that neutrality.

Beijing responded to the December package by urging Mexico to correct what it called “erroneous practices of unilateralism and protectionism” as soon as possible, and said an investigation it opened in September 2025 into trade and investment barriers in Mexico remained under way with no conclusion announced.

American businesses operating in Mexico have urged the government to move first rather than contest the accusation.

The American Society of Mexico, a group representing US companies and citizens in the country whose honorary president is the US ambassador, said on Wednesday that Mexico should raise traceability and demonstrate with data where value is actually created.

Larry Rubin, who leads the organisation, said the main risk was not individual cases of triangulation but the absence of verification mechanisms robust enough to stop suspicion spreading to entire manufacturing sectors.

“Mexico has to reach the USMCA review with clean hands and open data,” Rubin said.

Traceability, information-sharing and origin verification mechanisms for sensitive sectors should be built with the US and Canada, the group said, without turning them into obstacles for legitimate supply chains.

It also argued that Mexican export growth could not be explained by an equivalent rise in goods arriving from China.

Mexico’s Economy Ministry and the Chinese embassy in Mexico City did not respond to requests for comment.
