Beyblade battles drive up sales at Hong Kong malls, but how long will the trend last?

SCMP | FREE | 1157 words | 2026-08-22

The sharp clatter of spinning metal tops, cheers, sighs of frustration and shouts of “Three, two, one … Go shoot!” echo across a shopping centre in Hong Kong’s New Territories on a weekday.

Dish-shaped plastic battle arenas known as “Beystadiums” have been set up not only in Tuen Mun Town Plaza, but also across the mall in different shops, where some players challenge shop staff to collect stamps for shopping vouchers.

The spinning toy popularised by Japanese anime in the early 2000s made a comeback in Hong Kong and across Asia early this year, with local fans flocking to public arenas to battle against other players and scrambling to buy hot models and rare parts.

Spotting the economic potential behind the hobby, local shopping centre operators across Hong Kong are converting public spaces into free battle zones to drive up foot traffic and sales and retain visitors.

The Tuen Mun Town Plaza even went a step further by scaling up the concept into a mall-wide strategy ahead of a major Beyblade tournament in August, encouraging shop operators to take part in the game and offer discounts to shoppers who present their tops.

“The strategy is about how a tenant can engage with customers through a shared interest, instead of directly selling to them,” said Bella Chhoa Peck-lim, chief commercial officer of Sino Group, which owns the shopping centre.

“It’s a soft-selling approach that allows staff and shoppers to start a conversation, drawing people into the store before making a sales pitch.”

What appeared to be a desperate measure by Hong Kong’s retail and catering sectors – still struggling with the trend of residents heading to mainland China for cheaper shopping and dining options – turned into an initial success.

Chhoa said the shopping centre had witnessed a “noticeable” 20 per cent surge in holiday foot traffic at the shopping centre after the promotion, as well as a 10 to 15 per cent rise in weekday attendance.

All in the game

At Hanamaruken, a ramen shop inside the Tuen Mun complex, co-founder Jelly Tong said her team upgraded the basic Beyblade provided by the landlord to a high-performance model to better compete with visiting players as they recognised the game’s potential to drive up business.

“Words then spread in their Beyblade chat groups that our store had an exceptionally strong set-up, and some kids ended up bringing six or seven friends over to challenge us,” Tong said. “Afterwards, they stayed here to eat and discuss our custom gear.”

The commercial benefits have extended beyond restaurants and toy retailers to high-end luxury tenants such as Luk Fook Jewellery. A regional manager said that setting up an in-store battle station helped break down traditional stereotypes surrounding luxury retail.

“We saw many new faces coming in after matching with our staff to browse our products and some actually made transactions,” said Patrick Ng Pui-kuen, the brand’s New Territories West district manager, who oversees the branches in Tuen Mun.

Among dozens of enthusiasts gathered at the shopping complex were 24-year-old warehouse worker Liu Chun-yin and his friend.

“Sometimes I show my spinning top at the nearby ice cream shop here to get special offers, or battle against the staff at the tea shop to win a discount,” Liu said.

His friend, a recent Beyblade fan, recalled a visit where he stayed at the venue from 2pm until 9pm, spending seven hours straight battling fellow players and purchasing food and drinks in between matches.

Over in D-Park in Tsuen Wan, dedicated free-play zones equipped with rows of desks have also drawn parents with young children alongside seasoned adult hobbyists.

Andy Yip, a 34-year-old civil servant from Kowloon East, made a dedicated trip to Tsuen Wan with his 11-year-old son and other family members, knowing there would be players at the venue.

“Before my son picked up the hobby, we spent most of our weekends in mainland China, but now we stay more in Hong Kong to accompany him, as he wants to play Beyblade with others,” he said.

Yip added that these outings were typically paired with dining and retail shopping, noting that he typically spent HK$1,000 (US$128) on dinner after his son played at D-Park, while their family members browsed at nearby shops during matches.

At Pioneer Centre in Prince Edward, a similar trend has recently unfolded, with management introducing a few Beyblade stadiums to attract a steady stream of parents and children, and launching a spend-to-draw promotion that offered exclusive models of the tops as prizes.

Eric Cheng, senior leasing manager at the mall, said the set-up and promotion in August had driven a 5 to 10 per cent increase in foot traffic compared with the same period last year, while sales at electronic goods and food and beverage outlets had jumped by 15 and 20 per cent, respectively.

“Compared with other intellectual property photo spots or traditional pop-ups, Beyblade’s interactive nature brings us much higher foot traffic,” Cheng said.

“Visitors at photo spots often leave after taking pictures, while playing Beyblade at the mall gets them to stay longer.”

Cheng said that longer stays could translate to higher spending as shoppers sought food and beverage options in between battles.

Only a short-term cure?

For all its resurgent popularity, the Beyblade trend offered only short-term benefits and was a temporary solution rather than a cure-all for the city’s broader retail sector challenges, according to one economist.

Professor Terence Chong Tai-leung, executive director of the Chinese University of Hong Kong’s Lau Chor Tak Institute of Global Economics and Finance, said shopping centres were ideal venues for Beyblade matches, which required space, and operators could set up play zones at a low cost.

“On a micro level, it drives temporary F&B spending as visitors have a reason to come,” Chong said, noting that overall retail has still failed to show significant growth over the past year.

But he stressed that such initiatives were insufficient to counter broader macroeconomic headwinds or the trend of residents spending across the border, pointing out that price remained the most significant factor driving consumer behaviour.

To stay relevant in the long run, Chong suggested that local shopping centres rethink their structural function, shifting from traditional sales hubs towards specialised themed venues or physical showrooms.

“Most successful malls in Hong Kong have distinct themes, and if you want to attract people, you have to concentrate,” he said. “For example, becoming the definitive hub for sneakers so that people will visit whenever they want to buy sneakers.”

Chong also suggested shopping centres evolve into showrooms where customers preview products before purchasing them online, rather than relying solely on traditional storefront sales.

“Due to structural shifts in shopping habits, consumer needs that used to require a mall visit can now be easily met through various online platforms.”
