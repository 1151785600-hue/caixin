China banned ivory, but Vietnamese gangs keep the trade alive

SCMP | FREE | 1068 words | 2026-08-22

Three months after leaving an inland port far up the Congo River, a consignment of timber arrived in Vietnam this summer concealing a secret it would take customs officials three weeks to uncover: nearly 1.2 tonnes of elephant ivory, smuggled halfway across the world.

Tracking data reviewed by This Week in Asia shows the shipment travelled some 9,000 nautical miles (16,700km) – with stops in Sri Lanka and Singapore – before reaching Quy Nhon, a port on Vietnam’s central coast.

Custom officers were tipped off to the contraband, sealed inside hidden compartments with wax, by port scanners that flagged hollow cavities in the timber.

On August 6, they drilled into the hollowed-out logs and found 221 pieces of sawn tusk, most likely destined for China and potentially worth millions of US dollars.

Port authorities called it the largest seizure on record for Quy Nhon, yet it was far from the first time smuggled ivory was uncovered at a Vietnamese port.

Between 2009 and 2018 alone, some 56 tonnes of ivory were seized in Vietnam, according to international NGO the Environmental Investigation Agency (EIA), with a further 20 tonnes linked to the country seized elsewhere.

CITES, the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora, bans the trade in elephant ivory, requiring permits for the sale and transport of old artefacts.

After decades of being decimated by poaching, some of Africa’s elephant populations are now stabilising, largely thanks to China’s ban on elephant ivory sales. Imposed at the end of 2017, the ban slashed demand in what was the world’s largest market.

China’s domestic ivory ban, which leaves a loophole for people to keep possession of registered tusks highly prized in ornate carvings, followed its ban on the domestic rhino horn trade in 1993.

Prices for both ivory and rhino horn have tumbled since, and Africa’s elephants, decimated by poaching that cut numbers from around 1.3 million in 1979 to roughly 415,000 by 2015, have begun a tentative recovery, with conservationists now putting the total population at 450,000-490,000.

Compared to a decade ago, when “we had industrial-scale poaching and smuggling, we have made significant inroads”, said John Scanlon, CEO of the Elephant Protection Initiative, a pan-African alliance aimed at ending the ivory trade.

He credits a coordinated push by source, transit and destination countries, and praises China for crushing ivory stockpiles rather than merely banning their sale. But he is careful not to declare victory yet.

“That doesn’t mean to say [poaching] has stopped, or it cannot, in some unpredictable way increase again,” he told This Week in Asia. “You can’t rest on your laurels.”

The Hydra network

Demand for ivory has fallen in China since the ban, but black-market smugglers still carry out their illegal trade through Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia, where the newly wealthy prize elephant tusks as a marker of status.

Vietnamese syndicates now sit among the main players in a trade estimated to be worth hundreds of millions of US dollars a year.

In 2023, Nigerian courts jailed three Vietnamese nationals – Phan Viet Chi, Phan Hong Quan and Duong Van Thang – for six years apiece after convicting them of running the African end of a smuggling network nicknamed “Hydra”.

Hydra was not new to law enforcement. Five years earlier, Thai police had already moved against the Bach brothers, dual Vietnamese-Thai nationals linked to the network, arresting a suspected kingpin and even a wildlife-crime inspector at a Bangkok airport.

One of the brothers, Boonchai Bach, was detained in 2018 for allegedly smuggling 14 rhino horns worth around US$1 million from Africa to Thailand, but the case against him collapsed after a witness recanted.

An NGO later lamented that “one of the biggest wildlife crooks ever caught in history” had been let go. The Thai Supreme Court did sentence Bach to five years in prison in 2022, but he remains a fugitive.

At the market’s peak, a kilogram of rhino horn fetched around US$60,000 to US$65,000 – more than the price of cocaine and roughly on par with gold.

Impunity, still

This month’s seizure in Quy Nhon showed that “many of the other faces behind it [the ivory trade] still operate with impunity”, said Linh Nguyen, a senior wildlife campaigner with the EIA.

“Vietnamese and Chinese-led networks are still working across Africa and there is a sense that they are moving between southern Africa to Kenya, Tanzania and west,” she said, following the herds – and gaps in enforcement – wherever they lead.

Vietnam has made strides to shake off its unwanted reputation as a smuggling hub. Sentences for wildlife crime now run as high as 15 years behind bars and the EIA reports that both arrest and imprisonment rates have climbed.

“Vietnam has made a lot of efforts, but it can’t solve this problem alone,” Linh said, citing the circuitous route smugglers take as evidence of a global supply chain. “The government needs to engage with countries throughout the chain.”

The pressure has not let up elsewhere in the region, either. On August 16, authorities in Laos’ Bolikhamsai province, bordering Vietnam, seized 1.75 tonnes of tusks tied to a trafficking network and arrested 11 foreign nationals, the Laotian Times reported. Their nationalities were not disclosed.

CSI Savannah

Scientific advances have made it harder for wildlife traffickers to prosper. Forensic testing and DNA analysis can now trace a seized tusk back to the region that it came from, while intelligence sharing between customs agencies helps investigators zero-in on the syndicates working specific ports or smuggling corridors.

Interpol, the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime and a clutch of investigative NGOs now stand ready to assist almost any customs post in the world.

The Elephant Trade Information System, a global seizure database maintained by the WWF’s animal-trade monitoring arm Traffic, shows that more than 12 tonnes of ivory were seized in Vietnam at the trade’s peak in 2018.

“We can now find out very easily from forensic samples whether seized ivory comes from recent poaching, long-standing stockpiles or natural mortality of an elephant,’’ Scanlon said.

But conservationists know they face a formidable adversary, with deep pockets.

“A lot of this larger-scale smuggling is facilitated by corruption … transnational and organised by corrupting officials across boundaries,” Scanlon said.

In the illicit ivory trade, money still opens borders just as effortlessly as it unlocks commercial markets.

“Vigilance is key,” Scanlon added. “We don’t want to see this illegal market explode again.’’
