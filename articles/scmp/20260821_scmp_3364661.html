China’s drone production capacity can only meet 60% of war demand: study

SCMP | FREE | 1051 words | 2026-08-21

Even when fully mobilised, China’s industrial system would only be able to meet 60 per cent of the country’s military demand for drones in a conflict, according to a rare study looking into the expected effects of a sudden war.

The estimates were produced by an artificial intelligence model that covered a multilevel supply chain linking drone assembly plants with subsystem and component suppliers. The study also accounted for inventories, production capacity, order backlogs, budgets and civilian factory conversion.

The paper was published online in the Journal of System Simulation on July 24. It was led by Professor Zhang Jihai, director of the National Defence Mobilisation Research Centre, and his team from the Beijing Institute of Technology.

The university has a strong military research background and is among the few Chinese universities subject to US sanctions.

“Modern warfare competition is no longer merely about the performance of weapon systems, but more fundamentally about industrial capacity – especially the comprehensive capability of production mobilisation for military equipment,” the researchers wrote.

The study found that capacity expansion could not quickly close the gap because new production lines took time to build.

Recent conflicts have shown how quickly modern wars can consume precision weapons, with Reuters reporting on August 4 that the US Army had used “virtually all” of its army tactical missile systems and precision strike missiles during its five-month war with Iran.

The war in Ukraine gives an even clearer example for drones, with a Pentagon official saying on July 27 that Ukraine was expected to produce 6 million to 7 million small first-person view attack drones in 2026 – about 500,000 a month – while US production remained a fraction of that level.

“Under high-intensity demand shocks, the supply chain, although possessing certain mobilisation and recovery capabilities, still faces a significant supply-demand gap,” the researchers wrote.

But the simulation also raises a bigger question about the scale of mobilisation, as the result would look very different if a major war brought companies that did not normally produce military equipment into the defence supply chain.

“Modern warfare, like wars in the past, is ultimately about supply chains,” said a Chinese weapons and equipment expert, who declined to give his name because of the sensitive nature of his work.

He warned that the ability to rapidly expand production could matter as much as the weapons already available. The expert holds a doctorate in weapons and equipment engineering and works in a field involving classified facilities.

“The rapid production capability can fully meet the demand, but the supply of core components is not that simple,” he said.

What would happen in China during a period of war would likely be similar to what happened “during the Three Major Campaigns”, the weapons specialist said.

He was referring to the three decisive strategic battles between the People’s Liberation Army and Kuomintang forces during the Chinese civil war. “It is a classic example [when] ordinary people were all mobilised to supply materials,” he said.

Such mobilisation could produce a very different outcome from a model that counted only existing military and civilian weapons manufacturers, according to the expert.

During the Covid-19 pandemic, for example, Chinese companies outside the medical sector rapidly switched production to masks and other protective equipment when demand surged.

In a major war, manufacturers that do not normally produce weapons could similarly become part of the military supply chain. These could include companies producing electronics, batteries, vehicles, machinery and consumer technology.

The distinction matters because a factory that does not make drones today may still have machinery, electronics expertise, battery technology, supply networks or manufacturing capacity that could be adapted for military production.

Zhang’s simulation therefore describes only one version of wartime capacity. A nationwide industrial mobilisation involving large civilian manufacturers could produce a very different result.

China already dominates large parts of the global drone supply chain and holds a huge share of the consumer drone market. Chinese-made consumer drones account for roughly 90 per cent of the US consumer market.

But industrial dominance in peacetime does not necessarily mean that production can be rapidly converted into military output during a major war.

The study used an unmanned aerial vehicle supply chain to simulate a sudden wartime surge in demand. The model linked military demand with a multilevel supply chain covering equipment and component suppliers, subsystems and a final assembly plant.

It also included inventories, production capacity, order backlogs, budgets, production safety and the ability of civilian manufacturers to switch to military production.

Civilian factories could not be converted instantly because of limits on capacity, technical adaptation and conversion efficiency.

“Capacity expansion and conversion are not activated continuously, but rather exhibit phased triggering and discontinuous implementation,” the researchers wrote.

In one simulation, demand rose to three times its normal level after the war began.

The system responded by releasing inventories, increasing overtime production, building new production lines and converting civilian capacity. Yet cumulative wartime order fulfilment reached only 63.4 per cent.

The unnamed expert said the study should therefore be treated mainly as an “argumentative study” rather than a prediction of what would happen in an actual war.

Zhang’s team compared no mobilisation, rule-based mobilisation and an AI-based strategy across 30 simulations. Median wartime order fulfilment rates were about 34 per cent, 51 per cent and 58 per cent respectively.

The AI strategy’s average fulfilment rate was 8.4 per cent higher than the rule-based approach. It was also 24.3 per cent higher than no mobilisation.

“The large language model-based [AI] mobilisation strategy achieves superior delivery performance and a higher wartime order fulfilment rate compared with no-mobilisation and rule-based mobilisation strategies,” the researchers wrote.

The AI model assessed inventory, production capacity, order pressure and budgets before selecting suppliers for expansion and civilian firms for conversion.

The model also found that increasing available mobilisation funding from 60 per cent to 140 per cent of the baseline raised wartime order fulfilment from 54.9 per cent to 62.3 per cent. Further spending produced smaller gains.

Factory construction times, conversion capacity and other physical constraints limited how quickly additional funding could create usable production capacity.

“There is a clear trade-off constraint between equipment supply assurance and production safety,” the researchers wrote.
