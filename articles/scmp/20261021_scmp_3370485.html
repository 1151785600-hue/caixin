Kurt Campbell on US deterrence, the Quad, Aukus and Taiwan after Xi-Trump summit

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 3397 words | 2026-10-21

Kurt Campbell is a veteran US diplomat and strategist who has played a leading role in shaping Washington’s approach to China and the Indo-Pacific for more than a decade.

He served as the White House coordinator for Indo-Pacific affairs from 2021 to 2024 and as US deputy secretary of state from 2024 to 2025. Previously, he served as assistant secretary of state for East Asian and Pacific affairs from 2009 to 2013 and was a key architect of the Obama administration’s “pivot to Asia”.

Often described as the Biden administration’s “Asia tsar”, Campbell also helped develop the Quad and the “small yard, high fence” approach to technology competition.

A former US Navy officer, Harvard professor and co-founder of the Centre for a New American Security, he is now chairman and co-founder of The Asia Group, a Washington-based strategic advisory firm.

You once argued that America’s enduring advantage over China was “allied scale” rather than unilateral dominance. But China’s strategy today looks different from the one Washington confronted in 2021. China now presents itself as a champion of sovereignty, non-interference and a more “multipolar” order while expanding its diplomatic and security presence. Is this a genuine strategic shift or a new form of competition expressed in different language? What is the realistic objective of US policy towards China today?

I think it is the determination of both leaders to take steps to stabilise a complex dynamic relationship between the two countries.

Both leaders understand that at the current moment, it is in the best interests of both countries to get along strategically. There are a variety of reasons for that. Some of them rest in a desire to improve competitive advantage. And some are motivated by a desire to genuinely work together.

My own sense is that the US is most effective as a global power when it works constructively with its allies and partners. President [Donald] Trump has taken more time and put more focus on building a stronger relationship with China than he has on working with some of his allies and partners. I very much support efforts to stabilise US-China relations. But I would like to see President Trump make more effort to stabilise relations with other partners. For instance, what we’re seeing in relations with Canada is quite shocking, suggesting that Canada is no longer one of the closest friends and partners to the US.

I find the fact that Canada is seeking to join the EU quite troubling. My general argument would be that US technology strategy, US efforts to sustain the global order are best achieved not just by trying to stabilise the US-China relationship, but by also seeking to maintain a strong posture with allies and partners.

So what is the realistic objective for US policy towards China?

There are a number of things that are necessary for US-China relations as we head into the next critical phase. I personally believe that the path to true cooperation between the United States and China on AI is very complicated, especially since President Trump said he seeks a dialogue with China but insists on dominance.

That message is a complicated one for Chinese friends to hear. It probably is a disincentive to seek true collaboration, because it suggests that the terms are implicitly or explicitly negative for China. What I think is important going forward is that both countries understand clearly the risks of frontier work, especially when researchers on both sides are increasingly voicing concerns about AI that we don’t fully understand or comprehend.

It is important that both the United States and China recognise that racing for advantage in a technological space that holds such potential risk – as well as promise – raises concerns. I would commend the work that the two countries have taken to date to at least find a degree of common ground for a continuing dialogue. But I will also say that this issue is developing rapidly, and both sides are going to need to adapt their position to establish a true dialogue around potential areas of collaboration, on safety reporting and mutual engagement.

The question for many of us is what is the appropriate metaphor when we think about AI. Is it nuclear weapons? Is it Covid? Is it climate change? Are there other experiences historically that we can utilise and apply to this new technology in the US-China context?

I think the one thing that is unique here is the role of the labs. These are not just technological capacities. They are the most powerful economic and commercial players of modern times. They are absolutely dominant. So they will want a voice in any deliberation between the United States and China. We don’t have as many experiences in the past in which it’s not just government to government, but the most powerful commercial and technological voices as well.

So are you particularly concerned about AI competition between China and the US?

In the larger context, I am worried about AI writ large. I believe that the politics of AI in the United States are becoming increasingly complicated. There is a bipartisan anxiety about data centres and their development. You’re seeing many states now questioning the buildout of these large entities. There are questions about capital use. There is an incredible level of investment in AI that has taken investment from other areas. In fact, many believe that to the extent that we have economic growth in the United States currently, it is largely because of the investment in AI. But I think more directly, it is undeniable when the most senior people in various companies are calling for government intervention and restraint that alerts us that there is a problem here.

Every week there is a new story of an AI entity that has escaped captivity and gone on an excursion, often creating havoc. There are also stories of engineers choosing to resign from these prestigious posts because they are worried not just about an incident but the status of humankind. Many very senior people have worried about human extinction.

It seems to me that if that is a truly serious matter and debated among engineers and designers of AI, it is incumbent on us to reflect, to think about guardrails, to consider safety and to understand that there are existential issues here that in many respects transcend simple competition.

Let’s talk about Taiwan and Japan. Does strategic ambiguity remain the best policy or have China’s military capabilities and pressure campaigns made greater clarity necessary?

I still believe that one of the great success stories of US-China relations – this is rarely discussed – is the complex maintenance of peace and stability between the two countries with respect to Taiwan.

There have been lots of tensions and disappointments, but the truth is the status quo has served the interests of the people of Taiwan. I would argue the framework that the United States and China have maintained for decades is being increasingly tested by military and political developments. But I still believe that this framework is in the best interests of all concerned. And so it is often said that we have a degree of strategic ambiguity but tactical clarity; I think that it is important for the United States to underscore our determination to maintain peace and stability.

But it is also the case that a dialogue over Taiwan is handled carefully between the two leaders. It’s undeniable that this issue has come up and that it is being actively discussed.

If Beijing imposed a quarantine or blockade without launching a conventional attack, would that trigger a US response?

I’m out of government now. I’m not really in the business of offering views on particular scenarios. My own sense is that the more we discuss openly potential scenarios, the less it might be stabilising to the situation. If anything, they tend to draw sharp contours in a complex environment. And, in many respects, create even greater distrust.

My own sense is that the United States should maintain a strong unofficial relationship with Taiwan, recognise how critical this issue is in a larger US-China relationship and manage this with care and dexterity.

Despite the ups and downs, history reflects that it is possible to take the necessary steps to maintain peace and stability. And I fully expect that this can continue.

On Taiwan, what is the strategic cost of treating arms sales to Taiwan as negotiable within the broader US-China relationship? Is there any practical form of Taiwan-related cooperation that Washington and Beijing could undertake? What would a credible cross-strait dialogue look like if Beijing continues to reject engagement with Taiwan’s current government?

The strategic risk of treating arms sales to Taiwan as negotiable is that it can inject doubt into the credibility that has helped preserve cross-strait stability for decades. While there are deep differences over Taiwan, both Washington and Beijing have a common interest in crisis management, military-to-military communications and reducing the risk of unintended escalation.

Given the tensions between China and Japan, what responsibility does Washington have to ensure that the US-Japan alliance remains a mechanism for deterrence rather than escalation? Is Japan primarily becoming a frontline deterrent, an industrial and technological partner or a military coequal?

I would simply say that Japan is an important partner of the United States. It has a relatively small military and its defence investments are quite modest. I believe that one of the most critical paths of diplomacy in Asia is to make sure that lines of communication between Beijing and Tokyo are open.

My own sense is that the Japanese would prefer to have a more open dialogue with Beijing. I think one of the issues occasionally challenging is that the historical issues still animate Beijing’s thinking about Japan.

One of the things that I’ve noted is the effectiveness of Chinese diplomacy generally, their ability to reach out to countries in which they have a complex history. In particular, what we’ve seen is renewed diplomacy between China and India. I think it would be in China’s interests to open up more lines of communication with Tokyo. I do not believe that Japan is somehow an American cat’s paw. The people of Japan are deeply peace-loving. Efforts to revise the constitution or to move Japan in a more assertive direction have actually met with substantial domestic opposition.

I think some of the military modernisation in Japan is indeed modest. It is designed to reflect increasing challenges in the Indo-Pacific. And remember, those do not come exclusively in the US or in the Japan-China relationship.

What Japan is confronting is an increasingly hostile North Korea that is well armed and has nuclear capabilities. So Japan is seeking to play a larger role in Asia. I support that. But at the same time, I do believe there have been periods in the past where the lines of communication between Tokyo and Beijing have been much stronger. I do know that the Japanese are troubled by how difficult it has been to establish communication with Beijing. And I think just as President Trump and President Xi [Jinping] have found some common purpose and would meet in a complex time, I think President Xi and Prime Minister [Sanae] Takaichi can find the political courage to sit down as well.

China increasingly portrays this sort of alliance – whether it’s with Japan, or trilateral cooperation with South Korea and Japan, or quadrilateral with India, Australia and Japan – as an instrument of containment. Given China’s thinking, how should Washington support their expanded security role while assuring China that these alliances are defensive?

The US seeking closer relations between Japan and South Korea has more to do with their own histories than it does with China. I would simply remind Chinese friends that not every development in Asia is about China: South Korea and Japan have profound historical tensions, but they are close allies of the United States. It is only natural that the United States would seek the two countries to coordinate more closely. Each country has its own unique relationship with China. It is also the case that closer coordination among the three could have a stabilising impact across the Indo-Pacific. The Quad has not met at the leader level under President Trump, but its first incarnation in the early 2000s was a result of the tragedy that struck Indonesia and Malaysia. The terrible tsunami.

I myself had been actively engaged in the Quad. Much of what the Quad was involved with in its most recent incarnation was Covid vaccine development and South Pacific issues related to education and training.

I would say that the Quad was primarily about delivering well-being to underserved groups and creating more capacity. I don’t believe that any country in the Quad would support the institution becoming an alliance aimed at any one country. I see it mostly as developing common approaches to complex societal and security problems. These are four maritime democracies and each again has its own set of relationships.

With Beijing, more recently, we are seeing India and China edging closer to a more substantial dialogue. I think the Quad can exist with a range of diplomatic formations in Asia. As much as anything, it helps anchor the United States in the Indo-Pacific, which is in the strategic interests of Washington.

Should the Quad become more institutionalised? Or would that make it less flexible and more threatening to China? How vulnerable is it to domestic political changes in these four member states?

I cannot speak to what’s expected next in the Trump administration, but there has been no discussion recently of reconvening the Quad at the leader level. I think without that, it’s difficult to sustain active forward momentum. I do not know what President Trump’s ambitions are for the Quad besides the foreign ministry meetings and a variety of other assessments. But I think at this juncture, there is no push or intention towards institutionalisation. If anything, the focus continues to be on areas which are primarily about the well-being, for instance, of Pacific island nations. One of the areas of cooperation in the past, for instance, was on dealing with illegal fishing in the Pacific, which is an issue that Pacific island nations care deeply about and do not have the capacity to address.

So I do not see the institution emerging as a direct vehicle for somehow containing China. And I just think that framework is outmoded. China is a great, powerful country with deep interests across the world. It’s not possible to contain China and the most recent work towards this coming summit between the United States and China brings that point home. China is a country that needs to be engaged actively. It is not possible to think about China in the context of containment.

Would a reduced US-China rivalry lessen the importance of security partnerships such as the Quad, Aukus and other minilateral groupings? If so, what implications would that have for the balance of power, deterrence and Indo-Pacific stability?

Partnerships like the Quad and Aukus are valuable because countries across the Indo-Pacific recognise that many of the region’s challenges, from maritime security and technological change to supply chain resilience and disaster response, require closer cooperation. The value of these partnerships does not depend on the status of the US-China relationship.

Even in a period of more stable US-China relations, these partnerships would continue to play a critical role in strengthening deterrence, building collective capacity and fostering a regional balance that supports peace and prosperity. In many respects, the greatest contribution of a grouping like the Quad is the creation of habits of cooperation, strategic trust and practical capabilities.

What would the durable regional security architecture look like if it included China rather than being organised against China? Is it possible to have such a thing?

We’ve had a number of efforts at regional security or regional dialogue, such as the East Asia Summit, the Asean Regional Forum, which have been established for decades.

One of the challenges has been the uneven participation of the United States. We don’t attend all of the meetings. In fact, I think you’ve seen, particularly in the Trump administration, an ambivalence about regional institutions. We’ve long sought to create capacities for more dialogue and cooperation among Indo-Pacific actors. We’ve seen that at Apec. We’ve seen that at the Asean Regional Forum and in the East Asia Summit. I would go as far as to say that, by some measurements, nine of the 20 members in G20 are Indo-Pacific nations. In many respects that institutional innovation was designed to reflect the increasing importance, not just of China but of other rising nations in the Indo-Pacific.

I would like to see the United States more actively engaged in that multilateral diplomacy. I do not think it is a secret that the United States is less interested and focused on that diplomacy and seeks, in many respects, to act unilaterally without constraints with other countries. I do not believe that is in America’s strategic interests. I think investing in regional architecture is important and a critical dimension of our national power.

What’s your take on the US changing the name of INDOPACOM to USPACOM?

It was Trump’s first administration that originally changed it to Indo-Pacific. And now they’re changing it back. I don’t know if that is a desire to return to an earlier period framework. At first, the Indo-Pacific framework was adopted by Japanese Prime Minister Shinzo Abe, and that framework was accepted by parts of the Trump administration. When President [Joe] Biden came to power, the Japanese asked for that framework to be continued. We supported that. I do not know if this is a request from President Xi to President Trump, or whether this is a rethinking by Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth. There are too many things that have happened in terms of Pentagon policy to speculate about why they move in one direction or another.

How can Washington reassure its Asian allies and Southeast Asian countries that its partnerships do not force them to choose between the US and China? Is the current alliance-centred strategy sufficiently attentive to Asean priorities?

The most important dimension here is for the United States to invest actively in regional institutions and bilateral engagements. Most of the frameworks of engagement that we have seen most recently have been around highly difficult trade relations with a variety of countries in Southeast Asia. I increasingly hear from a variety of Southeast Asian countries that they are adopting policies that allow them to maintain a degree of flexibility in engagement with both Washington and Beijing.

The most effective US policy does not demand countries to make some sort of overt choice between the two.

And there is a deep recognition that commercial and political ties between Beijing and Southeast Asia are only natural, as is the United States maintaining close ties in Southeast Asia. These are not zero-sum relationships. They can be mutually beneficial.

What do you think is Hong Kong’s role in Sino-US relations? And how should the city move forward?

It’s a complex city. It’s undeniable. It is, in many respects, a financial launching point for China’s relations with the world. It still has some characteristics of its past, but it is increasingly a Chinese city that has an extraordinarily critical and important role in the global economy.

And as far as I can tell, it appears to continue to play a very dynamic role in the global financial economy. And it is continuing to be a meeting place between East and West and the financial centre of activities across the Indo-Pacific. There are other capitals that are emerging in that equation: Singapore, Tokyo and the like. But Hong Kong continues to play a vital role.
