From Ohio to Hong Kong: former NBA player Bryant Austin’s basketball journey

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1581 words | 2026-09-11

I WAS BORN in Lima, Ohio, a small city in the United States, to high-school sweethearts, in 1987. My childhood was split between the inner city and my great-grandparents’ farm: livestock, a wood-burning furnace, the whole thing. Although both my parents had been athletes, I never saw them in their prime. Instead, I watched my older sister. My parents split up when I was six or seven, and we moved to Columbus. My mum worked four jobs to support us. Money was tight, but there were constants: Bruce Lee and Jackie Chan films, and a meal at a Chinese restaurant as a reward when I did well at school.

AT MY GRANDFATHER’S house, I’d sit for hours watching old VHS basketball tapes. There was Magic Johnson, Michael Jordan. One clip stayed with me – a young Kobe Bryant growing up in Italy, speaking Italian, playing basketball on the other side of the world (his father played for an Italian team). Something shifted in me. I started to dream of playing overseas. I was also quietly obsessed with Asia. I watched anime, drew constantly – it felt like a world I couldn’t explain to anyone in Ohio. Every year, our teacher asked us what our top three ambitions were. Mine never changed: professional basketball player, marine biologist, or lawyer. In that order. I was already manifesting it.

MY SISTER IS two years older than me, and she was my hero before I knew what that meant. At school, she protected me from bullies, from older boys who wanted to jump me. When I was 13, a group of men broke into our home and held our family hostage at gunpoint for hours. While two of them left to move what they had stolen, one stayed behind. My sister – 15, maybe 16 then – wrestled the gun away from him. She shot him in the face. She saved all of us.

I WASN’T AN immediate star. I have always been super-introverted, and had anxiety interacting with crowds and fans. I didn’t make varsity until my senior year. No one was knocking down my door offering scholarships. So, I started clicking through university websites, cold-emailing coaches. That’s how mundane it was. I ended up playing at Notre Dame College, then Columbus State and Indiana Tech. After graduating, I played in the professional minor leagues, but it wasn’t enough.

MY TURNING POINT was a high-profile tournament in Portsmouth, Virginia, where I was up against players from Duke, Kentucky, North Carolina – the big blue-blood universities. I didn’t just hold my own; I dominated. There was a rage in me, the accumulated weight of being overlooked for years. My trainer and mentor, Greg “Radar” Williamson, spent two years helping me prepare for that moment.

BY THE END, three NBA (National Basketball Association) agents were competing to represent me: Andre Buck, who worked with LeBron James; Sean Rooks, who had played alongside Kobe and Shaq (Shaquille O’Neal); and John Spencer, who had a past playing in Hong Kong’s A1 Division against Yao Ming and China’s national team. I signed with John. At the time, I didn’t realise how significant he would become to me.

AT 25, I WAS drafted into the NBA Development League by the Fort Wayne Mad Ants. It was overwhelming. You’re meeting Magic Johnson and all these star players. So, on one side, you’re starstruck and on the other side, you’re thinking, “I’m supposed to be here.”

I BROKE MY thumb the day before training camp. I decided to play through it without a cast because the NBA is so cutthroat, I couldn’t risk losing my shot. I also had a newborn son at home and no room for excuses. That season, I was also working as an overnight carer for disabled adults and as a substitute teacher. Three jobs, one broken thumb, a new baby. On the court, I was playing beside Khris Middleton, who would go on to become an NBA All-Star. I was proud, exhausted and watching every penny.

AT THE END of that season, the team president, Jeff Potter, sat me down and was honest with me. He advised me to go overseas, build my profile and then come back, as I wasn’t getting much court time alongside so many more experienced players. John Spencer helped me get a contract in Israel. And when I landed in Tel Aviv (in October 2013), I was in heaven. I had an apartment right by the sea. The city was beautiful: new culture, new language, new food. Then came the blood clot.

I PLAYED THROUGH what I thought was a severe calf strain for a month – it felt like my leg was being sawn off – but ego and pride refused to let me sit it out. I had developed a blood clot in my leg and then one in my lung and didn’t know it. The pain started in my chest, and nearly ended everything – not just my career, but my life.

I WAS BACK home in the US (by December 2013), had almost died and was injecting blood thinners daily. The doctors told me I couldn’t play. I was depressed for six months. I didn’t talk to many people, I just retreated into myself and stopped training. I’d fought all those years to have my name on that jersey. Having it taken away so suddenly left me in a dark place. Then, a new drug, Xarelto, appeared and gave my cardiologist enough confidence to clear me. That same week (in April 2014), without knowing any of this, John Spencer called out of nowhere and asked if I’d go to Hong Kong. The league had been closed to American imports since the 2008 financial crisis, but had just reopened and the money was good. I call that timing a call from God.

I STEPPED OFF the plane into the heat and humidity, and looked up at the skyline. Instantly, I fell completely in love. I saw bamboo scaffolding around glass towers and felt like I’d walked onto a Rush Hour set. Apparently, I was all over the local media. My American teammate, Reggie Johnson, had full culture shock; the density, the noise, strangers staring at two tall black guys on trains and in lifts. But I didn’t mind any of it. It felt like arriving somewhere I’d been dreaming of since I was a kid.

THE BASKETBALL SYSTEM in Hong Kong is unlike anywhere else; it’s fast-paced and kind of chaotic. We won the championship with the Eastern Long Lions (now Hong Kong Eastern) team. I flew my mum over for the final and we celebrated with champagne.

IN AUGUST 2014, I went to Bangkok to party for a week, to celebrate my Hong Kong championship with my close friend and NBA G League teammate, Tommy Smith, who works with me today. I asked to train with his team to stay sharp between nights out. They begged me to stay and I said yes. I played in the Thai Super League season, and then for an Indonesian team in Surabaya.

INSTEAD OF GOING back to the US, I realised that life had a plan for me in Asia, and specifically in Hong Kong – it always drew me in. Now, I play forward and point guard for Chongde Flying Eagle here in the A1 Division. When I first got here, the Hong Kong fans were crazy, following me around, asking for autographs, photos. I just love that they love basketball as much as I do. I started a basketball academy with just five kids. I’d turn up in my jersey on the street courts of Southorn Playground, Nathan Road and Victoria Park, and ask to join games. Kids would clock who I was and ask if I coached. That’s how my academy, League MVP, started – organically.

TODAY, OUR TEAM works with up to 150 local boys as well as international students here on holiday camps. We’ve worked with refugee children from Africa, India and Pakistan. One of the players I coached, Hong Kong-born David Muoka, became the first from the city to be drafted into the NBA.

WHAT I WANT for Hong Kong basketball is more visibility, more opportunity. There’s talent here. It just needs investment, time and belief. Hong Kong is an underdog, but there are a lot of passionate players, and I want them to win.

MY SON IS 13 now. Every summer, he comes over from Ohio. I wake him early – he hates it – and we’re in the gym by 5am. I never forced basketball on him, but once he chose it, I made it clear: no short cuts. We train hard, then we enjoy the city – Disneyland, Ocean Park, beaches, hotpot, the MTR. I push him to explore independently; this city is safe enough to do that.

TWELVE YEARS AFTER I arrived, Hong Kong is home – not a posting, or a chapter. I’m 38, still playing, still growing my business, still up early doing weights – my life is kind of consumed by basketball. I’m manifesting my end game – the best is yet to come. And I know I want to be here when the next kid from a Hong Kong court makes it somewhere nobody expected, and gives the city another reason to believe.
