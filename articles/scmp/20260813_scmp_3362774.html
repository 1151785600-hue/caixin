Shaoxing wine goes upmarket: Dongqu’s luxury touch elevates the Chinese pantry staple

SCMP | FREE | 1281 words | 2026-08-13

For a small city of three million people, Shaoxing stands as a giant in the global Chinese collective consciousness. After all, the name of the eastern Chinese city is emblazoned on the quintessential bottles of cooking wine that are found in Asian households and restaurants the world over, with which numerous Chinese regional cuisines – from the complex braises of Shanghai and Zhejiang (the province where Shaoxing is found) to the spicy marinades of Sichuan province and the subtle steamed seafood of Cantonese fare – would be nigh on impossible to cook without.

Shaoxing wine is one of China’s oldest grain ferments, evolving from ancient rice ales into a polished yellow wine poured at rites and family banquets for more than two millennia. First linked to early ritual offerings in the Spring and Autumn period (770BC to 476BC) and Han era (206BC to AD220), it later soaked the verses of Tang and Song dynasty poets, becoming a favoured pour at riverside feasts and scholarly gatherings. By the Qing dynasty (1644-1911), it had settled into its role as a symbol of longevity and good fortune, especially around autumn and winter festivals.

The character of Shaoxing wine is defined by three markers of terroir: the base grain of glutinous rice, Jianhu lake water fed by the Kuaiji Mountains and the qu (a wheat-based starter) that seeds fermentation with a complex microbiome. Today, Shaoxing is protected as a geographical indication, so any bottle bearing the name must be born of this landscape.

Yet, for an industry that produces more than 600,000 tonnes annually, only a fraction of Shaoxing wine is intended for table-drinking, and just a hair’s sliver of that is produced by boutique wineries at the luxury end of the market.

Chen Zhi is looking to change that. In his late 30s, the boisterous, bespectacled businessman is a rare presence in a slow-moving, tradition-bound industry that is closely tied to the state, where the cultural concept of guanxi (connections) still reigns supreme.

In 2017, suffering from spontaneous nerve spasms that paralysed one side of his face, Chen was on his way to an acupuncture appointment when he chanced upon a fair organised by the China Alcoholic Drinks Association, where he was roped into a tasting of Shaoxing wine from the historic Pagoda Brand, leading him down a rabbit hole into which he has “been sinking deeper and deeper ever since, right up to this day”, he says.

Chen went on to launch Dongqu in 2019, defying many of the norms associated with Shaoxing wine.

First, there’s the price: at a starting price of HK$600 (US$77), even its most affordable bottling, the 2009 vintage, is almost triple the price of other high-quality sipping Shaoxing wines, such as the 10-year-aged bottling from the famed Nu’er Hong brand. This is largely a result of Dongqu’s laborious production processes, which depart from the industry practice of blending different wines for a consistent flavour. Instead, Dongqu opts for single-year vintages drawn directly from the earthenware jars they’re aged in – all the better to bring out the unique growing and fermentation conditions of each particular season.

Then there’s the move-fast-and-break-things approach that Chen has cultivated at Dongqu that, despite falling under the umbrella of the vast Pagoda Brand Winery, functions like a start-up.

“The mission of state-owned enterprises is not to make mistakes,” Chen tells me over a sumptuous dinner at the one-Michelin-star, Taizhou fine-dining restaurant Wild Yeast in Hangzhou. “There’s a slang term – mo yu [to stroke a fish] – which means to slack off and do nothing. Doing well isn’t necessarily a good thing, and doing badly also doesn’t really matter – you just have to guarantee a tiny bit of growth every year.”

In contrast, Dongqu has done everything but rest on its laurels. In just seven years, the boutique brand has expanded to more than 200 Michelin- and Black Pearl-awarded establishments across China, which have found it to be a perfect food pairing.

Like sake production, the brewing process for high-end Shaoxing wine takes place over the colder winter months, to prevent spoilage and allow for careful control of fermentation. On an overcast day in early November, the brewing halls at the Pagoda Brand Winery, on the shores of the Jianhu, are in full swing, pumping billowing, rice-scented columns of steam onto osmanthus-lined roads of the compound. There are 15 shifu (brewmasters) in charge of the process, each responsible for the operations of a brewing hall. Despite the sheer physicality of the job, experience is crucial here, with the youngest shifu already well into their 50s.

Mao-capped brewmaster Ni Xingjun is already working up a sweat despite the chill in the air, as he rakes a mixture of yeast, glutinous rice and Jianhu lake water in a large earthen jar – a labour-intensive task that must be repeated every four to five hours around the clock over four days for the hundreds of jars on the main brewing floor.

The winemaking is highly technical: the rice must be soaked and steamed to kick-start the brewing process, and the temperature of the grains meticulously controlled throughout by calibrating the steaming time to the temperature of the water as well as the ambient environment to ensure proper fermentation later. The shifu combines his intuition and years of experience with hard data to tease out the best qualities of the rice grains, a varietal from Anqing, Anhui province, that is allowed a longer growth cycle to increase its stores of starch, resulting in a wine with bolder flavours.

“If you’d like, lean in and take a deep breath of this aroma,” says Ni, pulling me towards a freshly raked vat. I breathe in, filling my nostrils with a milky, malty scent that is both gentle and all-encompassing, comforting and intoxicating all at once; a scent that is instantly nostalgic to anyone for whom a freshly steamed bowl of rice was a fixture at the dinner table.

Today, Dongqu is spearheading a return to dinner tables not just in homes, but across some of the most in-demand restaurant tables across the country. In Hong Kong, the brand’s layered, umami-forward pours have been featured in traditional-leaning pairings, such as with braised vermicelli with hairy crab at neo-Cantonese restaurant Jee and spritzed atop a crispy stuffed sea cucumber at Vea, to more left-field match-ups as with the signature king crab arroz caldoso at one-Michelin-star Ando, and even transformed into a Shaoxing wine ice cream to cap off an Edomae omakase meal at Sushi Kumogaku.

“Compared to sake, Dongqu offers a richer and more complex flavour profile, which is fully released when heated, making it particularly suited to grilling and simmering,” says Kumogaku’s head chef, Chan Wing-kin. He believes that it adds a deeper aroma to seafood that is difficult to find in Japanese cuisine.

This boundary-crossing milieu is all part of Chen’s mission to refashion the image of Shaoxing wine as a marker of Chinese haute gastronomy, rather than a cheap pantry staple relegated to the status of a mere condiment. He likens the winemaking process – tending to the yeast, keeping watch around the clock – and growing the brand as being akin to the process of raising his first daughter.

“As everyone knows, newborn babies need constant care, and what I saw at the brewing site was the same,” Chen recalls. “It’s like watching a child grow up. You don’t know what they’ll become; you can only wait patiently from the sidelines.”
