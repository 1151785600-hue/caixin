Why Filipino migrants are rethinking their futures in Japan

SCMP | FREE | 1138 words | 2026-08-28

When Japan’s embassy in the Philippines extended applications for healthcare jobs on April 15, the pitch was straightforward: 50 positions for nurses and 300 for care workers in Japan, a three- to four-year placement, free Japanese-language lessons in both countries and a daily allowance during training.

On social media, the post’s attractive terms drew widespread interest from Filipinos – a reminder that Japan is still urgently recruiting foreign labour even as Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi’s administration, amid growing political pressure over foreign residents, moves to make it harder for migrants to settle long-term.

The need is especially acute in care work: Japan will require about 2.72 million care workers by financial year 2040, roughly 570,000 more than in 2022, according to Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare estimates, while one think tank projection has warned the country could be nearly 1 million foreign workers short by then if current inflows continue.

For the Filipino care workers, nurses and other professionals who came to Japan seeking greener pastures, the contradiction has become personal: the country needs them, but high living costs, a weak yen and uncertainty over immigration rules make it harder to imagine staying long-term.

Job Galutan Andawey, a licensed nurse from the Philippines working as a carer in Kanagawa prefecture, is in his sixth year in Japan after arriving through the Japan-Philippines Economic Partnership Agreement, a treaty that includes career placement and language training programmes for nurses and care workers.

“I chose Japan because I was influenced by Japanese culture. I dreamed of working here in Japan since I was little,” Andawey told This Week in Asia.

But as time runs out on his work visa, the Filipino nurse is having second thoughts.

“The cost of living is getting expensive while the salary is stagnant. And with the increase in visa fees and not much aid coming from the company, it is hard to save up,” Andawey said, adding that he was considering moving to Europe or the Middle East if he could not remain in Japan.

Merrychris Urcales, a carer in Kagawa prefecture who came to Japan on a special skilled worker visa in 2024, said finding a job had been “relatively easier compared with other types of work because there is a high demand for carers in Japan”.

But the weak yen and rising costs made saving difficult, and Urcales said she would need to consider other countries that might offer “better opportunities and a higher income”.

For some workers, concerns over the yen’s declining value outweigh anxiety over immigration policy changes and reports of anti-foreigner sentiment, according to Alyssa Manalo, a PhD student at Hiroshima University who researches Filipino migration.

“These changes do not seem to be as significant” for carers, assistant language teachers and those on special skilled worker visas, Manalo said.

“For newer migrants who have their families in the Philippines, they generally just want to finish their contracts, save money, and return to the Philippines … they were more concerned about the declining yen than about changes in immigration policies or anti-foreigner sentiment.”

The low exchange rate made it harder for migrant workers to send the same amount of money home, Manalo said.

For those who have already spent years adapting to the country, however, Japan’s tighter immigration policies have increased the sense that long-term settlement is becoming harder.

“They made it easier to come here, but made staying more difficult,” Kevin*, an engineer from the Philippines who has lived in Tokyo for 10 years, told This Week in Asia.

The challenges are not lost on Catherine Ogalesco, a licensed care worker who first came to Japan nearly nine years ago to work in a factory.

Ogalesco said she saw no problem with tougher language requirements, as she believed preparing for life in Japan required fluency.

But she took issue with the proposed increase in the income threshold for permanent residency applications from 3 million yen (US$18,830) annually to 5.75 million yen, or 479,000 yen in monthly household income.

“I think it is unfair especially for single households like myself, and for foreign workers in Japan in fields such as nursing care, factories, hotels and agriculture, where workers are sorely needed … how many foreign workers earn that much? Probably less than 20 per cent,” Ogalesco said.

“With those rules, they are already excluding us from the possibility of applying for permanent residency.”

The policy changes have also unsettled students and white-collar workers.

Manalo said she had hoped to remain in Japan to pursue an academic career researching Filipino migrants, but was now re-evaluating her options.

“Part of the proposed law would require foreign nationals to have Japanese proficiency, and I’m not that good at speaking Japanese,” said Manalo, who is on a scholarship programme.

She said her last visa extension cost 6,000 yen, but the proposed guidelines would mean she might have to pay up to 50,000 yen depending on the length of the extension.

Language requirements are also a source of anxiety for Amanda*, a software programmer who moved to Japan in 2022.

Her company sponsored her language training until she passed level 3 of the Japanese-Language Proficiency Test, a condition for full-time employment, but has since asked her to continue studying towards the advanced-intermediate level 2.

“Even at level 3, I still don’t feel confident in my language skills … I’m getting anxious at the possibility of being asked for a level 2 certification just so I can keep my job,” Amanda told This Week in Asia.

She considered moving to a larger company for better prospects but said the shifting climate made her more cautious.

“I’m afraid of applying for other job positions as it might put my visa renewal at risk next year,” she said.

Despite the policy changes, foreign residents described reports of rising anti-immigrant sentiment as the views of a minority amplified by the political climate, rather than a universal experience.

“I live in a rural area of Japan, and I have not experienced any forms of racism or discrimination. As long as you follow Japanese etiquette and rules, I personally don’t think Japanese people will say anything bad to you,” Manalo said.

Ogalesco remains undeterred by the changing rules. “Even with the changes, I would still choose to stay and work here in Japan. Here, I feel safe and financially comfortable.”

Manalo is hopeful that Japan’s growing reliance on foreign workers will push the government to reconsider its approach as the country continues to grapple with labour shortages and an ageing population.

“Japan is already becoming more dependent on foreign workers, particularly in sectors such as caregiving, construction and agriculture,” she said.

“At some point, Japan needs to be more open to migrants, especially in care-related work where the demand for workers will continue to grow.”

*Names changed to protect interviewees’ identities
