Philippines’ Marcos uses national address to promise relief, but funding questions loom

SCMP | FREE | 1281 words | 2026-07-27

Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos Jnr used his 2026 State of the Nation Address (Sona) on Monday to promise relief from taxes, electricity charges and high rice prices, while pledging to pursue corruption cases linked to a flood-control scandal that has battered his administration.

But while analysts said the one-and-a-half-hour speech addressed issues Filipinos had expected Marcos to confront, they questioned how the government could afford the handouts and why he announced no austerity or revenue-raising measures to address continuing financial and economic pressures and a deepening debt burden.

Marcos began his speech by returning to the corruption issue that dominated last year’s address: an inquiry into alleged irregularities in flood-control projects, including claims of ghost, substandard and overpriced works, that investigators said had channelled public funds through kickbacks to politicians, public works officials and contractors.

The scandal has become one of the biggest tests of Marcos’ anti-corruption drive, not least because it has implicated former House speaker Martin Romualdez, the president’s first cousin and long-time political ally, who stepped down last year amid mounting pressure over the controversy.

In his speech, Marcos said cases had already been filed against and some arrests made of contractors, senior Department of Public Works and Highways officials and lawmakers.

He said that, according to the Ombudsman, the country’s anti-corruption body, authorities were close to filing “multiple cases against the former speaker”.

Without naming Romualdez, Marcos said: “This pains me, but we have to do what is right. I am not the president of my family. I am not the president of my friends. I am the president of the Philippines, and my duty is to the Filipino people.”

Romualdez has denied wrongdoing and said he is prepared to face any charges.

Marcos said the flood-control probe had resulted in the recovery, freezing or preservation of almost 25 billion pesos (US$405 million) in money and assets.

To help Filipinos cope with multiple crises, Marcos said he would ask Congress to pass laws that would remove the charge of “system loss” – the cost passed on to consumers for electricity lost in the power grid or through pilferage – from all electricity bills, raise the tax exemption threshold for individual taxpayers from 250,000 pesos to 350,000 pesos and remove the minimum corporate income tax on microenterprises.

He also announced an amnesty period for those with unpaid income tax, estate tax and donor tax, as well as value-added tax.

Marcos said he would expand the coverage of his rice subsidy programme, which sells rice at 20 pesos per kilogram instead of at current market rates of more than twice that amount, to up to 8 million families from the present 2.5 million families.

Former agriculture undersecretary and policy expert Fermin Adriano told This Week in Asia that the president’s speech was “filled with spending but no mention of how his government will raise required revenues”.

“In developed countries, any programme or project mentioned will require a calculation of how much it will cost, where the money will be generated or shifted from one priority to another,” Adriano said.

Financial risk analyst Jonathan Ravelas also told This Week in Asia: “After listening to the whole speech, the only thing I can think of is – where will the money [to do all that] be coming from?”

Ravelas, managing director of eManagement for Business and Marketing Services and retired chief market strategist of the Philippines’ largest bank BDO, noted that Marcos’ speech mentioned a lot of revenue cutbacks and more foreign direct investment.

But he said the country was currently in “a state of permanent crisis” because of economic difficulties brought on by the Russia-Ukraine war and the Middle East conflict.

“In a country where you have an emergency and you talk of continued spending, isn’t there something wrong with that?” he said.

He also cautioned that the revenue-easing measures Marcos announced would still need congressional approval.

Last week, Marcos’ public affairs undersecretary Claire Castro had said the speech would be “earth-shaking”.

However, Dr Jean Franco, a political-science professor at the University of the Philippines, was underwhelmed.

“It was not earth-shaking at all, but he said what people expected of him, given the crisis and the spectre of the flood control scandal,” she told This Week in Asia.

She also pointed out that it was not enough to file cases against those involved in the flood-control scandal without also proposing measures to reform the system. “Otherwise, the cycle of corruption continues.”

Franco said Marcos “did not stress long-term solutions to prevent another flood control scandal”, citing the need for a flood-control master plan to coordinate priority projects, funding and agency responsibilities rather than rely on piecemeal works.

“The implication is that he wants to be perceived as a president responsive to the demands of the people. I think he is humbled by his low approval and trust ratings, which somehow is a repudiation of his landslide win in 2022.”

Hours before the Sona, private pollster OCTA Research released its second-quarter survey showing both Marcos and Vice-President Sara Duterte-Carpio – his 2022 running mate and former ally turned political enemy – had fallen below 50 per cent on at least one key performance or trust measure.

Marcos’ performance rating fell by eight percentage points, from 55 per cent in March to 47 per cent, while his trust rating slipped from 54 per cent to 49 per cent, according to the survey conducted from July 4 to 11.

Duterte-Carpio’s performance rating slid from 50 per cent to 45 per cent, while her trust rating dropped from 55 per cent to 47 per cent, with the polling period coinciding with the opening of her Senate impeachment trial over allegations including misuse of public funds and threats against Marcos and first lady Liza Araneta-Marcos.

Duterte-Carpio has denied wrongdoing and accused Marcos’ allies of political persecution.

The vice-president, who was abroad, was a no-show, along with Marcos’ estranged elder sister, Senator Imee Marcos, and former Senate president Alan Peter Cayetano, who now leads the Senate minority.

Imee, whose relationship with her younger brother has deteriorated as she aligned herself more closely with the Duterte camp, wore black and a patchwork dress made of fabric scraps at the Senate session hours before the Sona, saying it reflected the “fragmented (gutay-gutay), sliced up (hiwa-hiwa), broken up (biyak-biyak)” state of the Philippines under his presidency.

Marcos turned to the South China Sea only near the end of the speech, framing the dispute not just as a sovereignty issue but also as a question of national dignity.

Although he did not name China, his remarks appeared to reference a diplomatic row over an AI-generated editorial video posted on state-run news outlet China Daily’s Facebook account, which Philippine officials condemned as racist and dehumanising over its depiction of Filipinos as monkeys. China’s foreign ministry said the video did not represent Beijing’s official position.

“A common threat lies before us, directed against our dignity and self-respect. Of late, our very humanity has been called into question,” Marcos said.

“Let me remind our friends and our detractors. Filipinos are a noble, kind, gracious and great race. We are not greedy. We are not racist. We are not liars. We are respectful and we are dignified. We are Filipinos and we do not yield.”

Marcos said his government “will do all that is necessary to continue upholding [the 2016 arbitral] award, utilising every peaceful and legal means available”, referring to the international tribunal ruling that rejected Beijing’s sweeping claims in the South China Sea and upheld Manila’s sovereign rights in its exclusive economic zone.
