Can Europe stay united in China trade battle? Ceuta migrant crisis offers a warning

SCMP | FREE | 1160 words | 2026-08-10

The European Union’s traditional August break was supposed to provide officials with some respite ahead of a potentially bruising autumn when it will try to set its fiery trade relationship with China on a new course.

Instead, a border crisis on its southern flank has created turmoil and division among its 27 members, raising questions over how prepared the bloc is for a dust-up with one of the world’s economic superpowers.

“We had a real-time test of what happens when one member state was under coercion,” said Alberto Rizzi, a Rome-based policy fellow at the European Council on Foreign Relations, specialising in geoeconomics. “Everybody was very quick to jump on that.”

The crisis was sparked on July 30, when an estimated 72,000 mainly Moroccan migrants surged into Ceuta, a Spanish enclave in North Africa, after Moroccan border controls appeared to collapse. At least 75 people died, mostly at sea, with the Spanish authorities estimating more than 100 died.

The surge raised suspicions that the Moroccan authorities had weaponised migrant flows in response to Spanish efforts to improve ties with its neighbour and rival Algeria.

In the immediate aftermath, however, many EU members placed the blame squarely on Madrid, whose openness to welcoming migrants is an outlier in a Europe that is becoming increasingly hawkish on the issue.

Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni said Spain’s “uncontrolled immigration” policy was a security threat and suspended the Schengen freedom of movement agreement with Madrid, even though Ceuta is not part of the Schengen area.

Finland and Denmark fell in behind Meloni, while Czech Prime Minister Andrej Babis called for Spain to be suspended from the Schengen agreement.

A day later, they were among the 22 EU members signing a letter to the European Commission expressing concern over the Ceuta crisis.

While member states later toned down their criticisms of Madrid, the dispute flared up again over the weekend – ensuring the EU’s dirty laundry remained on full public display for a third week – after Spain imposed its own border checks on air and sea travel from Italy.

The episode may seem far removed from the EU’s trade tensions with China, but the question of solidarity will underpin Brussels’ ability to confront Beijing this autumn – particularly once China starts retaliating.

After national leaders discussed how to move forward under the weight of a China shock to European industry in June, Commission President Ursula von der Leyen praised their “clear support for a European response based on unity among member states”.

The Ceuta crisis, however, is a reminder of how easily the capitals can be divided when one comes under attack.

“The reaction was not as united as we would expect from a union of states that aims to become a geoeconomic actor,” Rizzi said. “China doesn’t have to win 27 of them over to weaken the negotiating position.”

A central figure in the Ceuta crisis, Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez is also likely to be a major player in any contest with China.

One of Europe’s few remaining socialist leaders, his open-border policies, embrace of renewable energy, and willingness to stand up to US President Donald Trump have made him a poster boy for the global left.

He also stands out for his embrace of Beijing, making an annual visit to China for the last four years. He is also seeking Chinese investment in Spain’s electric vehicle supply chain and was the sole dissenting voice last October, when national leaders called for a tougher EU policy towards China.

“Sanchez was already the outlier arguing China should be seen as a partner, with repeated visits to Beijing, while France pushes ‘European preference’ and Germany remains the pivotal swing state,” said Sari Arho Havren, a China specialist at the Royal United Services Institute.

This, in turn, made it less likely that Sanchez would quietly fall in behind the Brussels-led push for a tougher China policy, she said.

He already has some history in such matters.

In 2024, the Spanish government flipped its vote in favour of EU tariffs on Chinese-made electric vehicles to an abstention, reportedly after Beijing warned investments could stall and threatened tariffs on the country’s pork exports.

“Madrid has just learned that European solidarity is conditional and retrospective. The rational lesson for a government that expects retaliation from China against its exposed sectors in any trade confrontation is to hedge bilaterally,” Arho Havren said.

The Ceuta episode was, she added, “a stress test” for the expected collision with Beijing, which would “depend on whether member states were ready to absorb costs for each other”.

“The answer we received was certainly discouraging,” she said. “When costs land asymmetrically, the majority’s instinct is to declare the exposed state the author of its own misfortune.”

Commission trade officials are trying to convince nervous capitals not to let the fear of consequences block EU-wide action, and they are already working on a “solidarity mechanism” that would compensate any companies targeted in a tit-for-tat trade war.

China has previously shown its willingness to respond asymmetrically to EU gambits.

When Brussels launched an investigation into EV subsidies in 2023, Beijing went after EU pork, brandy and dairy products, while state media carried copious threats of investigations into the aircraft and automotive sectors.

Frenzied lobbying from threatened industries led Germany to vote against the EV duties, but it failed to convince others to join it.

With the commission preparing new instruments and broader trade investigations to deal with what it sees as unfair trade policies, Beijing has once again vowed to respond in kind.

Matters may come to a head in October, when the two sides’ trade chiefs are due to meet and EU national leaders will meet to thrash out a plan.

Analysts believe the member states are more aligned on the need to act – but the Ceuta crisis has diluted belief that they can follow through.

“Being a geopolitical actor means being able to speak and to present yourself as one, and making it understood from the outside that if you attack any one of us, it’s an attack against all of us. If you coerce one of us, we’re going to respond with an entire EU marketwide response,” said Rizzi.

Cracks have appeared with increasing frequency of late, however, as national interest runs up against abstract notions of pan-European solidarity.

For example, when the EU lined up retaliatory tariffs against Trump’s “Liberation Day” duties last year, France – the most vocal supporter of EU strategic autonomy – quickly lobbied for alcohol to be removed to protect its wine and brandy makers.

Beijing’s promised retaliation will examine again whether Europe is willing to pay the price of unity – but the Ceuta affair provides an uncomfortable preview.

“The lack of solidarity towards a member under hybrid attack does not bode well as the EU stands on the brink of a trade war with Beijing,” said Arho Havren.
