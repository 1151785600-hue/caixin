CJ Hendry brings Juju World to Hong Kong with exclusive collectibles

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1537 words | 2026-08-13

The Australian artist Catherine Jenna Hendry, best known to the world as CJ Hendry, first entered the public consciousness through social media, by sharing her hyperrealistic interpretations of luxury items on Instagram. The ink drawings gained Hendry a following of almost a million, and celebrity collectors of her works include Kanye West, Vera Wang, Floyd Mayweather and Pharrell Williams – an impressive feat for someone without formal training or gallery representation.

The New York-based artist has expanded her drawing practice from black and white to colour. But not one to stay confined to a single medium, Hendry has also made waves with her viral plush “flower markets”.

She has also created Juju, a kawaii collectible with floppy ears and a flower for an eye. Running until August 16, Pacific Place is hosting “Juju World”, a fluffy and fun exhibition inspired by the character. Giant Jujus, swings and a chamber filled with 400 plushies await.

There’s also a pop-up store selling iridescent Jujus, created exclusively for Pacific Place. In this line, there are nine standard versions and five additional “secret” versions, which come in limited-edition blind boxes. Visitors can also unlock surprise accessories at the Capsule Toy Station and customise their Juju with interchangeable flower eyes, outfits and more.

At the launch of “Juju World”, we meet an energetic Hendry for a conversation about art, artificial intelligence and what it takes to make it in the industry today.

Describe your routine.

I have studios in Hong Kong and New York, so we’re here every other month. In the studio, every day is different. With three children aged between one and five, it’s mostly chaos. We are just piecing it together and there’s a lot we want to create and achieve, and that comes a little bit chaotically sometimes.

How has that influenced your art?

A lot of my artistic concepts are childlike and have a lot of whimsy. I’m not sure if that’s because I have children and I’m surrounded by childlike wonder, but creating ideas that appeal to both adults and kids is what I want to do. Sometimes, there’s no rhyme or reason to it.

You’ve tackled various mediums. Is there a thread that ties them all together?

Over the past three years, I have started to use this very feminine flower shape. Because I had so many unusual, different concepts, the flower shape allowed me to connect them all together. That has become a branding moment for everything. Even with the Juju logo, there’s a flower on top of the letter J.

Why the pivot from ink drawings to the kawaii collectibles realm?

My drawing practice is very labour-intensive, focused and intimate. I was finding it frustrating because those works are only available to a few people, as they are expensive and hard to come by. I was uncomfortable with that. I still draw, but maybe one-tenth of the time I used to. It has allowed me to spend time creating these other, kooky concepts, such as Juju and the flower markets.

I’ve created these concepts so that more people can appreciate and experience them. You don’t need money to appreciate the flower market: people can access it for free and even their first flower is free. Juju is not necessarily the same, but this fun, friendly character is appealing to many.

How would you explain Juju?

Juju is a fun character with a flower over their eye and no mouth, so they can’t talk back. They are a character that can take you through the happiest of times and the darkest of times, one who is with you no matter what.

How did Juju come about?

Juju’s name comes from my mother. Her name is Judy and my kids call her Juju. My mum is a very elegant lady who wears all white. So the rarest Jujus are always white.

I used AI to help design Juju in early 2025. AI is interesting because everyone kind of belittles it. But as an artist, it’s important to use different tools. I used AI for a very small portion of the design process.

AI is controversial, especially in art because it draws from existing works. Tell us more about your position on it

AI can be used very cleverly, and it can be used very poorly. It depends on what you want to do and the story you want to tell. Anyone can design anything in AI in seconds, but designing something and then making it a reality is a totally different skill set. My artistic process is more in bringing it to life and rolling it out.

I’ve been an artist for 15 years and I’ve been plagiarised for much of that time. So, I’ve become confident enough in my practice to know that no ideas are original, even before AI. Everyone’s referencing everyone else. AI is just speeding that up.

Art is all about collaboration and moving forward. If your entire practice is based on AI, I don’t think you’ll get very far. Mine is based pretty much entirely on design and creativity and using AI only for very small parts.

So, in the age of AI, do you see the role of an artist as more of a curator of sorts?

Yes, absolutely. It’s important to have your own voice. You can use AI to perhaps start a concept, but to build and develop that takes a lot of energy and thought.

Because I’ve been doing this for so long, I feel very comfortable talking about AI. People want to pretend it’s not there. But I’m like, “It’s here, you’ve got to go with it.” I’ve used AI and I will continue to use AI.

Building real-life experiences is so important to me. Like with “Juju World” in Pacific Place, for example, you can only buy these Jujus in person. I’ve purposely taken this offline. Also, we haven’t made many of them. So, when they sell out, they sell out. They’ll never be made again. We never revisit it and we never will.

Like Takashi Murakami, Kaws and others, you seem instinctively aware of how social media, branding and merchandising works. How important is that for an artist today?

It’s hard to be an artist nowadays. Everyone pines over the old days, but they are not coming back. Technology is a beautiful tool, if you can use it in a clever way. But because everyone has social media, it has become oversaturated. I’m interested in in-person experiences. When I build concepts and installations, it’s important to create something unique for people to experience.

That said, I also think there are so many ways to be an artist. I see my job as creating and starting discussions. I’m a commercially successful artist, but that’s not for everyone. Not everyone wants to have merch at their art exhibition. Some people just want to have one extraordinary painting that they’ve taken two years to make. That’s special and important. It depends on the artist you want to be. I’m just one type of artist. Art comes in so many ways, in so many voices.

Who inspires you?

I don’t think, for me, there are people who are inspirations. Like I said before, nothing is original any more. Everything’s being built on something else.

I’ve always interpreted my concepts off other things, blended them, meshed them, reinterpreted them in my own voice, in my own way. Actually, a lot of my ideas come from runway shows, which are extraordinarily inspiring in the way they bring together worlds to showcase concepts and clothing.

When we launched Juju at Phillips West Kowloon (in late 2025), a lot of that inspiration came from the Chanel runway show at the Grand Palais, in Paris, where they did it like a Chanel supermarket.

What do you want people to take away from this particular exhibition?

I’ve always been pretty open with the fact that when I build ideas and exhibitions, I don’t expect anything from anyone. That’s not my job. I believe, as an artist, you’re meant to build something and what people experience is none of my business. A lot of artists might put a plaque on the wall explaining the idea, but I’ve never done that and I never will. I build the idea and people can come and experience it however they want.

What’s next for you?

Gosh, I genuinely don’t know. It’s too hard to predict the future. My art could crash and burn, or I could reach the stars. You never know.

What I do know is that I’m relatively creative. I have a lot of energy, a lot of excitement and a lot of really kooky ideas. I love executing, building and creating. There’s no shortage of ideas. I just love working with my team to come up with stupid stuff all the time.
