From fishing boats to Michelin stars: Vietnam’s CoCo Dining turns the humble into haute cuisine

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2230 words | 2026-10-16

“You know the phrase ‘fish out of water’? Well, that’s me in the city,” chef Vo Thanh Vuong laughs. “Now, I’m back on the water.”

It’s just past dawn; we’re on a fishing boat off the coast of Vung Tau, roughly 100km (62 miles) southeast of Vietnam’s largest city, Ho Chi Minh. Vung Tau is the port that supplies Ho Chi Minh City’s best restaurants, but by the fleet’s clock, it’s already late; most of the other vessels have already unloaded their catch. Vuong is showing me how to hold a fishing rod. His hands seem to know the motion better than his mind does.

Vuong grew up in Phan Thiet, about 160km (100 miles) north along the coast, where his family’s livelihood depended on what the South China Sea had to offer. Vung Tau is where his family’s boat went down in 2000, when he was 11 years old, 40km (25 miles) off its coast. Losing the watercraft cost them dearly, and suddenly, every pair of hands mattered – including his. Vuong was fishing full-time until he was 20, long enough to know the fishing life was not for him.

So, he left for Ho Chi Minh City, also known as Saigon.

Now, almost two decades later, he is back on the water – not because he has to be, but because he wants to be.

By mid-morning, we have caught almost nothing. Vuong shrugs. He tells me this is what most trips are like: long stretches of nothing punctuated by work. Because “the sea gave you very little entertainment, you learned to be comfortable with silence”.

Back on shore, we head to a local fish market in Vung Tau to pick up something for lunch. Everything is laid out at knee-height. Vuong moves through the displays like he’s reading a familiar book. He picks up a big fish with one hand and turns it over. He doesn’t wince or fidget the way many people do when handling something slippery and cold. He checks the gills, the eyes, the firmness and sets it back down – or keeps it, and moves on.

“When an ingredient is truly good, you don’t need to do much to it. Sometimes, the best thing a cook can do is step out of the way.”

In Vung Tau, he cooks a meal from the catch of the day. “Do you want to see the fisherman or the chef cook today,” he asks. He explains that the fisherman cooks fast, because at sea, speed is survival: you cook to eat and move on. But the chef cooks slower, because he has learned to enjoy the process – the prepping, the seasoning, the tasting, the small adjustments.

We tell him we want both. He laughs and gets to work.

His preparation is simple, and he hums as he cooks – the way he did as the youngest boy in his family, with two brothers and a sister. Several gas stoves are burning at once. He chops the garnishes with precision and seasons the braising liquid, gauging measurements by eye.

He steams a mix of flower crabs and three-spot swimming crabs with beer. He sets a hotpot going with tamarind, pineapple and tomato, and portions the cobia into chunks. He treats the oriental trumpeter whiting like a ceviche: sliced thin, cured in lime juice for 15 minutes, then tossed with a home-made sweet-spicy sauce, raw onion and herbs – the way he used to make it on the boats.

On those trips, food was whatever the sea and the hold allowed. Rice and instant noodles were the foundation, and they carried mam (fermented-seafood paste), nuoc mam (fish sauce), dried fish and squid, pickled vegetables, eggs and water. A good meal might be muc com – baby squid with its roe – with sour fish soup and pickles. “We would spend the whole day catching fish for other people,” he recalls. “At night, we’d sit together and argue about which fish was best for ourselves.”

Some nights, the swells were heavy enough that seawater splashed into their rice bowls; the rice would turn salty, but they would look at each other, laugh and keep eating. “You didn’t complain,” he says. “You were hungry, and you were at sea.”

And the night the power died and the charcoal got too wet to burn, they ate a freshly caught grouper raw on deck – bled, sliced thin, with wild lime and sea salt, similar to what he prepares for us.

He serves the food the way he always does: everyone else first, himself last. It’s what he calls a staff meal, the food he makes for his team at CoCo Dining – his one-Michelin-star Vietnamese restaurant in Ho Chi Minh City, opened in 2021 – after service.

What he has prepared for us today is not, by his own admission, the food that made his name – that food was French. When Vuong left fishing behind and enrolled in culinary school in Ho Chi Minh City, he didn’t go in with a plan. He had no kitchen training and no technique. He also had no language for what he was learning.

What he did have was nine years at sea. At the time, that felt like something to hide. “I didn’t want to expose my past,” he says, recalling his inexperience. “I had self-esteem issues.”

Vuong didn’t finish Grade 6 and, later, quit culinary school. He became a chef by observing the people around him, figuring things out along the way. He spent a decade training under French-Cambodian culinarian Sakal Phoeung and learned to cook in the French tradition.

In Vietnam, that still carries a particular weight: post-colonisation, French cuisine remains the benchmark for fine dining. For a Vietnamese chef with no pedigree, no formal training and a past aboard fishing boats, French haute cuisine was how Vuong proved he belonged.

“I simply didn’t see [Vietnamese cuisine] as something I needed to learn,” he recalls. “It was everyday food, so I didn’t think about it as a cuisine I could build a career around.”

So, Vuong learned to cook French, well enough to find success with it. For years, that was his identity in the kitchen. It even won him the first prize on the televised cooking competition Top Chef Vietnam in 2019. From 2021 to 2023, he was changing the menu at CoCo Dining twice a year, working mainly with modern gastronomy and fermentation. Eventually, he felt like he was running out of ideas.

One night, towards the end of 2023, he and Daniel Dang Hung Minh – CoCo Dining’s managing director – were drinking together, unwinding after another week of trying to solve a menu that wouldn’t come together. Vuong started cooking just for the two of them, preparing the kind of Vietnamese drinking food he would naturally make for himself. After tasting it, Dang said: “This tastes much more like you; this is where you’re strongest. Why don’t you just be honest with your cooking?”

Vuong resisted. He thought, “No, this can’t be the direction of a fine-dining restaurant.” He had spent years learning modern techniques and developing himself as a chef. Going back to familiar food almost felt like going backwards.

But what Dang had said stayed with him, so Vuong started experimenting secretly at home. “Maybe I had been trying too hard to become the chef I thought I was supposed to be,” he says, “instead of being honest about the chef I actually was.”

In 2024, CoCo Dining served a menu called Ra Khoi – meaning “to set sail” – but for Vuong, it signified “a return to the source”, as the first tasting menu he designed after deciding to lean into his fishing heritage. His first version of the menu pulled together fragments of memories: the mam muc (fermented-squid paste) he used to eat on the boat became stuffed baby squid with fermented-squid sauce. A wind-dried prawn reminded him of how his family preserved seafood, so he worked with tiger prawn. A simple crab salad became blue crab with mustard greens.

Never before had Vuong put his childhood on a plate on purpose. Last year, the restaurant earned its Michelin star. It turns out, the fisherman and the chef could coexist after all.

“I wasn’t sitting there thinking to myself, ‘I’m going to make a menu about my childhood.’ I was just following one memory into another, and suddenly, there was a menu,” he says.

Back in Ho Chi Minh City, Vuong brings out his oldest jar of mam chao (fermented fish) from the fermentation room at CoCo Dining. A whiff of it brings us back to the market of Vung Tau. The fermented fish is threadfin bream, sun-dried for five days in Phan Thiet by his family, who have been fishing and making mam for generations. At the restaurant, he and his team mix it with a starter made from sticky rice, layer it in a jar and seal it tightly. Usually, it’s ready after a month, but he lets his develop for at least two. “We learned not to waste anything,” he says. “Even the fish that wasn’t going to be sold had a purpose.”

Vuong believes his role is to bring the ingredients to life again: “In a museum, you put an artefact behind glass to stop it from changing. In my kitchen, the things I want to preserve are alive: they ferment, they age, they dry and they continue to change.”

It’s what makes his “archive” different from a museum’s. Recipes can be written down and “collected” and remain the same for generations, but his fermentations live and breathe. “You can’t treat each batch exactly the same,” he says. “You have to observe it, smell it, taste it and understand how it is developing.”

At CoCo Dining, Vuong balances the deep umami of his fermented seafood with ginger and other aromatics. In his signature dish, baby squid is stuffed with squid trimmings and pork, then glazed with a sauce made from squid ink and a spoonful of the mam muc. If diners are curious, he brings out a jar so they can taste the fermented-squid paste as it is.

“For me, that’s the heart of my ‘Living Archive’. If you put something in a box and never touch it, eventually, it becomes history. If you cook it, share it and pass it to someone else, it continues to live,” he explains.

His menu, Luu Tich (“Living Archive”), arrives at a moment when what deserves to be preserved and presented as part of Vietnamese culinary heritage is under debate. Vietnam is bidding to become the next global gastronomy hub, with a pitch built on “luxury” through authenticity rather than European-style fine dining. Vuong is cautious about the language. He has spent too long on the other side of the word “authentic” to trust it easily.

Real necessity informed those techniques, he says. “Coming from a fishing boat off Phan Thiet, the word ‘luxury’ felt very far from my life. But there was a lot of knowledge in those practices.”

“Vietnam doesn’t need to borrow someone else’s definition of luxury”

Vo Thanh Vuong

For him, that knowledge of salt, patience and time is its own form of wealth. “Vietnam doesn’t need to borrow someone else’s definition of luxury,” Vuong says. He thinks that luxury today can be the time and attention we give to something that was once simply part of daily life, “not taking something humble and making it expensive just because we put it on a fine-dining plate”.

But that doesn’t mean he cooks the same way his grandmother did. In Vuong’s eyes, French technique is merely another set of tools – just as sorting and scaling fish is part of a fisherman’s toolkit. Both, he says, come from the same place: trying to understand an ingredient and bring out the best in it.

But does that make a dish less Vietnamese?

“If authenticity means following a recipe strictly without any changes, maybe some will feel that what we do is not authentic enough,” he says. “I’m not trying to recreate the past exactly. A dish doesn’t become inauthentic because it evolves. It becomes inauthentic only when you lose sight of where it came from.”

If he were to curate a museum piece for people 100 years from now to understand where he came from, he would choose a thung leu, the wooden barrel used to make fish sauce in Phan Thiet. His grandfather used to make fish sauce at home, and the whole area of Phan Thiet, Vuong remembers, smelled like fish sauce. “Now, Phan Thiet is much more modern; that smell has mostly disappeared,” he says. “Not many households make fish sauce at home any more, so something that was once part of everyday life has become much less visible.”

In the museum, the label for the exhibit would read: “Thung Leu, Phan Thiet, Vietnam – a wooden vessel for making nuoc mam. For one boy from the coast, it was simply part of home. Inside it are fish, salt and time. Around it is the knowledge of generations who learned how to live with the sea.”
