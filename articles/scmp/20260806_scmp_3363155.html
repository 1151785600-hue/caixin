Why the UK is doubling down on Asean ties amid European reset

SCMP | FREE | 1154 words | 2026-08-06

Britain’s new Labour government is trying to reassure Southeast Asia that its push to rebuild ties with Europe will not come at the region’s expense, analysts have said, after Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband made an Asean meeting in Manila his first overseas stop.

Appointed by Prime Minister Andy Burnham on July 20, Miliband attended the Association of Southeast Asian Nations Foreign Ministers’ Meeting about 48 hours after taking office – a choice observers said pointed less to a policy reset than to continuity in Britain’s post-Brexit engagement with the bloc.

Analysts said the visit also highlighted why Asean had become more important to London: trade and supply-chain links after Brexit, maritime and regional security, and a shared interest among middle powers in keeping diplomatic space open amid intensifying great-power pressure.

The Manila gathering also marked five years since the United Kingdom became a full dialogue partner of the bloc.

“I felt it was very, very important to come straight to the Asean Foreign Ministers’ Meeting as a sign of solidarity with Asean countries and of the importance we attach to this partnership,” Miliband said in remarks before the bloc’s foreign ministers.

He stressed his country’s “commitment to Asean and commitment for the long term”, which he said remained “just as strong for the UK’s new prime minister as it did for our previous prime minister and for me personally”.

The trip also saw the adoption of a new plan of action between the UK and Asean, which Miliband said would “guide our partnership through the next five years” through 2031, alongside a joint statement on the Asean Outlook on the Indo-Pacific that “reaffirms our commitment to upholding international law and addressing mutual challenges”.

“Whether on maritime security, energy security, economic resilience or emerging technologies, our future will increasingly be shaped by how we work together multilaterally,” Miliband said.

For Laura Southgate, a lecturer in politics and international relations at Aston University, the timing and destination of Miliband’s trip mattered as much as the official agenda.

“Ed Miliband’s presence at the Asean Foreign Ministers’ Meeting is an early indication of the priority the new Burnham government is attaching to Southeast Asia and the wider Indo-Pacific,” Southgate said.

“Miliband himself confirmed an ongoing commitment to Asean and its centrality, whilst also pledging deeper engagement moving forward.”

His decision to make the Asean meeting his first overseas visit was “a deliberate signal of this commitment”, Southgate said, but also suggested “continuity with the previous government rather than a complete change in UK policy”.

Bill Hayton, an associate fellow with Chatham House’s Asia Programme, said the visit reflected Britain’s view that it shared common ground with Asean as a grouping of middle powers navigating a more uncertain global environment.

“The UK wants to demonstrate our international presence, not just being caught up with the EU,” Hayton said.

That balancing act has become more important because Britain’s relationship with Asean is now part of its attempt to show that closer ties with Europe do not mean retreating from the Indo-Pacific.

The UK became an Asean dialogue partner in 2021, a status that formalised its engagement with the bloc after Britain left the EU and sought a broader “Global Britain” role.

Observers said Brexit made strengthening relations with Southeast Asia a greater priority, though it was not the only reason for London’s approach.

“The UK had already begun strengthening relations with Southeast Asia before 2016, but Brexit added urgency by requiring a reassessment of British foreign policy and encouraging a more outward-facing ‘Global Britain’ strategy,” Southgate said.

Consequently, Asean and the wider Indo-Pacific became more important to the UK’s economic, security and global ambitions, she said, including through closer trade ties, membership of the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP) and Asean dialogue partner status.

“However, Brexit accelerated rather than created this engagement,” she said. “The UK’s attention and resources remain divided between the Indo-Pacific and its commitments in Europe.”

Brexit remained “a big shaper” of Britain’s foreign policy because “we’re on our own now, so we have to craft much more of our own foreign policy”, Hayton said.

“Our trade policy is now entirely independent … Now that we are members of the CPTPP, we have an economic interest in closer ties with the other CPTPP members.”

The previous Conservative government had turned away from the EU and bet heavily on the Asia-Pacific region, Hayton said, while Burnham’s first days in office showed a clear intent to repair European ties without abandoning that wider focus.

Downing Street said Burnham used early calls with European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and European Council President Antonio Costa to stress the importance of a closer UK-EU relationship, including work towards a summit later this year.

“This government wants to rebuild its relations with the EU and Europe, but it’s keen to show that that doesn’t mean giving up on Asia,” Hayton said.

“They are still engaged, and the fact that the foreign secretary, just two or three days into office, still wanted to get to the AMM summit reflects that – they want to be part of it.”

Middle-power ties

At the same time, Britain’s engagement with Asean is increasingly shaped by its position as a middle power, even as its former colonial role continues to influence relations with parts of Southeast Asia, analysts say.

Southgate pointed out that the UK maintained strong bilateral ties with former British colonies such as Malaysia and Singapore, both Commonwealth members, and retained a regional footprint through the Five Power Defence Arrangements and a permanent military garrison in Brunei.

“However, its position as a middle power means that engagement now depends more on consultation, partnership and practical cooperation than on historical influence,” she said.

“It is therefore important that the UK remains sensitive to its colonial history and respects Asean’s priorities, agency and centrality.”

Miliband’s Asean meeting showed the UK viewed the bloc not only through strategic interests but also as a partner in upholding multilateralism and international law, Southgate said.

“The UK identified Asean as a critical strategic partner in 2021 because of its location, economic growth and integration, and role in regional stability and diplomacy,” she said.

At a time of conflict in the Middle East and Ukraine, alongside wider geopolitical and economic uncertainty, Southgate said Asean’s role in supporting dialogue, regional stability and resilient supply chains had become increasingly important.

Hayton said Britain was presenting itself as a middle power that could support multilateral ties and help preserve Southeast Asian autonomy amid pressure from larger powers, including China.

“We see Asean as an important regional organisation – not the only one you can interact with, but one of the more effective ones in the sense that it has such important convening power,” he said.
