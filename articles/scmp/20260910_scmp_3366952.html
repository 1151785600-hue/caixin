Japan’s protest over Russian soldier statue aggravates ‘summer of tension’

SCMP | FREE | 1081 words | 2026-09-10

Russia has rebuffed a strongly worded complaint from Japan about the unveiling of a statue in the city of Khabarovsk depicting a Soviet soldier smashing an imperial-era border marker bearing the emblem of the Japanese emperor, with analysts suggesting the bilateral relations will inevitably remain chilly.

The 15-metre granite monument to the Soviet Union’s victory over Japan in 1945 shows a soldier using the butt of his rifle to shatter the border post, identical to the former markers installed along the 50th parallel on Sakhalin Island to mark the boundary between the then Japanese-controlled southern section of the island north of Japan and the Russian-held north.

The Soviet Union took back control of southern Sakhalin near the end of World War II, and Japan renounced its claims to the territory in 1951.

A 16-petalled chrysanthemum motif, the symbol of the emperor, is depicted on the statue. Its unveiling last week went ahead despite Tokyo’s requests for an amendment through diplomatic channels, with its foreign ministry describing the statue as “extremely regrettable”.

In a statement, Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi said she had also requested that the statue be removed, adding: “The Chrysanthemum Crest is widely recognised as a symbol of Japan, and it is completely unacceptable to erect a monument depicting its destruction.”

Speaking at a press conference in Moscow on Tuesday, Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov made it clear that Tokyo’s protests would be ignored.

“It is difficult to say what they have forgotten and what they remember in Tokyo, but at least we have not forgotten,” the Tass news agency quoted Peskov as saying. “We have not forgotten Japanese militarism and the victory we achieved over it.”

James Brown, a professor of international relations specialising in Russian affairs at the Tokyo campus of Temple University, noted “a summer of real tension between Japan and Russia”.

The two sides have been at loggerheads since Japan imposed sanctions on Russia over its invasion of Ukraine.

Relations were further rocked earlier this year when Tokyo accused a former official of the Russian trade delegation in Japan of espionage. This was followed by Russian warships and aircraft operating in areas close to Japan, including taking part in exercises with Chinese units.

Russian President Vladimir Putin also visited Iturup, north of Hokkaido, in August. The Japanese foreign ministry and Takaichi criticised the visit to the island, which Tokyo calls Etorofu. Moscow also ignored this protest.

Etorofu is part of the four southern Kuril Islands chain off Hokkaido that Soviet forces seized after Japan’s surrender in 1945. Japan continues to claim the islands as its Northern Territories, and the dispute has prevented Tokyo and Moscow from signing a peace treaty to end their World War II hostilities.

“This is especially awkward for Takaichi,” Brown said. “She is supposed to be a nationalist, a hawk, but Putin has visited what she sees as sovereign Japanese territory. Now a statue has been unveiled showing the smashing of the imperial chrysanthemum, and she is not able to respond in a meaningful way.”

Takaichi could not do much about these issues in part due to concerns over energy supplies, Brown added. With the ongoing conflict in the Middle East and rising oil prices, Japan is keen to continue having access to energy supplies from Sakhalin, despite its diversification efforts.

“There have been complaints through diplomatic channels, but nothing further,” Brown said. “It’s just empty rhetoric.”

Conservatives were infuriated at Putin’s visit to the Northern Territories, with Kiyofumi Iwata, former chief of staff of the Japan Ground Self-Defence Forces, slamming the Russian leader in an opinion article published by the Japan Institute for National Fundamentals, a right-wing think tank.

Iwata called it “an outrageous action designed to justify the former Soviet Union’s entry into the war against Japan in violation of the Japan-Soviet neutrality treaty [signed in 1941] and its illegal occupation of the Japanese territories after the war”.

He was also scathing about Takaichi’s response. “The Japanese government’s response to the Russian action that openly trampled on Japan’s sovereignty and public sentiment was limited to verbal protests.”

Calling for “deterrence through action”, Iwata urged steps by Japan to “make Russia feel pain”, such as by providing Ukraine with air defence weapons and additional military technology, including jointly developing drone capabilities.

Ben Ascione, an assistant professor of politics and international relations at Tokyo’s Waseda University, suggested that after taking criticism from allies as well as the opposition, the media and the Japanese public, Takaichi might feel she had to be more resolute.

“The statue showing the imperial chrysanthemum being smashed is extremely provocative,” Ascione said.

“The emperor and the imperial family are held in very high regard here. Conservatives expect her to be tough on territorial issues and protect the dignity of the imperial family, and we have had two hot-button issues in a short space of time.”

Failing to take a firmer line could see some of her supporters shifting further to the right and backing Sanseito, an even more conservative party than Takaichi’s Liberal Democratic Party, which would damage her standing and electoral prospects.

Andrei Lankov, a Russian-born professor of history and international relations at Seoul’s Kookmin University, said both leaders were being driven by domestic political concerns, with Putin facing a legislative election later this month.

“Both leaders need to please a domestic audience. Pushing a line that a leader is tough is always popular, especially when it does not cost the taxpayer anything,” Lankov said.

“I do not think there is any amount of protest that Japan can make that will change how Russia represents World War II. That is especially the case now as Russia emphasises victory over Japan at the same time as it is improving relations with North Korea and China.”

Brown said that any lingering hopes that Moscow and Tokyo might come to some agreement on joint development of the southern Kuril Islands – as appeared possible when former prime minister Shinzo Abe met Putin on several occasions a decade ago – had vanished.

“This must be a major disappointment to Russia because even this year it had hoped that it would be able to make progress on this issue with Takaichi, in part because she was Abe’s protege,” he said.

“But as is so often the way with Russia, they pushed harder when they detected softness and that very quickly killed off any engagement on the issue.”
