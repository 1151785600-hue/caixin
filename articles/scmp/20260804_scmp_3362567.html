Cambodia and Thailand must define the border to achieve lasting peace

SCMP | FREE | 1011 words | 2026-08-04

Cambodia has adopted a comprehensive approach to resolving its border dispute with Thailand, yet substantive progress will only occur once the two sides agree on where the border actually is.

The Cambodia-Thailand border dispute is complex: the result of a tangle of colonial-era treaties, competing maps, contested temples, unsettled demarcation and recurring nationalism. The rise of ultranationalist groups on both sides has made reconciliation harder still. Armed clashes, partly driven by domestic political developments, erupted in July and December 2025, showing how quickly the dispute can turn violent and how costly that violence can be for both economies.

From Phnom Penh’s perspective, the immediate priority is pressing Thailand to withdraw its troops while rejecting attempts to redraw borders through force, coercion or fait accompli. Cambodia’s approach rests on three tracks: bilateral mechanisms, international legal processes and third-party mediation. For any of these to deliver results, however, both sides must first settle demarcation itself.

1. Bilateral negotiation. The first track runs through existing mechanisms. These include the Joint Boundary Commission (JBC), established in 2000 under a memorandum of understanding on land boundary survey and demarcation. The JBC is the main technical body for survey, mapping and demarcation, working alongside the General Border Committee on security and military matters and the Regional Border Committee on local incidents.

In April, Prime Minister Hun Manet stated that “even if only 1 per cent” of diplomatic opportunity remained, Cambodia was “committed to fully exhausting all peaceful mechanisms rather than resorting to prolonged conflict”. Existing mechanisms are often slow-moving and constrained by domestic political pressures, yet they remain indispensable for confidence-building, preventive diplomacy and the pursuit of practical technical solutions.

2. International law. The second track concerns international legal processes. Cambodia’s approach to border demarcation centres on the 1904 and 1907 Franco-Siamese instruments and the maps drawn from them. In 1962, the International Court of Justice ruled that the Preah Vihear Temple lay within Cambodian territory; in 2013, it clarified that Cambodia had sovereignty over the whole promontory. These rulings continue to shape Phnom Penh’s strategic thinking, reinforcing its belief that international adjudication – though time-consuming – can provide legal solutions where bilateral talks are blocked by nationalism, political shifts or competing historical narratives.

Last year’s renewed border tensions hardened this logic. In May 2025, a Cambodian soldier was killed and disputes escalated around several contested areas. The following month, Cambodia brought a case before the ICJ concerning the Mom Bei area and the Ta Moan Thom, Ta Moan Touch and Ta Krabey temples. Thailand rejected the court’s jurisdiction, arguing that submission would undermine its sovereignty and territorial integrity.

A parallel legal track has opened at sea. After Thailand terminated an MOU on overlapping maritime claims in the Gulf of Thailand this May, Phnom Penh invoked compulsory conciliation under the 1982 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea and Bangkok agreed to participate in the process. Conciliation under Unclos is designed to produce recommendations rather than binding rulings, but it still serves an important purpose by internationalising the issue, creating structure, reducing the risk of unilateral action and subjecting both parties to greater diplomatic scrutiny.

3. Third-party mediation. The third track is mediation. Cambodia has sought support from Asean, China, the United States and others during periods of armed conflict. As last year’s chair of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, Malaysia played a crucial role in facilitating ceasefire efforts, with Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim’s mediation helping to create diplomatic space when direct contact between Phnom Penh and Bangkok had broken down. Beijing and Washington lent the process strategic weight, with US President Donald Trump actively involved, in particular.

The July 2025 ceasefire showed the effectiveness of coordinated diplomacy. It was followed by the Kuala Lumpur peace accord that October, which, among other things, saw the two sides agree to de-escalation and confidence-building. But these agreements failed to prevent a second round of armed conflict largely due to the lack of credible monitoring, verification and enforcement. Fighting resumed in December on a much larger scale, with the conflict lasting 21 days and displacing more than half a million people.

China’s subsequent engagement was decisive. Both Cambodia and Thailand accepted China’s role as a mediator and the China-led trilateral meeting in Yunnan that December, also known as the Fuxian Consensus, helped restore the ceasefire through quiet diplomacy and high-level strategic engagement. Beijing also offered to provide a platform for both sides to engage in more comprehensive and effective communication and trust-building, including through track-two diplomacy, while providing parallel humanitarian aid to communities affected by the conflict and supporting demining efforts along the border.

What next?

Cambodia’s approach combines bilateral and multilateral frameworks while placing primary emphasis on bilateral settlement mechanisms. Phnom Penh has sent multiple diplomatic notes calling for a special JBC session and the deployment of joint survey teams to resume fieldwork. Bangkok has requested repeated postponements, however, citing internal procedures, elections, the appointment of a new prime minister, cabinet formation and the need to approve a new JBC leadership structure. Thailand has also shown little enthusiasm for Cambodia’s decision to initiate compulsory conciliation.

Moving forward, political will, good faith, reliable hotline communication and confidence-building measures are essential to creating a solid foundation for bilateral negotiations. There are some positive signs of a revival in bilateral negotiations, which makes establishing stable and effective channels of communication between the two capitals all the more urgent. Cooperation against online scams should be further strengthened, while people-to-people exchanges and economic ties ought to be restored and gradually normalised.

But cooperation on scams and tourism cannot substitute for progress on the border issue. Until the JBC resumes substantive boundary surveys – and the disputed temples and maritime zones move from contention to formal demarcation – the current ceasefire, like the ones before it, will remain tenuous and fragile rather than permanent.
