Tsui Ao-lung’s journey from airport worker to Hong Kong fashion champion

SCMP | FREE | 1463 words | 2026-10-07

Tsui Ao-lung did not walk a conventional path to becoming Hong Kong’s newest fashion-design champion. He has no formal training in garment design and he spent three years away from the industry altogether, earning an income moving cargo at the Hong Kong International Airport and waiting restaurant tables to pay off debts from a T-shirt brand he had co-founded. When he finally returned to the craft last year, it was almost by accident: a stylist had asked him to create stage outfits for a concert, so he taught himself how to design for the stage.

It was a circuitous route that led him, on September 5, to claim the top prize at the Hong Kong Young Fashion Designers’ Contest 2026 (YDC), a highlight of the annual Centrestage showcase, itself a flagship event of the third Hong Kong Fashion Fest, organised by the Hong Kong Trade Development Council. Competing against nine other finalists under the theme “You”, the 29-year-old took home the HK$60,000 cash prize, champion title and an overseas internship opportunity sponsored by knitwear manufacturing firm Fang Brothers Knitting.

His winning collection, “The Tide”, draws its name and mood from “Kuang Chao”, a 1970s Cantopop hit by Hong Kong singer Susanna Kwan Kuk-ying. Tsui used the song as a springboard to dive into the journey of a woman’s life across four seasons, capturing her highs, lows and the experience of existence in four hand-stitched pieces.

However, what the judges and the audience could not have anticipated, when the designer’s creations strutted down the catwalk, is that Tsui had let his exacting nature take the wheel, scrapping and overhauling entire looks in the days before the show, with the final touches to his pieces added right before he had to present them. A real-life Project Runway – Hong Kong edition, that is.

We talk to Tsui about winning the competition, his three years away from design, his ambition to eventually launch his own brand again and how he thinks he can work 18 hours a day without ever feeling tired.

What motivated you to enter YDC this year?

It was to tell stories, to express things I wanted to say. I think YDC is a great opportunity. It has a good platform and a complete system that lets people from all walks of life see your work. I never thought of taking part before, because I wanted to try and rely on myself, to strive on my own. But then, at a certain point, I thought, “Since the opportunity is there, why not give it a shot?”

How did it feel to win the contest?

I felt happy, very happy, but also relieved. So many people were screaming. I worked long shifts leading up to the finale. After that, I slept for hours.

What was your biggest challenge?

I used to do casual wear – simple clothes. YDC was my first time doing fittings, organising so many outfits, shoes and so on – I felt like I couldn’t handle it all. When I finished the four designs, I was very unsatisfied. So, I kept tweaking them, completely redoing some of the looks from scratch, and I ended up remaking the pieces I wasn’t happy with after the fittings, working on them up to the last minute before I had to leave for the show.

What was your workspace like?

I actually went back and forth to Guangzhou to make the clothes. A lot of suppliers are concentrated there, so if I needed materials or components, I could buy them straight away. Because I don’t have my own studio, I worked out of a shared “central sample room” – a space used by several shops. I didn’t have a fixed area on the premises, so whenever a space opened up, I’d drag my dress dummy and tools over there to work. Most of the time, I’d wait until late at night, after everyone else had left and the lights were off, so I could spread all my stuff out and sew in peace, staying until 4am or 5am. It took a lot of patience, and I kept pricking my fingers, but that’s how I got it done. Many friends were helping me. I didn’t ask them to, and I never shared what I was making with anyone. But in the last two weeks, they came to the fittings and helped me. I am so grateful.

How did you arrive at the core theme for your winning collection?

I often refer to music and films to capture a mood or feeling. For “The Tide”, the theme came from a Cantopop song called “Kuang Chao”. I came across it and was inspired to take that mood and expand on it. Since the song is about ups and downs, I thought about leaning into a woman’s perspective to explore the cycles of her life, moving through spring, summer, autumn and winter. I also wanted to connect it to the city – you’ll find elements of Hong Kong hidden in the clothes. Whether it’s a person or a place, there will always be moments of glory, but also moments of decline. It’s just as the lyrics go, “It’s them; it is also you and me”.

What was the most important quality you wanted to showcase?

For me, it’s all about the handiwork and hand-stitched details – they really make up the core of the whole collection. Having that focus on craftsmanship gives the collection a natural, cohesive feel.

What first got you into making clothes?

It was a total accident. After secondary school, a friend – my former business partner – had a group of friends who were really passionate about making clothes and starting brands, so I tagged along to join the fun. Back then, our mindset was purely about having a good time and experimenting together. “You make one for me to wear, I make one for you to wear” – that kind of thing. Slowly, everyone else stopped making clothes and abandoned dressmaking. I’m the only one left.

You spent three years away from designing. What was that period like?

During those three years, I actually prepared a lot; I mapped out plans for the next five seasons. I specifically avoided taking on design work because I’m uncompromising – I didn’t want to enter a system of creating commercial filler to make money. I waited tables and moved goods at the airport to make ends meet. Ironically, even while trying hard to avoid seeing them, I’d still spot people wearing clothes from my previous brand on their way to work. I really want to start a brand again, but I know one thing: you have to be ready before you do it. Once you come out, you have to be confident, professional, strong. You can’t just do it half-heartedly.

Describe your creative process.

First, I think of a story. When we actually start making the clothes, we often look at the fabric first and then build on the basic concept, step by step. I write a narrative, and then build the storyline, block by block, although whether it turns out the way I originally intended is a different matter.

How does Hong Kong shape your designs?

I watch a lot of concerts; they often inspire my work. For example, the second look in my collection, the cheongsam – nobody guessed that the inspiration came from one of Eason Chan Yick-shun’s concerts, where he happened to be wearing a belt styled like a corset. I pull inspiration from plenty of other concert looks, too, though I never copy them directly; I just use them as a starting point.

What kind of designer do you want to be?

I’m open to pretty much anything. My advantage, or maybe my flaw, is that I can see the positives in any style, so I don’t mind doing whatever type. But I’m more drawn to pieces with character. I’ll mostly stick to a story-driven line. I want to tell a story naturally, with relevant clothing elements to package it, while keeping my own touch.

What’s your ideal way of working?

I hope it’s one where I don’t have to rest, because I don’t like being inactive. I did YDC and worked 18-hour days – eating just one late-night meal a day – and had absolutely no problem with it. I didn’t feel tired. I can work straight through the night. It’s not just passion – my body’s simply wired that way. It’s like an engine: once it starts, it runs non-stop. Once I start working, I can’t stop.
