Word on the StrEAT: 6 Hong Kong chefs share pre-service rituals that fuel their kitchens

SCMP | FREE | 1231 words | 2026-08-27

Before the first ticket lands, every restaurant team prepares for a night of service in its own way. In kitchens where the pace can shift from quiet to chaotic in minutes, those final moments are crucial for getting mentally and physically ready.

From espresso shots and sparkling water to staff karaoke, rotating playlists and meticulous station checks, rituals – both practical and personal – help chefs set the tone for the hours ahead. Here, chefs from across Hong Kong’s dining scene share the routines, superstitions and small acts of self-care that help them find focus before their doors open.

Chef Tony Mok, Path

“We have a fairly small team of four – including [a] dishwasher! We [start the night] by greeting everyone and checking on each other. Show a bit of love to everyone.

“The playlist changes from Cantopop to more casual lofi pop songs like Keshi. Andy Hui is one of Sean’s (our Texan GM’s) favourites. Our team ritual is to sing together. It opens up our voice more before the night starts. ⁠After service begins, we are definitely more locked in to serve our guests.

“We also always stash gummies inside our kitchen to sweeten our service – a bit of a sugar rush also helps!”

Chef Sean Yuen, Jija by Vicky Lau

“Five minutes before service is the ‘calm before the storm’. Everyone is running their final checks, and the air is noticeably tense. I review tonight’s cover count, find out when the peak hour hits, and check for any VIPs or special dietary requirements. Once I have those numbers in mind, the rhythm of the night becomes completely clear.

“Downing a shot of espresso right before service not only wakes me up, but also acts as a mental trigger to switch into battle mode. [We] turn on all the heat lamps at the pass and the moment those lights go on, it means all the prep work is done.

“One hour into service, everyone is fully in the zone – hyper-focused, with the entire kitchen running at a fast yet meticulously organised pace. During peak rush hours, taking a two-second break to grab a sip of water gives me a quick moment to pause, clear my mind, and jump straight back into position.”

Chef Tara Margarita, Bourke’s

“Music is essential for us. Kitchens get hot and high-stress, so the right tunes keep the energy light during prep before the inevitable service rush. Anyone in hospitality knows the absolute pain of listening to the exact same pre-service loop for years on end. We’re lucky to have a diverse team with different cultural backgrounds, so we like to rotate the prep-DJ duties.

“If I’m on the aux, my recent go-to tracks are ‘Summer Romance’ by Incubus, ‘Given Up’ by Linkin Park and ‘Killing in the Name’ by Rage Against the Machine.

“Work is intense, but work isn’t life – it’s important not to lose sight of that. So personally, I always make time to stretch and check in with my team on a human level.

“On fully staffed days, those last five minutes are pure ‘calm before the storm’ and your final chance to double-check line set-ups or fire off last-minute backups. I change into my shift shoes and jacket and pull my hair back in a way that’s guaranteed to hold up for the next 14 hours.

“During service, front of house has made a habit of buying me Kinder Buenos – especially if I’m looking a bit stressed on the pass. I keep a dedicated 1/9 pan in my lowboy [refrigerator] stocked with bite-sized Kinder Buenos to get through the push.”

Chef Ryo Matsui, Torikaze Hong Kong

“Before anything else, I want to see, touch and taste what has arrived that day. It tells me how we need to cook.

“We make sure every station is clean, organised and complete. It may sound simple, but this is important – the state of your station reflects the state of your mind. I need to see the charcoal glowing properly and have my fan beside the grill. The fire is alive, and it changes constantly, so the fan is not only a tool to control the heat; it is how I communicate with it. When the binchotan is ready, the whole station feels ready.

“There is a quiet tension. Everyone is focused on their own station, checking small details one more time. [Then] the kitchen becomes more fluid – less thinking, more instinct. Communication is shorter, movements become more precise, and the team finds its rhythm together.

“When it becomes busy, I return to the next plate. Not the whole service, not the tickets waiting behind it, just the dish in front of me. You cannot control everything at once, but you can always control the quality of the next action.”

Chef Uwe Opocensky, Petrus

“As a junior cook, I was on a station, and being ‘ready’ meant my mise en place was perfect and I was set for whatever came at me during service. These days I run the pass, so readiness looks different. It’s less about my own station and more about checking that everyone else is ready and everything is exactly where it should be.

“The first thing I always do is greet everyone; that sets the tone for the whole day. Then I do a walk-through to see what’s going on: checking the bookings, events and anything that may have changed since the night before. After that, I sit down with my senior chefs and go through notes I’ve written the night before – ideas, things we could do better, things I think we can improve on. It’s important to me that the day starts with the team, not just with the food.

“Consistency is the ritual. I go over the booking sheet one more time. I check that everything is set up and ready – down to the tasting spoons and seat numbers. Then I get a large glass of sparkling water for myself before service starts. By the time I step through the doors of Petrus, I’m focused.

“The one constant through all of it has been a small espresso. It’s less about the caffeine and more about the signal; it’s my way of telling myself I’m ready to go.”

Chef Alex Fargas, La Paloma

“I believe every dish matters, and every person we work with and serve matters. Every day at 5pm we hold a team meeting. We dedicate the first 20 minutes to discussing the previous day’s situations and focusing on customer satisfaction. The next 20 minutes are dedicated to staff training, motivation and development. Finally, the last 10 minutes are spent discussing personal healthy habits, life goals or any important matters anyone has on their mind.

“We take that time to connect, share how we’re feeling, and discuss what we are aiming to achieve on that particular day.

“We set clear objectives for every day and week, assigning both individual and team goals. This keeps everyone connected, aligned with our targets, and fully engaged during our daily meetings.

“We take it one dish and one person at a time, ensuring we give everything the attention it needs to achieve excellence. One step at a time – that’s how we operate at La Paloma.”
