3 South Asian actors challenge Hong Kong cinema’s casting rules

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1887 words | 2026-08-07

At its best, Hong Kong cinema has always been about more than the box office. It’s about a city showing the world what it is – tough, funny, romantic, contradictory and alive.

What Hong Kong cinema is not, however, is inclusive. It never has been.

Three young men working on the margins of the industry are quietly trying to change that and, in the process, prove that the city is more colourful than its films have been letting on. They might be taking a long shot, but fortune favours the bold in Hong Kong.

“I don’t want to just be famous. I want to be able to get inside a new industry with the right heart,” says Bipin Karma. At 29 years old, the performer, born and raised in Hong Kong, knows that he is in this “for the long term”.

Karan Cholia, a 25-year-old Punjabi, nods in agreement. Hong Kong born and bred, he speaks Cantonese like a native and one day wants to play a superhero.

“In Hong Kong, they’re only making police movies and romance. I would love to play a superhero who would represent Hong Kong differently.”

The other actor at the table is Inderjeet Singh, 30, who was also born and raised in the city.

“I just want to have a say in the local industry [and prove] that the outsider can also be an actor,” he says. “You don’t have to be Chinese to give your best.”

The three men share a single ambition: to build acting careers in the Hong Kong film industry, which has thus far proven a hard nut to crack for those of South Asian heritage. Gill Mohindepaul Singh – known affectionately as Q Bobo – is the closest thing their community has had to a film role model.

The TVB actor speaks fluent Cantonese and is recognised on the street by Hongkongers who have watched him for decades in films such as Rob-B-Hood (2006), Marriage With a Liar (2010) and The White Storm 2 (2019). While all three look up to Q Bobo, they are aware that the door he stepped through may never open fully.

Ethnic minorities make up roughly 8 per cent of Hong Kong’s population, according to Race Relations Unit Demographics data. Yet, their presence on local screens remains “negligible”, as casting director Kate Sullivan puts it.

“Not enough local films are writing and producing work with diverse characters,” she says. Even when local productions do turn to Sullivan for non-Chinese talent, they ask for Western faces.

“The talent is there – ambitious, hard-working, doing crazy hours for tragic pay – and doing it for the love of it. But they need good representation. They need to know where to showcase their craft beyond the Hong Kong and mainland China market,” says Sullivan. Thus, her advice for minority actors is to lean into what makes them unique.

“Own your culture, where you are from; embrace it and find a way to showcase it and the many roles you can play,” she says. “Create and write your own work and collaborate with the right filmmakers. And don’t undersell yourself.”

Karma has done just that, having written and acted in his own projects.

“The film I got discovered from was a film I was writing,” he recalls, of the 15-minute The Bench (2018), co-written with director Mo Lai Yan-chi, which led to a pivotal role in Hand Rolled Cigarette (2020).

“The Bench was seen by a [independent film distribution and production company] Golden Scene member, who recommended me to the director of Hand Rolled Cigarette. I’m an accidental actor; I had no initial drive to be an actor or famous. I just wanted to hide behind the camera and create cool stories.”

Thanks to his parkour background, Karma had been given his first screen break at 15, in filmmaker Adam Wong Sau-ping’s 2013 breakdancing film, The Way We Dance.

His upcoming short film, Bitter Apples, sees him play a villain in English for the first time. The film sheds light on artistic freedom and power, and reminded him of the strength of Hong Kong’s English-speaking and diverse filmmaking community.

But more than a decade on from his silver-screen baptism, Karma is backing away from the social media demands and anxiety of the casting carousel. Instead, he teaches, writes, directs and acts only in projects that genuinely interest him. Now and then, Karma disconnects from the business by attending silent-meditation retreats.

The other two are constantly in hustle mode.

“In the field [of acting], you have to memorise the script,” says Cholia. “For my higher education, I deliberately chose studying history – it made my memorisation skills better.”

Cholia speaks Cantonese to friends and Punjabi to family, and consumes a mix of Bollywood and TVB shows. He landed his first significant role, the lead in the 2021 romantic comedy My Indian Boyfriend, after the director found him on Instagram.

However, the film received mixed reviews and Chinese audiences found certain cultural conventions bewildering.

“When I was going for the premiere, one audience member asked me, ‘Is it really normal for you guys to say ‘I love you’ on the first meeting?’ I couldn’t think of anything so I just said, ‘Yeah, but in India,’” recalls Cholia.

“In most [Bollywood] movies I watched, it’s like this; the hero falls in love with the leading lady in Act One, almost immediately. But in Hong Kong, you say ‘I like you’, instead of ‘I love you’. That was one of the problems in the movie.”

More recently, he has appeared in the television series Homeland Guardian, playing a police officer, a role that delighted his parents. It required him to memorise complex Cantonese dialogue, prompting him to spend every minute in the make-up chair going over the lines.

“The script was all in Chinese. I can read Chinese, but not clearly. So I found a friend who helped me with the translation, and I wrote every Pinyin on the script. When I didn’t know how to read a word, I’d ask the make-up artist and they’d practise with me,” says Cholia.

It didn’t get easier on set. “The director was not reacting, not saying ‘Good take’ or anything. You get pressured. Whatever scene you’re doing, it is the constant fear that you’re going to ruin it. But, I got the hang of it.”

The structural challenges weigh heavily on Cholia, who works as a tutor to pay the bills while searching for his big break.

“The main challenge is the face card, man. If there’s a Chinese person over a brown person, obviously they’ll prefer the Chinese actor, because the audience can relate. But you see Squid Game? That guy from Pakistan – people understood his background and where he came from,” says Cholia, referring to Player 199, portrayed by Indian actor Anupam Tripathi, in the hit Netflix series. “If they give us more roles, people can have a better understanding about us.”

Singh appears to be the most settled of the trio when it comes to his identity. “I’m more of a Hongkonger than anything else at this point, because this is what the city provides and gives you.”

His filmography is the most eclectic. He shot his first commercial at 11, after an open casting call at his school. But his career began in earnest at 18, working as an extra alongside cinema royalty Chow Yun-fat, Andy Lau Tak-wah and Jacky Cheung Hok-yau in From Vegas to Macau III (2016).

Since then, Singh has appeared in Hollywood miniseries The Expats, a local film and, most significantly, this year’s Let’s Have a Cup of Doodh Patti Chai, a feature about ethnic minorities in Hong Kong that was among the officially selected titles at the Guwahati Asian Film Festival, in India. But his favourite on-set experience was working with Anthony Wong Chau-sang on The Sunny Side of the Street (2022).

“Observing how [Wong] was delivering the lines, the way he was guiding the whole set, making sure that everyone around him was on the same page – that was really fun,” recalls Singh. “Then, going to the Golden Horse Awards in Taiwan and seeing him win. And seeing my co-actor [Sahal Zaman] – who played my son – win [a nomination for] the newcomer award. Even though that’s my dream, seeing that happen, it was …,” he says, his voice trailing off.

“I’m aware of my limited opportunities,” says Singh, who runs an electronics trading business to make ends meet. “The main viewership [in Hong Kong] is Chinese, so they will only cast Chinese leads. But it can happen. It will happen. Don’t expect a lot, but don’t give up.”

Historically, minority actors in Hong Kong cinema have been relegated to either the villain or the comical sidekick roles. Then, there are the more problematic stereotypes, akin to racial caricatures.

“If you watch old Chinese movies, there was always one Indian guy, with his voice dubbed into some typical racist accent. The movies promoted that. That’s why they continue it,” says Cholia, who declines projects that disrespect his community. He says he once turned down a script that opened with his character addressing himself by a racist slur, with no narrative justification offered.

Similarly, Karma says he would turn down a role if it depicted Nepali people in a gratuitously negative light.

Singh’s worst casting experience was not race-related but involved an advertisement for which he was not forewarned that he would be expected to play a patient near death. “I walked off,” he says. “And then I had to [suggest] replacement actors on the spot from my own friends.”

Why do all three would-be film stars persevere?

“For me, I enjoy the process,” says Cholia. “When there’s work, you want it to finish fast, but when there’s no work, you want to do it again. I want more chances to get dream roles.”

For Singh, it’s “the magic of making movies” that keeps him going. “It’s fun, it’s challenging,” he says. “Working with different people, you get to learn so many things. Someone, somewhere, has seen you. One fine day in the future, my [as yet unconceived] kids can see it. I want to leave a mark.”

Karma is embracing the journey, rather than the destination: “Nothing is permanent. Instead of always trying to land a job, I want to find love in the process of it.”

The challenges persist, however, and are likely to increase as Hong Kong’s film output dwindles. Peak production was in 1993, with the release of 234 feature films, according to the Hong Kong Film Development Council. Three decades later, that number had plummeted to just 43 films released in 2023.

“Hong Kong wants a more vibrant cinema scene,” insists Karma. “It wants to regain its name for being a place that produces great cinema, like back in the day with Stanley Kwan, Wong Kar-wai, all of them.

“The new wave of filmmakers should look at their own people who are of different colours, so you can make your film as Hong Kong is, instead of what it pretends to be. It is multicultural; it is not one note.”
