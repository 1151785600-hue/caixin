‘An intrusion’: data centre fights test Southeast Asia’s AI ambitions

SCMP | FREE | 1167 words | 2026-08-25

Residents of Section 9 in Kota Damansara thought they knew what was coming to the vacant land opposite their homes. The site, beside a community forest in the Malaysian township, was expected to become a high street retail development.

Instead, they learned that Petaling Jaya City Council (MBPJ) had received an application for two three-storey data centre blocks – part of the vast physical infrastructure now being built across Southeast Asia to support cloud computing and artificial intelligence.

To governments, data centres promise foreign investment, digital jobs and a place in the fast-growing AI economy. To nearby residents, however, they can look very different: utility-hungry buildings raising questions over noise, water use, traffic and land suitability.

By July 21, the council had received about 290 objections, and nine days later it announced that the developer’s consultant had withdrawn the application to reassess the site’s feasibility and development components.

A similar scene played out this month in Oton, a municipality in the central Philippine province of Iloilo, where residents and farmers questioned a proposed 50-megawatt AI data centre during a public hearing.

The proponent walked out, and local media reported two days later that the proposal had been withdrawn.

Both disputes point to an emerging fault line in Southeast Asia’s race to build the infrastructure of the AI era: national governments want the investment but municipal authorities and residents are often left to decide where its power-hungry, water-dependent footprint should land.

Wrong place, not wrong project

In Kota Damansara, the 1.1-hectare (2.7-acre) site was zoned for “business and services”, while part of it was marked for transport as a road reserve under MBPJ’s plan.

The council said the first notice did not follow its prescribed format and ordered corrections. A fresh objection period was later opened from July 21-27 for homeowners within 20 metres (65 feet).

The file on the proposed data centre in Kota Damansara was now fully closed, the council said, although the landowner could still submit a new application for the same site or nearby land, subject to its proposed use, permitted zoning, impact studies and other planning factors.

Aiman Zahry, a project director with Kuala Lumpur-based NRY Project Management – a project management consultancy and affiliate of NRY Architects – said the dispute arose less from the building itself than from the absence of an agreed strategy for locating such infrastructure.

“Framing it as ‘residents vs data centres’ describes the symptom, not the problem,” Aiman said.

The same facility beside an office tower would be infrastructure, he said: “Beside a landed house backing onto a community forest, it is an intrusion. Nothing about the building changes … Only the adjacency,.”

Malaysia’s national planning guideline on data centres, approved and published in October 2024, allows data centres larger than one megavolt-ampere, or roughly equal to 1MW, in commercial and industrial zones.

For new developments, the guideline calls for 50 metres between a data centre building and a residential lot boundary, with the buffer potentially comprising roads, landscaping or parking.

The buffer can be waived if noise is no more than 5 decibels above the original level, but Aiman said the guideline did not specify who should measure it, where or over what period, or whether it must be checked again after operations began.

He said the federal guideline contained “no schedule of technical submissions” requiring details such as connected load, water demand, acoustic assessment or cooling design.

MBPJ told This Week in Asia the federal guideline was a key reference when assessing the proposal, alongside the city’s own local plan and inputs such as impact reports, comments from technical departments and other relevant agencies, and residents’ feedback.

The council said data centre applications should first obtain approval from the Data Centre Task Force, a special national-level committee, before seeking further approval from local authorities.

Kota Damansara resident Narindera Pall Singh said he and his fellow residents were not against development but “we are against inappropriate development in the wrong location”. The neighbourhood already had industrial zones, he said, and shop lots would have better served the community.

Questions before answers

In Oton, residents were brought into the process, but details remained unresolved.

Local media reported that Daniel Stefan Robertsen, who presented himself as managing director of Norway Green Energy, proposed a 10-hectare, 50MW data centre in the municipality.

A consultation hearing, held on August 14 and broadcast on the Oton council board’s Facebook page, broke down after a local resident questioned the project’s water use and treatment, with Robertsen walking out.

That same day, Oton Vice-Mayor Jose Neil P. Olivares refuted claims the council board had endorsed the proposal, saying on social media the project remained at the consultation stage.

Local media later reported that Robertsen had emailed the council to say the project would not proceed further in Oton.

Metro Pacific Iloilo Water later clarified on social media that it had never entered into any agreement or discussion with Norway Green Energy regarding the proposed Oton data centre, and that the only letter of intent it had received was for a separate, preliminary facility proposed in Pavia, not Oton.

Pavia Vice-Mayor Edsel Gerochi was reported by local outlet Panay News as saying the municipal council had received no proposal or communication from the company.

The regional test

On August 5, Thailand’s cabinet approved a draft regulation establishing a national data centre policy committee to coordinate policies and standards covering energy, water, land use, public safety and community impacts.

Thailand’s Board of Investment subsequently said it would tighten its project-screening criteria to align with the committee’s direction, while authorities are also preparing national power and water maps to guide zoning and site selection.

Aiman said none of the three countries had developed a system capable of preventing conflict before it emerged; instead, they intervened at different points of the planning and siting process.

“Malaysia has the most substantive technical standards, including an alternative to prescribed buffer distances, but lacks mechanisms to verify compliance with their protective provisions,” Aiman said.

By contrast, he said, the Philippines provided early consultation at the local level but imposed no equivalent technical baseline.

Thailand, meanwhile, was beginning to address power, water, siting and public safety concerns through its new national policy committee, although this remained at the policy stage without an accompanying licensing regime.

However, power was the main limiting factor for all three countries.

“All three sit in developing economies where grid headroom is finite and the direction of demand is pushing costs up,” Aiman said.

Governments needed to develop power generation, grid capacity and data centre policy as a single sequence before demand overwhelmed available infrastructure, he said.

Narindera welcomed the data centre’s withdrawal from Kota Damansara but said this was not the end and residents would continue monitoring any new application.

“As residents, we simply want development to be carried out in a proper and responsible manner, taking into consideration the surrounding community and existing approved planning,” Narindera said.
