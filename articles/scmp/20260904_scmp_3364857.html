Malaysia’s Anwar traded reform for power. Now Umno wants it all

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1964 words | 2026-09-04

The roar from thousands of People’s Justice Party (PKR) delegates gathered at a grand convention centre in Melaka last weekend exuded all the confidence expected of a party leading Malaysia’s government.

The past three years have been the culmination of more than two decades of struggle for PKR, with it finally achieving its long-held goal of installing leader Anwar Ibrahim as prime minister and wielding the power to push through reforms.

Yet beneath the confident facade lurks a ruling party now scrambling to find its way.

PKR’s victory in the 2022 general election came at the cost of the very reforms it had promised to deliver, as Anwar had little choice but to negotiate compromises with erstwhile rivals, including former ruling party Umno, to steady his administration. Those trade-offs did not go unnoticed.

Anwar’s ruling Pakatan Harapan (PH) coalition, of which PKR is a founding and principal member, has suffered a series of bruising defeats across three state elections since November – and whatever platform the prime minister gave a once-deflated Umno has turned the party into the greatest threat to his rule.

Staring down the possibility of being turfed out of Putrajaya after just one term, PKR members are left wondering how to reverse the trend, especially as surging Malay nationalist sentiment carries a resurgent Umno forward.

“The delegates are all confused. They need clear direction,” a senior party leader told This Week in Asia, speaking anonymously due to the sensitivity of the matter.

“Before, it was about coming to power and raising Anwar as prime minister. Now what?”

Senior members who attended last weekend’s congress pointed to a disconnect between Anwar, his party’s top leadership and the rank and file, who have been left without a common purpose to rally around.

That gulf became apparent during Anwar’s opening speech at the congress last Saturday.

Delegates gave only a lukewarm response as their party president and prime minister listed out, at length, his administration’s successes in bolstering growth and investment numbers.

It was only when Anwar touched on the multibillion-ringgit scandal at the Tabung Haji pilgrim fund – made public last month – that the crowd roared to life, with some delegates passionately demanding retribution against Umno, the party in power when the alleged offences occurred.

That reaction offered the clearest indication yet that the prime minister and PH need to coalesce around a clear message if they hope to slow Umno’s momentum, and that of its nascent partnership with the opposition Pan-Malaysian Islamic Party (PAS).

“You see Umno and PAS talking about uniting the ummah [Muslim faithful], and claims that [PKR coalition partner] DAP is out to wrest power from the Malays. It’s simple messaging that people can easily understand,” the senior PKR leader said.

When comparing investments and other economic indicators, Anwar scored higher than past leaders, he said. “But the people here don’t understand all that. You need to present things using simple language.”

House of cards

The problems trace back to November 2022, when Anwar was named prime minister by former king Sultan Abdullah Ahmad Shah on condition that he form a unity government that included allies and rivals alike, to avoid a hung parliament.

The arrangement threw a lifeline to Umno, which had just suffered its worst electoral outing, winning fewer than 30 of parliament’s 222 seats.

From there, Anwar has been accused of setting aside his reform agenda and steering his government rightward, mirroring the previous Umno administration’s penchant for cracking down on sexual minorities and free speech in a bid to win over a Malay-Muslim majority suspicious of his motives.

Despite growing outrage from long-time supporters, the prime minister enjoyed a period of relative political stability over the first half of his five-year term, as economic growth and foreign investment hit record highs.

But that proved to be merely the calm before the storm.

The first salvo was fired in the Sabah state election last November, when Anwar’s PH coalition was all but wiped out. Hardest hit was the Democratic Action Party (DAP), the largest party in Anwar’s federal coalition, which lost every seat it contested.

Over the past two months, PH’s tenuous grip on power has truly sunk in, as it suffered back-to-back defeats at the hand of fellow federal partner, Umno.

Umno cruised to an extended supermajority in the southern state of Johor in July, then struck an informal alliance with Islamist rivals PAS to wrest control of Negeri Sembilan state from PH earlier this month.

The losses in the three state elections were the first tangible signs that Anwar and PH were in real trouble, after years of complaints from civil society groups and PH’s own support base about the lack of reforms following decades of promises.

Wong Chen, a three-term MP who quit PKR this month after being unceremoniously removed from a government post and cut off from his constituency fund, said the current trend pointed to PH losing the next national poll, which must be held by February 2028 at the latest.

If they don’t quickly reform in the last few months that they have, then they won’t even be able to win enough support

PKR defector Wong Chen on his former party

Wong, who later joined fellow former PKR leader Rafizi Ramli’s Bersama party, said the recent string of electoral losses proved that Anwar’s personal popularity was not enough to guarantee PH’s survival against Umno and PAS.

“They have to start accepting the fact that if they don’t quickly reform in the last few months that they have, then they won’t even be able to win enough support to be a strong opposition,” Wong said.

The losses did trigger a response from Anwar. Last weekend, he pledged that the government would proceed to repeal the controversial University and University Colleges Act 1971 – which student activists have long said stifles freedom of speech and expression – and fulfil promises to impose a two-term limit on the prime minister and separate the powers of the attorney general and public prosecutor.

But those pledges may amount to little if they only translate into legislative change, according to Timothy Wong, a senior analyst with government affairs consultancy BowerGroupAsia.

“There needs to be real action behind institutional reforms rather than relying solely on legislation to convince the majority,” he said.

“They will need to do more to convince a politically fatigued electorate, especially urban and middle-class voters who are feeling increasingly disenfranchised by the country’s politics.”

Complicating matters further is Anwar’s continued reliance on Umno, which long-time Malaysia watcher James Chin says has every reason to sabotage the prime minister’s reform push, leveraging its cabinet positions – and the budgets that come with them – to bolster its own electoral chances.

“If you want to do real deep reforms, you have to inform the cabinet. And of course Umno, which has a lot of ministers in cabinet, will try to sabotage it or not agree to it, so you’re stuck,” said Chin, a professor of Asian studies at the University of Tasmania.

“He [Anwar] knows deep down inside that Umno is plotting to win the next election and replace him as prime minister.”

Cracks in PH

Anwar caught a break, of sorts, last weekend, when delegates at DAP’s own convention voted decisively to remain within the governing coalition, after months of speculation the party might walk away.

But that lopsided vote belied the seething dissent within DAP’s ranks over how Anwar has managed the ruling coalition – especially his perceived favouring of Umno.

“Instead of bringing the coalition partners together, what has he done? He has divided us, pitted one against the other and empowered Umno. We cannot afford to ignore that,” DAP leader Sangeet Kaur Deo told her party’s convention.

“We cannot run away from the fact that today, under our government, Malaysia is more divided on racial lines than ever before. We cannot deny that there are scandals relating to corruption that have escalated during the time that we have been in power.”

Within PKR itself, the complaints are just as pointed. Some argue Anwar’s political instincts have curdled into self preservation.

His decision, in closing the party congress last Sunday, to announce an extra 222 million ringgit (US$54.6 million) in annual constituency allocations for MPs – money used for everything from pothole repairs to cash handouts for the needy – struck many grass-roots members as besides the point.

It did nothing to address the growing anger over stalled reform, critics say – with no explanation offered either for why three PKR MPs seen as loyal to Rafizi had their own allocations slashed.

Rafizi quit PKR after losing the deputy presidency to Anwar’s daughter, Nurul Izzah, in a contest he claimed was rife with nepotism.

Nurul Izzah has since stepped back too, beginning what the party has called a “leave of absence” to pursue her studies after serving her final days as her father’s deputy at the congress.

The extra funding fits a pattern: over the past two years, Anwar has pushed to increase cash handouts and maintain costly fuel subsidies despite the strain on public coffers, in a bid to win broader support.

One grass-roots leader offered a warning from history. “You can’t keep giving people freebies and expect them to love you. Look at what happened during BN’s time,” he said, invoking the Umno-led Barisan Nasional coalition that lost power in 2018 at the height of the 1MDB corruption scandal despite its own cash-handout scheme. “They still lost. We are doing exactly the same thing now.”

These internal rifts may not trigger an outright collapse of PH, but analysts warn that pressure from DAP and PKR supporters will keep mounting as long as Anwar prioritises political stability and his relationship with Umno over the reform agenda.

“The bigger danger is not PH formally breaking apart, but becoming increasingly hollowed out as an electoral alliance,” said public policy adviser Kamles Kumar, associate director for Malaysia at strategic advisory group Asia Group Advisors.

“If electoral losses continue, the question will increasingly shift from whether PH can stay together to whether staying together still benefits each component party.”

And the next test for PH is not far off. The Melaka state election, which must be called by February next year, is already looming and the Umno-led BN – which holds 21 of the state assembly’s 28 seats – has signalled its terms for any future deal: PH can have a shot at the seven seats BN does not currently control, but nothing more.

The bigger danger is not PH formally breaking apart, but becoming increasingly hollowed out

Kamles Kumar, public policy adviser

According to one Melaka-based PKR grass-roots leader, the party leadership is seriously considering the offer, even if it means accepting a subordinate role to Umno.

Yet this appears badly out of step with sentiments on the ground. “The top are talking about negotiating with BN, but we’ve already seen what happened in the two past state elections. On the ground, people are already sharpening their knives,” the insider said.

“As far as the grass roots are concerned, we have two choices. Fight and lose, or not do anything and die [politically].”

Anwar has promised party delegates he will listen: to their opinions, their suggestions and even their criticism.

For a party that spent 20 years fighting to put him in power – and is now watching that power slip through its fingers – the question is no longer whether he hears them.

It is whether, after everything, just listening will be enough.
