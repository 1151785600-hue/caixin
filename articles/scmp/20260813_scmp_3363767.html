Singapore and South Korea eye science opportunities as last main US-China link shatters

SCMP | FREE | 1404 words | 2026-08-13

As President Xi Jinping prepares for summit talks with Donald Trump in Washington next month, the United States is closing one of the few remaining openings between the two countries by targeting scientific research.

This field has been one of the most durable links in the US-China relationship, but a series of new security-related restrictions on federally funded collaborations with Chinese institutions mark the latest, and arguably most consequential, step in a years-long shift away from cooperation.

Experts say the measures are unlikely to halt Chinese scientific progress. Instead, they are reshaping where international collaboration takes place – creating opportunities for intermediary research hubs while narrowing one of the most enduring channels of bilateral engagement.

The timing is striking. The latest restrictions arrive as Washington and Beijing prepare for a summit intended to manage an increasingly adversarial relationship.

But they also expose a growing contradiction. On paper, the diplomatic architecture of scientific cooperation remains intact. The US-China Science and Technology Agreement, first signed in 1979, was quietly renewed in late 2024.

In practice, however, the space for collaboration is shrinking under an expanding web of funding restrictions, blacklists and security rules.

For diplomats who spent decades managing US-China ties, the shift goes beyond science policy. It marks the erosion of one of the few long-standing areas of common interest.

“When I served in the Foreign Service, we always described our environment, health and science cooperation as the ballast of the relationship,” said Deborah Seligsohn, a former US diplomat who worked extensively on environmental and scientific cooperation with China.

Now an assistant professor at Pennsylvania’s Villanova University, Seligsohn said the field embodied the principle of “mutual benefit”, one of the founding principles of the two countries’ formal diplomatic relationship.

“We all benefited from advancing science and improving areas like public health, medical treatment and environmental protection,” she added.

This had provided a form of ballast in the relationship, but Seligsohn warned this was steadily being lost.

Scientific collaboration between the world’s two largest research powers was already slowing before Trump’s return to the White House last year.

Scientific papers with American and Chinese co-authors peaked between 2017 and 2019 before falling by roughly 20 per cent, according to analyses of National Science Foundation and Clarivate data.

The decline has been particularly steep in strategically sensitive fields such as artificial intelligence (AI), advanced engineering, telecommunications and quantum technologies.

The Australian Strategic Policy Institute estimates that the share of China’s international collaborations involving US researchers has fallen from nearly half in 2016 to just over a quarter in recent years.

This trajectory began under the first Trump administration’s China Initiative, which targeted alleged industrial spying and the theft of intellectual property. While the programme ended, the broader shift away from scientific cooperation continued throughout Joe Biden’s presidency.

Recent measures have accelerated this trend. Private sector research, non-federally funded academic work and collaborations in clearly non-sensitive basic science still occur, but even these have to overcome barriers such as rising compliance burdens, risk-averse universities and reputational concerns.

Visa scrutiny of Chinese students and researchers in sensitive fields, reciprocal access difficulties and intensifying global competition for scientific talent have further amplified this practical decoupling.

In July, the US defence department expanded its Section 1286 list of foreign institutions that engage in “problematic activities” to include leading civilian universities such as Fudan and Shanghai Jiao Tong. Previously it had mainly focused on universities seen as linked to the Chinese military.

The Pentagon has banned any funding that supports fundamental research involving collaboration with any institution on the list, while researchers receiving grants from the National Science Foundation are banned from collaborating with institutions appearing on US restricted-party lists or employing researchers affiliated with them.

The White House Office of Management and Budget has also proposed a government-wide ban on using federal grants to support collaborations involving “countries of concern”, including China.

Taken together, these measures mark a significant departure from previous efforts to balance scientific openness with targeted security safeguards.

“The risk-benefit equation has shifted fundamentally,” said Denis Simon, senior research fellow at the Quincy Institute and former executive vice-chancellor of Duke Kunshan University.

In the pre-Trump era, he said, researchers generally evaluated collaborations based on scientific merit and complementary expertise while federal agencies relied on disclosure requirements and oversight to manage risks.

“Today researchers must weigh export controls, sanctions lists, funding restrictions, compliance risks and potential reputational costs.”

The consequences extend beyond collaborations explicitly prohibited by law.

“The transaction costs of international collaboration have increased considerably,” Simon said. “Many principal investigators now avoid otherwise permissible collaborations because university compliance offices recommend caution, funding uncertainty threatens future grants and administrative burdens have increased substantially.”

As direct collaboration narrows, other countries are positioning themselves to capture some of the movement of talent, capital and international partnerships.

Singapore has emerged as perhaps the clearest beneficiary. Its combination of political neutrality, English-language universities, strong intellectual property protections, relatively predictable regulation and ability to attract international talent has drawn multinational research operations from both American and Chinese companies.

“Singapore has deliberately cultivated an ecosystem that remains scientifically open to both countries while offering world-class universities, biomedical research and multinational corporate R&D,” Simon said.

The Gulf states are pursuing a similar strategy. The United Arab Emirates has invested heavily in AI, advanced computing and international talent recruitment, while Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 programme has channelled its sovereign wealth into AI, biotechnology, clean energy and advanced manufacturing.

“Rather than replacing US-China scientific cooperation, these countries increasingly function as multinational hubs connecting researchers who might otherwise have collaborated directly,” Simon said.

He also said Australia, Canada, India, South Korea, Switzerland and parts of the European Union were emerging as new research hubs in an increasingly multipolar system.

Yet their emergence does not mean the global scientific system is simply relocating.

Seligsohn argued that while countries such as Singapore, the UAE and Switzerland could host international meetings and enable collaborations, they could not replicate the scale of the world’s two largest scientific powers.

She said their spending on research was not “anywhere near as great as the US and China”, adding: “The issue with third countries becoming research hubs is that they don’t solve the problems US researchers have with export controls and funding restrictions.”

“The advantage the US had was that it has been one of the two major funders – traditionally the largest – and it has been the most open: the place people from other countries could come to and traditionally wanted to come to.

“Now our funding is decreasing, but other than China there really aren’t any countries increasing spending on the same scale. And with all our visa restrictions, we simply aren’t as open as we were.”

Rather than shifting from one centre to another, she predicted global science would become increasingly fragmented.

“We will see other places become venues for meetings and the like,” she said. “But the reality is that overall science is going to become more fractured – with some in the US, some in China, some in Europe or Canada or Australia or other countries. The centrality that came from having one interconnected system that promoted rapid movement in global science is simply being lost.”

Sourabh Gupta, from the Washington-based Institute for China-American Studies, argued that the restrictions were unlikely to derail China’s scientific rise. China was already a near peer in high-quality, highly cited research, he said, and Chinese and American academics remained each other’s largest international collaborators.

“The US is welcome to drop out of the race of its own volition and cede its leadership spot,” Gupta said. “But that will not stop the Chinese side from powering ahead – by itself, and in collaboration with others.”

He expected tighter visa restrictions, weaker university partnerships and greater controls on research collaboration would be introduced in future, leaving both sides “poorer off, in relative terms”.

For Seligsohn, the biggest loss is not simply fewer joint papers or shared discoveries but the erosion of one of the last channels through which the world’s two most powerful countries could understand one another.

That carries both geopolitical and practical risks, she added, pointing to the mistrust that emerged during the Covid-19 pandemic. “Understanding each other is important for maintaining peace between the world’s two most powerful countries.”
