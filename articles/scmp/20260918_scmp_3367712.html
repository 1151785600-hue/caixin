Is Japan’s Okinawa finally moving past anti-base politics?

SCMP | FREE | 1183 words | 2026-09-18

Okinawa has handed a landslide victory to a conservative who accepts Tokyo’s plans for relocating a controversial US airbase – delivering a blow to 12 years of anti-base politics in Japan’s southernmost prefecture.

Genta Koja’s victory on Sunday over two-term governor Denny Tamaki raised a question cutting to the heart of Okinawa’s post-war politics: is it finally starting to move on from the US military base issue?

Koja, a 42-year-old conservative backed by Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi’s Liberal Democratic Party, campaigned on economic revival and accepting Tokyo’s plan to relocate the US Marine Corps’ Futenma air station from densely populated Ginowan to the coastal Henoko district in Nago, both on Okinawa Island.

Tamaki, by contrast, had opposed the base project for his entire tenure as governor, a stance he inherited from his late predecessor after winning 2018’s election on an anti-base platform.

Exit polls conducted by Kyodo News on Sunday found that nearly half of voters ranked economic revitalisation as their top concern, while barely one-fifth of respondents cited issues related to US bases.

That was reflected in the election results. Koja, a former deputy mayor of Okinawa’s capital Naha, defeated Tamaki with 59 per cent of the vote, officials said on Monday.

He will become the prefecture’s first governor born after its return to Japanese sovereignty by the United States in 1972 and the first in over a decade not to have built his career on opposition to US military bases.

Generational rift

Okinawa has been seen as a bastion of pacifist, left-leaning opposition forces for years.

Despite accounting for less than 1 per cent of Japan’s total land area, it hosts roughly 70 per cent of the land dedicated exclusively to US military facilities in the country – a burden that has made it central to arguments over the US-Japan alliance.

Tamaki and his All Okinawa movement, a broad political and civic alliance that opposes the relocation and construction of US military bases on Okinawa Island, had long framed the dispute as a test of local autonomy against central authority.

Sunday’s result illustrated a generational divide, according to Sebastian Maslow, an associate professor at the University of Tokyo’s Institute of Social Science.

While voters over 60 largely backed Tamaki and All Okinawa, “younger voters were more concerned about the economy”, he said.

The outcome reflected “pragmatism as well as frustration” with Tamaki for failing to stop new base construction, Maslow said – though that did not mean voters were unconcerned about national security implications.

Japan’s “Nansei shift” – the build-up of defence capabilities across its southern islands – was expected to continue under the new governor and “this will place an even higher burden on Okinawa”, he said.

Masato Kamikubo, a political scientist and professor at Ritsumeikan University, said successive LDP governments dating back to Shinzo Abe’s administration had cut subsidies to Okinawa in an attempt to “bully” the prefecture for its confrontational approach to central authority.

A younger generation is now beginning to create a new form of politics

Masato Kamikubo, political scientist

“Faced with rising prices and other economic difficulties, Okinawan voters appear to have wanted a way out of the present situation and therefore prioritised the economy over the military base issue,” he said.

The election result reflected a move away from the decades-old contest between conservative and liberal blocs, Kamikubo said, arguing that All Okinawa’s “crushing defeat” revealed a movement built on ideological resistance struggling to hold a new generation’s attention.

“A younger generation is now beginning to create a new form of politics from the grass roots, moving beyond the traditional divide between left and right,” he said. “This election may have symbolised that emerging transformation.”

Meanwhile, Takaichi’s firm stance on national security, particularly the tough line she had taken on China, also resonated with Okinawan voters, Kamikubo said.

“Okinawa is on the front line in confronting the perceived threat from China,” he said, noting that liberal politicians such as Tamaki and the All Okinawa movement had done little more than appealed for peace.

“Some voters are anxious about whether these groups would actually be able to protect them in the event of a crisis.”

Tokyo’s cash carrot

On Monday, following Koja’s victory, Takaichi pledged to raise Okinawa’s budget to more than 300 billion yen (US$1.94 billion) from around 260 billion yen in recent years.

Kamikubo said he expected her “to present Okinawa as a model demonstrating that economic growth can be achieved and rising living costs overcome when the central and prefectural governments work together”.

“For a prime minister struggling with declining approval ratings, the victory in Okinawa will be seen as a significant glimmer of hope,” he added.

A recent poll by national broadcaster NHK found public approval of Takaichi’s cabinet at 53 per cent, down from 66 per cent in November last year, amid concerns about inflation and the rising cost of living.

Koja’s economic focus and his image as a fresh face – in contrast to Tamaki, who staked his political legacy on opposing the US military presence – had all helped at the polls, said Rintaro Nishimura, a Tokyo-based senior associate at strategic advisory firm The Asia Group.

But Sunday’s result did not mean that Okinawans no longer cared about the base issue, he said, nor that they would accept every central government policy.

“He [Koja] has been clear in how he views his role: a coordinator who will seek out the most practical and best option for the people of Okinawa,” Nishimura said.

Still, the LDP backing a winning candidate for governor in a historically anti-LDP prefecture was “a good narrative for Takaichi to build on”, he said – particularly heading into next year’s local elections, as it helped counter concerns that the party was unpopular at the local level.

Masaaki Gabe, a professor emeritus at the University of the Ryukyus in Okinawa, said many Okinawans had voted out of dissatisfaction with rising prices and economic hardship rather than security concerns.

“The sense of immediacy regarding a potential conflict stemming from the Taiwan Strait crisis remains relatively limited,” he said, adding that further militarisation of Okinawa was likely to be “tolerated” by a significant segment of voters.

Beijing sees Taiwan as part of China to be reunited by force if necessary. Most countries, including the US and Japan, do not recognise Taiwan as an independent state, but are opposed to any attempt to take the self-governed island by force.

The two central challenges facing Okinawa today were poverty and the burden of hosting US bases, Gabe said.

“Regardless of who serves as governor, these two challenges continue to exist with unwavering force and successive prefectural administrations have been compelled to confront them,” he said, adding that “the central government holds the upper hand on the base issue and its control over policy is unlikely to be shaken”.

Okinawa consistently ranks as the least economically developed of Japan’s prefectures, with average incomes and employment roughly 20 to 30 per cent behind mainland standards.
