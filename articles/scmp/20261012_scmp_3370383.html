As AI and the Iran war loom, China’s energy technocrats are on the fast track

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1676 words | 2026-10-12

This is a special contribution from the Centre on Contemporary China and the World at the University of Hong Kong. Read the full report at the end of the article. It will be available on the institute website cccw.hku.hk from October 13.

With the rising strategic significance of energy, especially due to the need to support China’s artificial intelligence (AI) development, members of the Communist Party’s 20th Central Committee with energy backgrounds – the country’s “movers and shakers” in this strategic sector – merit closer attention.

Among them are former or current power-company executives, petroleum managers, nuclear-safety officials and specialists in water administration. Many built their expertise over decades, establishing themselves as energy technocrats before taking on responsibilities beyond their original industries. Their biographies reveal a recurring route into political leadership – deep specialisation first and diversification of political experience later, highlighting the growing presence and power of this leadership cohort.

More experience, greater representation

Our data set identifies 34 energy sector members, including 21 full members and 13 alternates. They account for 10.2 per cent of the 205 full members, 7.6 per cent of the 171 alternates and 9 per cent of the combined membership. Three of them were elected to the Politburo in October 2022, representing 12.5 per cent of its 24 members. As of this year, their median ages are 64 among full members and 60 among alternates.

By October 2022, 29 of the 34 had at least 10 years of energy experience. This included 16 of the 21 full members. Nineteen had careers spanning 20 years or more.

The comparison with earlier committees further illustrates the long tenure and solid leadership experience of these energy technocrats, especially among the full members. In the energy cohort, the share with at least 10 years of energy experience was 75 per cent in the 18th committee, falling to 67.7 per cent in the 19th before reaching 85.3 per cent in the 20th. Among full members, the number with energy backgrounds increased from 15 in the 19th committee to 21 in the 20th committee; within that group, the number with at least 10 years of experience increased from nine to 16.

A long apprenticeship

The career of Zhang Yuzhuo, president and Communist Party secretary of the Chinese Academy of Engineering, is illustrative. He began work at a coal research institute in 1985, combining research with progressively greater institutional responsibilities. In 2002, he moved to Shenhua Group, where he held senior management positions before becoming chairman in 2014. When he joined Tianjin’s municipal party leadership and became party secretary of the Binhai New Area in March 2017, he brought more than three decades of coal-related research and enterprise experience into local government.

That was a specialised career, but not a narrow one. Zhang’s progression from research to institute leadership and then corporate management placed him at different points in the relationship between technology and industrial production. The professional foundation remained rooted in the energy industry as the scale of his responsibilities expanded.

This is an important feature of deep specialisation. Remaining within an industry can involve considerable movement across functions – from solving technical problems to managing people, allocating capital and taking responsibility for operations. Each role exposes a different part of the same system and offers opportunities to understand how an engineering choice becomes a financial commitment, an organisational challenge and, eventually, an operating result.

The resulting knowledge is both technical and managerial. A manager who understands why a grid connection is delayed – whether due to network capacity, equipment procurement or the timing of another investment – can ask better questions of the organisations involved.

Read in this light, the long careers represent an extended apprenticeship in complex systems. Their significance lies in sustained exposure to implementation – what it takes to turn a plan into a functioning operation. Repeated responsibility for delivery can sharpen judgment about whether a timetable is credible and whether the organisations involved can meet their commitments. For those who later enter government, this experience provides a practical and solid foundation for much broader and more strategic decisions.

From an industry to governance

The next stage changes the scope of responsibility. Among the energy cohort in the 20th Central Committee, 22 had acquired party-government experience by the 20th party congress in 2022, in local or central posts. Their careers connect prolonged work within an industry with appointments spanning a broader range of public responsibilities.

Career diversification, in this context, means an expansion of governing responsibilities. The transition from responsibility for a particular enterprise or administrative field to responsibility for a wider set of institutions, interests and public priorities is a demanding task. China’s personnel framework provides institutional channels for such movement. The Communist Party has an established tradition of recruiting leaders from enterprises, universities and research institutions and allows exchanges across organisational boundaries. Technical experience can enter leadership selection through a system of party appointment and evaluation.

Liu Ning, who until recently served as Henan’s provincial party secretary, illustrates the scale of that transition. He began work in the Yangtze water-planning and design system in 1983 and later became chief engineer and vice-minister at the Ministry of Water Resources. After roughly 34 years in water-related work, he moved to Qinghai province in 2017. He subsequently served as governor of Qinghai and then Liaoning before becoming Guangxi’s party secretary in 2021. By the 2022 party congress, a long specialist career had been followed by senior responsibilities in three different provincial settings.

His trajectory illustrates two stages of increasing complexity. Water administration already requires decisions across connected territories and competing uses, whereas provincial leadership extends that problem across the whole economy. A water official may approach a proposed project through its implications for supply, flood protection or infrastructure. A provincial leader must place those considerations alongside employment, public finances, industrial development and social services. Earlier expertise remains useful, but its holder must now decide how much weight it deserves among competing claims.

Yang Jinbai, deputy secretary of Hainan’s provincial party committee and concurrently secretary of its Political and Legal Affairs Commission, followed a parallel route through electricity. He began work in power dispatch in 1994, progressing through technical and operational positions before taking senior roles at Guizhou Power Grid, China Southern Power Grid and State Grid. In 2018, after 24 years in the power sector, he became a vice-chairman of the Guangxi regional government. He became a vice mayor of Beijing in 2020 and was elected an alternate member of the 20th Central Committee.

His industrial career had already widened from dispatch and engineering to strategic planning and corporate management. The move into government expanded its scope again. In Guangxi, he concurrently headed the office responsible for planning and construction in the Beibu Gulf Economic Zone. Now his responsibilities on the Hainan party committee encompass public security, social stability and coordination across law-enforcement and judicial institutions.

Yang’s trajectory makes the distinction between the two stages particularly clear. Within the power sector, his responsibilities grew around a connected set of technical and commercial problems. His move into government transferred that experience to the regional economic agenda. His subsequent appointment in Hainan shows that diversification can extend across distinct functions of party-state governance.

A city cannot be governed like an enlarged enterprise. Its leadership must consider firms with conflicting interests, residents affected by development and public bodies pursuing different objectives. An investment that makes commercial sense for one company may impose costs elsewhere. A project that promises industrial growth may compete with housing, transport or public services for land and funding.

For an energy veteran, this changes the central question. Experience in industry helps explain how a project can be delivered. Governing requires deciding whether it deserves to be a priority, how its costs should be distributed and what other commitments must accompany it. This is precisely the substance of the second apprenticeship of governing. Higher office calls for negotiation across departments, choices under budget constraints and engagement with interests outside the original professional community.

The two stages reinforce each other. Professional depth provides a basis for scrutinising technical advice. Wider governing experience places that advice within a larger set of economic and social choices. The sequence is essential because depth and breadth address different weaknesses. Rapid movement across unrelated posts can produce extensive exposure without sustained understanding.

Energy technocrats, China’s security and AI capacity

This combination of deep energy expertise and broad governing experience helps officials manage the complex demands of energy security and AI development. It contributes to the rapid industrial deployment of cost-efficient open-source models, despite US chip export curbs. Energy veterans who developed technical competence alongside political judgment are uniquely prepared to anticipate and manage risks and to pursue long-term strategies. Power plants, grids and reserves take time to build, so waiting until a need becomes urgent can leave few options. Officials must judge when the benefits of early investment justify its costs and when preparing for a possible shortage is necessary.

Expanding AI capacity, for example, requires power infrastructure to be planned alongside computing facilities. In Guian, Guizhou, the supercomputing centre handles AI training orders from across China. As the surrounding data centre cluster expanded, a new 500-kilovolt substation entered service three months ahead of schedule to meet demand. The project illustrates the value of coordinating power and computing investment to bring computing capacity into use sooner and provide reliable electricity for training and running AI models.

Energy security also depends on investments made ahead of need. During the recent US-Iran war, oil stocks accumulated before the conflict helped China weather disruptions to oil supplies through the Strait of Hormuz, according to the International Energy Agency. Accumulating oil before the crisis illustrated the foresight energy planning requires – weighing demand prospects against the risk of interrupted supply. Using reserves effectively also calls for coordination across enterprises and government. China’s energy veterans can bring both industrial knowledge and governing experience to such decisions, helping the country prepare for external shocks while investing in future growth.

It is no coincidence that China has excelled in energy security while confronting the dual challenges of a global oil shortage and the massive electricity demands of the AI revolution.
