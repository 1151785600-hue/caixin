Hong Kong helps mainland Chinese firms go global – but can they really win now?

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2350 words | 2026-11-08

When Shenzhen-listed digital infrastructure provider Runjian Corporation expanded into the Middle East and Southeast Asia through Hong Kong, adapting meant more than translating software interfaces or navigating local data privacy laws.

It also meant changing the company’s working practices and abandoning the expectation, carried over from its domestic operations, that one mainland Chinese employee could match the output of three local hires.

“When expanding overseas, companies must do more than just take their products and technology abroad. We also need to respect local culture and truly integrate into the local market,” said Athena Zhao Hua, Runjian’s vice-president and president of its overseas business group. “When in Rome, you have to align with the local rhythm.”

The adjustments ranged from installing prayer rooms and arranging halal catering for team-building activities to an unwritten rule for Chinese staff: avoid eating or drinking in front of local colleagues fasting during Ramadan.

While Runjian relied on the Hong Kong government to establish local connections when it entered a new territory, such cultural adaptation was learned through its own hard-won experience. Zhao said she hoped Hong Kong could offer more targeted cultural training, market intelligence and links to local business networks to help expanding firms acclimatise more quickly.

Runjian was one of 340 mainland Chinese enterprises spearheading their global expansion with the help of Hong Kong’s GoGlobal Task Force over the past year. Its experience reflected how mainland enterprises venturing into Southeast and Central Asia were evolving, navigating steep cultural learning curves to survive.

While Hong Kong has positioned itself as an indispensable springboard – offering the cultural insights, regulatory advice and commercial networks these firms lacked – their rapid maturation poses a critical test for the city.

According to a local think tank, the real challenge is whether the city’s role as a “superconnector” can offer enough strategic depth and durability to remain relevant once these firms learn to stand on their own feet.

Ryan Ip Man-ki, vice-president of the think tank Our Hong Kong Foundation and executive director of its Public Policy Institute, said the task force had a positive start.

“The next test, however, should be the depth and durability of this engagement, rather than just the number of enterprises assisted,” he said.

“We should look at whether enterprises establish international functions, deploy talent and capital, and continue to use Hong Kong as they expand into overseas markets.”

East meets West

In an exclusive interview with the South China Morning Post, Secretary for Commerce and Economic Development Algernon Yau Ying-wah said the 340 mainland firms the task force had helped operated across sectors including logistics, construction, finance and technology. About 30 per cent sought to establish corporate treasury centres in Hong Kong to raise capital or handle offshore trading.

“Hong Kong is a place where East meets West. We had a good connection with the Western world for so many years in the past,” said Yau, who overseas the task force.

“We understand the way business is being done, and we understand their culture and language.”

He added that mainland enterprises did not have sufficient information regarding the situation in other countries, including legal systems, business practices, culture, communication, languages and professional services.

To bridge that gap, the government’s Economic and Trade Offices, together with offices operated by the Hong Kong Trade Development Council and InvestHK, provided information on countries’ legal requirements and business environments. The combined network spans 68 cities and covers 129 economies.

InvestHK, the government’s investment promotion arm, helped these enterprises plan and coordinate their visits to Hong Kong. It arranged meetings with potential partners and accompanied firms on key site visits to map out their internationalisation strategies, prioritise localisation and understand local culture.

It also organised industry-specific forums and media round tables where companies could share their expertise, connect with global industry stakeholders and strengthen their brand positioning.

The Trade Development Council also launched the GoGlobal Cross-sectoral Professional Services Platform, with a database of 200 qualified Hong Kong service providers in fields including law, accounting, testing and certification, to support companies expanding abroad.

“We work closely with companies to understand their needs, explore application scenarios in Hong Kong, and match them with suitable partners,” Yau said, adding that the government actively helped companies identify land suitable for AI data centres and other R&D operations.

With more than 60 million small and medium-sized enterprises on the mainland, Yau acknowledged that carefully matching companies with suitable markets was essential to avoid wasted effort.

“How can we take all of them going overseas? I think until the end of my life, I will not be able to take all of them going overseas. So, the most important thing is to do the matching correctly” he said.

“In all different places on the mainland, enterprises are willing to go overseas, but we have to be selective to pick the right enterprise to the right place at the right time, to the right country, with the right focus … Otherwise, that will be a waste of effort and time.

“Let’s say mining companies going to Uzbekistan or Kazakhstan will work, but if they are going to other places, it does not work.”

Yau added that Hong Kong’s overseas offices were identifying local market needs and matching them with suitable mainland enterprises. Those companies were then invited to establish a base in Hong Kong, raise capital and recruit local staff before expanding abroad.

He said officials and agencies supporting the effort included the chief executive, Yau himself, the Belt and Road Office, the Trade Development Council and InvestHK.

That matching process is increasingly being handled through a cross-border pipeline with Shenzhen.

Earlier this year, Shenzhen’s Qianhai Authority repositioned itself as an onshore scout to identify suitable companies and refer them to Hong Kong as a springboard for international expansion. The approach complemented Hong Kong’s efforts to attract overseas businesses into the Greater Bay Area.

“So, the point is how we are going to complement each other and use the strength of each other to make the Go Global effort even stronger and more eventful and fruitful,” Yau said.

Gary Wong Chi-him, chief Hong Kong and Macau liaison expert at the Qianhai Authority, said the Shenzhen Qianhai international business e-station had recorded 1.5 million visits and attracted 55,000 registered users since its launch in July 2024. The platform connects government services with market resources to help Chinese enterprises expand globally.

“By seamlessly combining Hong Kong’s world-class financial infrastructure with Qianhai’s status as the Chinese mainland’s institutional innovation pilot zone, the two regions are actively helping domestic firms scale internationally while simultaneously bringing global enterprises into the GBA,” he said.

Wong cited Nailoong, a chubby yellow cartoon dinosaur developed by a Qianhai-based company, as an example of how businesses were working with Hong Kong’s creative industries to take their intellectual property worldwide.

In the first half of 2026, Qianhai’s modern services sector recorded 25.01 billion yuan in foreign capital actually utilised, accounting for 76.5 per cent of Shenzhen’s total. It also attracted 1,335 new foreign-invested firms.

Actual utilised capital from Hong Kong reached 24 billion yuan, while 1,092 new Hong Kong-funded enterprises were established in Qianhai.

According to Wong, Qianhai was home to 11,333 Hong Kong-funded enterprises in total.

“The integration of Qianhai and Hong Kong establishes a powerful ‘dual headquarters’ infrastructure designed to eliminate operational friction and mitigate geopolitical risks for modern enterprises,” he said.

‘If you do not go out, you get out’

Behind Runjian’s willingness to absorb those cultural challenges in the workplace is fierce domestic competition.

“As the saying now goes in China, ‘If you do not go out, you get out’,” Zhao said, explaining that companies risked being pushed out of the market if they failed to expand overseas.

Since beginning its international expansion through Hong Kong in 2019, Runjian has entered Indonesia, Thailand, Malaysia, the Philippines and Singapore, achieving a compound annual growth rate of 33 per cent in its overseas business.

Earlier this year, the company expanded into Saudi Arabia after identifying opportunities in new energy to support AI development in the Middle East, amid geopolitical tensions and surging oil prices.

Zhao said Runjian had sought advice from the Hong Kong government on local policy incentives, compliance requirements, tax efficiency and whether to invest directly through its mainland parent company or its Hong Kong subsidiary for its expansion into Saudi Arabia.

With demand for new energy and data centres rising, the company also planned to expand into Central Asia, including Kyrgyzstan and Kazakhstan.

Despite Runjian’s extensive international footprint, it continued to rely on the Hong Kong government’s backing across various expansion phases.

Zhao said Runjian was part of a delegation Yau led to Malaysia in August, where the company was connected to senior local officials, business chambers, legal bodies and potential business partners, even though it first entered the country in 2023.

She noted that the Hong Kong government could connect the company to high-level contacts that it normally would not have access to, adding that this official endorsement also bolstered local clients’ trust in the firm.

Zhao also said that as the company gained a foothold locally, it faced another challenge.

“How do we better adapt to local markets or we risk operational friction?” she said.

In Indonesia, its first overseas market, Runjian recruited local executives to gradually replace Chinese staff in senior leadership roles. Zhao said those executives had a better understanding of the local business environment, sharpening the company’s competitive edge.

While Runjian focused on adapting its operations to local cultures, other Chinese businesses sought to take centuries-old cultural traditions to international markets.

State-owned Guangzhou Pharmaceutical Corporation (GPC), the parent company of herbal tea brand Walovi, recently drew global attention with viral advertising campaigns featuring Norwegian football star Erling Haaland during the World Cup.

Tracing its roots to the Chen Li Ji pharmacy, founded in 1600, GPC established a presence in Hong Kong in 1988 to pursue international markets. Last year, it based its global research and development and treasury operations in the city.

In a statement, the company cited Hong Kong’s strengths in scientific research, talent, capital, access to global resources and international communications.

According to GPC, Walovi’s international footprint has expanded 6.5-fold over the past decade to cover more than 100 countries. Recent launches included Riyadh, Kuala Lumpur and Sydney, while a new international headquarters opened in Singapore in August.

The company also planned to establish an overseas production line for its flagship drink.

But taking a traditional Chinese medicine business global presented a different set of challenges. GPC said it had encountered fragmented overseas regulatory frameworks, complex drug registration systems and strict cold-chain distribution requirements for medical products.

Despite those hurdles, the Fortune 500 company was pressing ahead with plans to establish a subsidiary in Vietnam and explore emerging markets in Central Asia.

With some of its medicines already registered and available overseas, GPC aimed to expand the international reach of its products and brand while promoting the industry and the culture of traditional Chinese medicine.

“We are committed to preserving and promoting traditional Chinese medicine culture, allowing consumers worldwide to experience its unique charm,” the company said.

Evolving role

Lawmaker Rock Chen Chung-nin said Hong Kong’s role had evolved from providing basic business referrals to serving as a high-value regional operating base, a problem-solving centre and a “resource allocation centre” for more mature enterprises.

He said the city could attract mainland enterprises to establish regional headquarters and treasury centres in Hong Kong for cross-border settlement, remittances and financing. He also suggested Hong Kong further refine listing mechanisms and lower listing thresholds for technology and innovation enterprises and small and medium-sized enterprises.

Chen said the government could establish “market-corridor teams” for priority markets such as Asean, Central Asia and the Middle East to combine the practical experience of mature enterprises with the objective insights of Hong Kong professionals to build highly resilient, compliance-ready international business operations.

“In my view, the challenge is not a lack of willingness among Hong Kong service providers; rather, it is the increasing complexity of the overseas expansion environment,” he said.

He added that the government should remind professional service providers to proactively assist enterprises in assessing geopolitical risks, data governance and legal and regulatory compliance in target markets.

It should also address the talent gap in emerging markets such as the Middle East and Central Asia, and expand outreach on the mainland and strengthen interdepartmental coordination.

Ip of Our Hong Kong Foundation said that although some mainland companies could share their experiences in overseas expansion and operations, such knowledge was often highly market-specific. Moreover, they could not provide the tailored professional services other companies needed.

He said companies that had already expanded globally still needed high-quality professional services and a hub to cluster their international functions.

Hong Kong could provide highly customised support with its comprehensive professional services. “Hong Kong can offer these unique, irreplaceable values,” he said.

Looking ahead, Ip said Hong Kong could continue to play a key role in facilitating the overseas expansion of mainland enterprises.

He said the city should move from one-stop services to end-to-end support, from setting up in Hong Kong to operating overseas. It should also strengthen its role as a place where enterprises could test, certify and showcase products and services for global markets.

Ip also urged the government to deepen market-specific support, focus on creating lasting economic value for Hong Kong and strengthen the city’s role as a two-way gateway for both companies expanding overseas and those entering the mainland.

“The objective should gradually move from helping companies ‘go through’ Hong Kong to ‘grow through and expand in’ Hong Kong,” he said.

“It means that enterprises do not simply use Hong Kong for a one-off service to access overseas markets, but continue to use Hong Kong’s professional and financial services, retain substantive business functions here, and generate sustained economic value for the city.”
