3 new pastry chefs in Hong Kong who are redefining desserts with innovative twists on classics

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1615 words | 2026-09-17

Upon entering a restaurant, the way you are greeted and seen to your table provides that vital first impression. But come the end of a meal, it is up to dessert to ensure diners leave on a high note, no matter what came before.

“It’s the finale … a bad dessert can ruin the meal,” insisted Anne McManus, then executive pastry chef at Manhattan’s Maloney & Porcelli, in an interview in 2000.

Since the pandemic, as Hong Kong’s F&B industry deals with a labour shortage and a change in dining habits, the role of many pastry chefs has shifted. No longer is their sole focus crafting plated desserts. Now, they are frequently a more involved member of the wider chef’s team, responsible too for innovation and polishing recipes for takeaway.

Despite these changes, Hongkongers’ fascination with sweet treats remains strong, and not all restaurants are abandoning the old ways. This summer, three new faces are making waves in the dessert scene, facing down the challenges and looking to leave a lasting impression.

Eve Minialai – Louise

Born in Hong Kong but having relocated to France with her family at just one year old, Eve Minialai spent her formative years in Europe’s culinary heartland.

After an apprenticeship at La Violette in Paris in 2014, the then future pastry chef perfected her craft with stints at Hotel Plaza Athénée alongside the legendary Alain Ducasse, and as a pastry sous chef at Pavyllon Paris under patissier Thomas Moulin and owner Yannick Alléno.

Working with some of the most celebrated chefs in the world provided Minialai with a thorough education, albeit a particular French one.

“Everything is different working in Paris,” she says. “The management style, the mentality and the market are all different to anywhere else in the world … including satisfying the French palate, with their stronger preference for crispy textures over Hong Kong’s preference for smoother textures.”

Now back in Hong Kong, in her first role in the city as head pastry chef for French fine dining restaurant Louise, Minialai is channelling all her abilities into crafting desserts for a clientele that also prefers them “sweet but not too sweet”. As a result, Minialai, an avid fan of contemporary pastry, has been creating innovative takes on traditional desserts. “My creations are the classics but with modern nuances. I don’t want to fixate on change for the sake of change,” she says.

The use of a calamansi sorbet in her crepes Suzette is a case in point. Swapping the traditional ice cream for a sorbet brings a refreshing zing of citrus tang that cuts through the buttery orange sauce. The Paris-Brest works according to the same principle. There’s an explosive abundance of roasted hazelnuts in every bite, but set against crunchy praline and a tender choux pastry exterior, the result is sufficiently sweet.

Minialai enjoys working with variations on a common theme. Her new Pineapple dessert is just that – a varying array of preparations of the tropical fruit: roasted, pickled, infused and more. The Rice is similar, featuring everything from a Niigata rice pudding to caramelised puffed rice, each take offering bewitching and contrasting textures.

Leo Faler – Cristal Room by Anne-Sophie Pic

Although barely 30, Leo Faler, head pastry chef at Cristal Room by Anne-Sophie Pic, has spent much of his career working alongside culinary masters. Born in Normandy, he did all his pastry training in France, eventually working alongside Yannick Alléno before moving to Singapore, and subsequently Hong Kong, for roles with Anne-Sophie Pic.

“I learned to make desserts with my mother when I was small,” Faler tells us. “Growing up in a family surrounded by home-grown fruits and vegetables for sweets and bakes helped me understand the science and art of desserts, especially the healthier options that bring a smile without [you] feeling bloated or unhealthy.”

Working for the likes of Anne-Sophie Pic, Faler now has to design a list of desserts that highlight the French chef’s signature style and flavour profiles. Often, for each new menu, the signature mille-feuille blanc gets a makeover, together with the creation of a chocolate dessert, an item featuring fruit and nuts, and a further optional fruit-based dessert that rotates according to what’s available that season. “The seasons are the most honest source for inspiration,” Faler says in summary.

After two years in Hong Kong, Faler believes he has established a sound understanding of local tastes. His Pineapple Vacherin, for instance, pairs pineapple with lovage, a herb with vegetal notes most similar to celery. This dessert combines herb-cooked pineapple cubes with a sorbet of pineapple, as well as lovage, green lovage tuile and lovage powder dusted on French meringue and pistachio shortbread. Its refreshing balance of sweet and sour provides a beguiling invitation to appreciate the popular European herb.

Raspberry and Seaweed is another playful harmony of flavours. The fruit comes in forms of a pâte de fruits confit, gastrique and sorbet, each sweetly paired with the micro cress and ice cream of sea fennel, with deep-fried dried seaweed sprinkled all over the dessert for a dry crunch. The striking contrast of flame red and forest green yields a highly photogenic dessert, and a beloved melange of herbs helps bring out the sweetness of the raspberries.

Faler’s recent Chocolate dessert introduces a playful twist on traditional after-dinner chocolate mints. Featuring five different kinds of mint, a soft chocolate sponge cake and minty chocolate mousse are stacked up against chocolate crèmeux and tempered chocolate in a vibrant mix. Another of his desserts saw a spread of pandan-infused coconut tapioca and coconut pandan sorbet pay homage to his time in Singapore.

“At the beginning, guests often responded that our desserts were too sweet, with ingredients too unfamiliar to them, such as rose geranium, rhubarb and tonka beans. We respect our guests’ palates and we’ve learned to slowly introduce these ingredients to them while making sure to remember what they enjoy the most. After all, our job as pastry chefs is to create sweet satisfaction for our guests.”

Astoria Koh – Caprice

Despite having worked at Caprice for seven years, where she is a crucial member of the chef’s team led by Guillaume Galliot, seasoned pastry chef Astoria Koh has only recently come into the spotlight.

Starting out, the Singaporean native worked part-time in a local bakery in the Lion City while majoring in interior architecture and design at university. The job helped her gain expertise, both theoretical and hands-on, in breads and pastries. Once she graduated, she chose to make pastries her career.

Determined to pursue her dream, Koh moved to Macau to work under Galliot when he was at City of Dreams’ The Tasting Room. She spent five years there working with him designing degustation menus, where the sweets needed to cohere with savoury courses. She returned to Singapore for a nine month stint working for Joël Robuchon before an offer arrived to reunite with Galliot, by then in Hong Kong, at Caprice, where she has been ever since.

“When I first started, I tried to jam in everything to show my guests that I could do it all,” Koh says. “It was only later that I really came to agree with the notion that ‘simplicity is best’. It’s never easy to execute but I’m up for the challenge.”

Koh introduces to us a trio of desserts that are now staples at Caprice.

The Strawberry is an annual dish that changes style and content each time strawberry season comes round. The 2025 version leverages the flexibility of the fruit – presenting it in the form of ice cream, yogurt mousse and consomme, accented with a fanned display of gariguette strawberries and semolina sable for texture. Visually straightforward, the dessert relies on surprises in texture and flavour to impress – the strawberries’ sweetness is balanced with just the right amount of tartness from the yogurt and lemon cream.

The festive Chocolate dessert is a playful interpretation of pairing chocolate and caramel, where the caramel cremeux and praline sable match well with chocolate mousse, whipped ganache, dark chocolate ice cream, and a sharp passion fruit caramel. That tartness can often be found in Astoria’s creations, lending a balancing element to tame the inherent sweetness in desserts, creating an alchemy, if not illusion, of a less-sweet dessert while also offering a wider range of textures too.

If there is one skill Koh hasn’t quite mastered yet, it’s customer interaction. She rarely shares her creations on social media, instead creating a feed filled with travelogues and K-pop concerts

“Being a chef is hard work but … I learned that I can do my best work when I can be myself when I’m not in the kitchen,” she says. “I feel my hobbies and fondness for K-pop give me room to breathe and relax. They often get me pumping and filled with high spirits to work harder when I come back from concerts.”

Koh says she is working on becoming more sociable and better at introducing her work through interactions with her guests, adding that whatever nerves she displays on the outside, inwardly, sharing the nuances of her creations makes her feel invincible as a chef. Clearly, her efforts to get her where she is today have been worth the long hours of dedicated practice.
