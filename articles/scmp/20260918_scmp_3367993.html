South Korea’s president rejects US request for Iran war support

SCMP | FREE | 1019 words | 2026-09-18

South Korean President Lee Jae Myung has drawn a clear line against any US request for Seoul to join the war against Iran, explicitly ruling out the dispatch of troops or military assets that could involve his country in the conflict.

The stance comes amid mounting public opposition to any military deployment and growing pressure from Washington for Seoul to contribute to efforts to secure freedom of navigation through the Strait of Hormuz.

Lee’s government faces the difficult task of balancing its security alliance with the United States against the risks of becoming entangled in a distant conflict that could expose South Korean forces, citizens and commercial interests to Iranian retaliation.

“There will be no dispatch of troops or military assets that would lead to involvement in a conflict. There will be no engagement or involvement in war. I can say that clearly,” Lee said during a televised press conference on Friday. “We’ve been sticking to this principle, and this principle won’t change.”

His remarks represent the clearest signal yet of Seoul’s position regarding US President Donald Trump’s demands. Washington argues that countries benefiting from American operations in the Strait of Hormuz should contribute to the military effort.

South Korea initially appeared to be weighing a role in the strategic waterway, sending a fact-finding team to the Middle East while balancing US pressure against Iranian threats of retaliation. A security briefing on the team’s findings – originally scheduled for Thursday – did not take place ahead of Lee’s press conference.

Lee ruled out sending combat troops or placing them under foreign command, an issue of particular sensitivity as his administration seeks to regain wartime operational control over South Korean forces from the US military leadership by 2030.

He acknowledged that distinguishing between defensive protection and military involvement could be challenging, but he said Seoul would not cross that line.

“Let me make it clear once again: there will be no deployment for war,” Lee said.

However, Lee said South Korea would maintain “the minimum level of activities” to protect its merchant ships, crude oil transport routes and citizens abroad, pointing to the deployment of the Cheonghae Unit – a naval anti-piracy task force operating in Middle Eastern waters.

He said the government was considering ways to strengthen the unit’s defensive capabilities without escalating into direct military intervention.

Trump’s pressure

South Korea’s president noted that several other countries had deployed military assets near the Strait of Hormuz, with some committing to post-conflict recovery while others focused strictly on protecting commercial shipping and their citizens.

Yang Moo-jin, a professor at the University of North Korean Studies, said Lee “firmly stated his stance against dispatching military assets or troops to the raging war”.

But he noted that Seoul had promised to support post-war efforts to restore freedom of navigation through the strait, a route that accounted for roughly 70 per cent of South Korea’s crude oil imports before the war and a significant share of its natural gas and petrochemical feedstocks.

“His statement on this issue will help put domestic controversies and concerns to rest, although Washington is unlikely to be happy with it,” Yang said.

Doo Jin-ho, a research fellow at the Institute for Far Eastern Studies at Kyungnam University, said Lee had made it clear that a deployment would only be for “post-conflict multinational stabilisation efforts”, such as mine clearing.

The Trump administration has repeatedly singled out South Korea over its reluctance to help the US in its war against Iran. Speaking to reporters at the White House last month, Trump recounted a conversation in which Lee rejected his request for support.

“I called him, I said, ‘Would you like to give us a little hand? We don’t need help with Iran. But if you would like, give us a hand with Iran.’ He said, ‘No thanks’,” Trump recalled. “And I said, ‘Wait a minute. We have 39,000 soldiers over there guarding you from Kim Jong-un, your next-door neighbour. And you’re not going to help us on a very easy military operation in Iran?’ That’s strange.”

South Korea hosts about 28,500 US troops.

A delicate balancing act

Aside from Hormuz, the two allies are currently navigating several other major economic, trade and security issues.

These include the implementation of South Korea’s commitment to a US$350 billion investment package in the United States – which includes US$150 billion for shipbuilding – regulatory scrutiny over US-listed technology platforms such as Coupang and stalled progress on Seoul’s acquisition of nuclear-powered submarines.

Against this backdrop, South Korean Foreign Minister Cho Hyun left for Washington on Thursday for talks with US Secretary of State Marco Rubio.

Domestically, Lee is also under pressure. Jhee Byong-kuen, a political-science professor at Chosun University, said that the president was “putting his foot down” on the deployment issue to avoid further damaging his sliding approval ratings, which took a hit from recent real estate reforms.

Hundreds of South Korean civic, religious and labour organisations have held rallies demanding that the government reject US demands. Opposition to military action has also surfaced within the ruling coalition.

“Even US public opinion polls show that 66 per cent of Americans do not support this war. We could be driving our young people into a situation where their lives and safety are jeopardised,” said Woo Won-shik of the ruling Democratic Party of Korea, a former National Assembly speaker.

South Korea sent about 320,000 troops to the Vietnam war – the second-largest foreign force after the US – and sent 3,600 personnel from the non-combat Zaytun Division to northern Iraq in 2004 under liberal president Roh Moo-hyun.

The Iraqi deployment sparked deep domestic political divisions and severely eroded Roh’s support base. Roh later reflected in his memoir, Destiny: “I authorised the deployment not because I believed it was right, but because it was an unavoidable choice … The Iraq troop deployment will go down in history as a wrong decision.”

Professor Jhee said: “President Lee must have learned a lesson from former president Roh’s bitter experience”.
