Uzbekistan tests US-China AI rift with bid to join rival tech bloc

SCMP | FREE | 1153 words | 2026-08-28

The Central Asian country of Uzbekistan has approached the United States about joining Pax Silica, Washington’s flagship initiative on artificial intelligence (AI) supply chains, a month after signing up for China’s own global grouping, the World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization (Waico).

Uzbekistan’s ambassador to the US, Furqat Sidikov, said Tashkent had formally notified the US State Department in a diplomatic note last week requesting to join the US-led initiative.

“Now, the US should confirm. We are ready to sign,” he said in Washington on Thursday.

Sidikov added that countries should have a “chance to be part of any kind of international cooperation”, and that drawing red lines around participation would be “counterproductive”.

“This gives us opportunities, and it’s good competition,” he said.

The move comes as countries face growing pressure to choose between Washington and Beijing in the race for AI dominance, with both powers promoting competing visions to shape the technology’s standards, supply chains and strategic partnerships.

Kazakhstan, also in Central Asia, is the only nation so far with membership in both Pax Silica and Waico.

Reuters reported on August 14 that Washington planned to warn partners they could not join both Pax Silica and China’s rival AI framework, citing an internal draft.

Earlier this month, a US State Department official told the South China Morning Post that Washington was “actively engaging with global partners to advance responsible, pro-innovation, pro-growth AI development, both bilaterally and through multilateral forums”.

The 24-member Pax Silica aims to build AI supply chains independent of China, covering critical minerals, semiconductors and logistics.

While the programme does not explicitly target China, one of its key objectives is to reduce “coercive dependencies” – a phrase widely understood to mean Chinese dominance in rare earths and other minerals essential to semiconductor production.

Pax Silica architect Jacob Helberg, who serves as US undersecretary of state for economic affairs, said last month that while China had pursued a state-led approach, the US and its allies were leveraging “incredible companies that build transformative products that people really love”.

“We realise that the US government is not set up in a way to compete with the Chinese tit-for-tat on that same basis,” Helberg told the SCMP in an exclusive interview.

“We decided to lean into that strength and leverage the innovative, creative capacity and dynamism of our private sector,” he said.

Waico, which describes its mission as advancing “AI for good and for all”, was launched by Chinese President Xi Jinping in Shanghai last month during the World Artificial Intelligence Conference, with 29 founding members. The grouping has since expanded, adding nine more members.

Xi called it a “major move by China to answer the call of the Global South and unite the international community together to promote vigorously AI development and governance”.

“It will be an important milestone in the history of AI development,” he added.

Uzbekistan has large reserves of gold, copper, uranium, tungsten and molybdenum. Officials say new mines and processing plants could produce up to 28 types of critical minerals, including rare earths and rare metals. The country has also made a US$4.2 billion minerals programme a key part of its industrial policy through 2030.

China already has a strong presence in Uzbekistan, with foreign direct investment reaching about US$10.7 billion as of the middle of last year, according to the Eurasian Development Bank.

China’s pipeline of projects in the country spans energy, mining and potential data centres, and its proximity to Central Asia makes it a difficult partner to displace.

US President Donald Trump’s administration is seeking a bigger role in the region. In February, the US and Uzbekistan signed an agreement on critical minerals and rare earths, which was followed by a Development Finance Corporation (DFC) investment framework and a proposed joint holding company.

For Washington, dual membership in Waico and Pax Silica clashes with the latter’s mandate, which aims to keep critical minerals, processing and transit routes inside a trusted, China-free chain.

Middle powers see the opposite challenge. They cannot build a full AI stack alone, and they do not want to treat AI as a loyalty test.

“As a developing country, we want to have access to all available technologies,” said Bobur Khodjaev, an aide with Uzbekistan’s presidential administration, speaking at the Silk Road Finance and Technology Forum in Tashkent this week.

“American tech companies offer cutting-edge AI chips and AI models. China, in turn, offers open-source models that are significantly cheaper than American ones. And we would like to have access to both,” he added.

Ken Moriyasu, senior fellow at the Washington-based Hudson Institute, said it was “unrealistic” to expect countries such as Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan to make an “exclusive geopolitical choice between American and Chinese technology”.

“Their entire foreign policy is built around avoiding precisely that kind of dependence,” he said, stressing that Washington must find a way to protect “genuinely sensitive” technologies without turning technological safeguards into a geopolitical loyalty test.

Moriyasu contended that the larger strategic objective was not to make Kazakhstan or Uzbekistan choose America. “It is to make sure they always have a choice – and that they never have to choose China exclusively,” he said.

Kazakhstan is the live test, and the region is watching. The country “shares an enormous border with China. No government in Astana can pursue a serious strategy based on excluding Beijing,” Moriyasu said.

Political scientist Zamir Karazhanov told The Times of Central Asia that Astana was “probably closer to the Chinese approach at the moment” on open source and keeping AI out of bloc politics.

“Kazakhstan also does not want AI to be used as an instrument of politics, and on this point, we agree with China,” he said.

“I think an attempt to pressure Kazakhstan would be unsuccessful,” he said, adding that Washington could not easily squeeze a country that held minerals it needed.

Indonesia is running a parallel hedge. While the country has signed on to Waico, Indonesian Coordinating Minister for Economic Affairs Airlangga Hartarto has insisted Jakarta is still “involved in the development of Pax Silica” and that the two groupings “should not be pitted against each other”.

Malaysia is also a founding member of Waico, despite its deep US semiconductor ties.

A Chatham House paper on sovereign AI from February argued that middle powers could not match the US or China in capital, computing power or talent, so they must choose where to compete, where to partner and where to accept dependence.

“The critical question is not whether to depend on other nations for AI, but on which nations, to what extent and on what terms,” it said.
