How the Zhuhai air show charted China’s path from catching up to flying high

SCMP | FREE | 1283 words | 2026-11-02

When Zhuhai hosted its inaugural air show in November 1996, the event showcased the J-8IIM fighter, a second-generation fighter equipped with Russian radar and weapons, alongside a limited line-up of home-made aircraft.

Imported planes were the focus and Chinese audiences curiously studied the much more advanced foreign designs. They crowded around two Russian fourth-generation Su-27 fighters and were wowed as the jets performed their famous cobra manoeuvre, sharply braking in mid-air.

At the time, China had yet to build its own fourth-generation fighter.

But over the next three decades, China went from being heavily reliant on Russian technology to becoming the only country other than the United States to have flight-tested sixth-generation fighters.

The Zhuhai air show – officially the China International Aviation and Aerospace Exhibition – has charted this journey year by year, becoming a major global event and a showcase for the nation’s growing military might.

This year’s edition has drawn extra attention, not only because it marks the event’s 30th anniversary but also because it comes ahead of the People’s Liberation Army’s centenary on August 1 next year, described by Beijing as a key milestone for the military’s modernisation.

International participation has doubled since 1996, with nearly 50 countries taking part this year. More than 1,100 exhibitors are expected for the December 7-13 event in the southern Guangdong city, up from around 400 at its debut.

The scale of the exhibition has also grown – from 8,000 square metres (86,110 square feet) 30 years ago to 135,000 square metres this year. More than 3,000 pieces of equipment will be on display, including 170 aircraft. There will also be a dedicated naval section for the first time.

The event mirrors China’s growing aviation confidence, showcasing advanced systems earlier than in the past for both domestic enthusiasts and prospective foreign buyers.

For example, the show did not feature China’s J-10 until 2008, 10 years after the fourth-generation fighter jet conducted its initial flight. The J-20, China’s most advanced stealth fighter in service, was first displayed five years after its 2011 maiden flight.

Now, China is not only exhibiting its most advanced operational aircraft but also offering static displays of platforms still in development.

In 2024, an airborne drone mother ship called Jiutian debuted at the air show, a year ahead of its maiden flight.

“Why could a drone that has not been flight-tested be shown at the air show? It’s because China has every confidence to send it into the sky,” military expert Fu Qianshao was quoted as saying by state broadcaster CCTV on October 14.

Overcoming underestimation

In July 2009, then US defence secretary Robert Gates said in a speech that the US should cap its production of fifth-generation stealth fighters because China would not have a fifth-generation jet by 2020. A week later, the US Senate voted in support of this policy.

At the time, the Chinese military’s most cutting-edge planes were the fourth-generation J-10A and J-11.

But less than two years later, in January 2011, while Gates was meeting with then Chinese president Hu Jintao in Beijing, the J-20 fifth-generation stealth fighter performed its maiden flight.

On his flight back, Gates admitted that US intelligence had underestimated how quickly China was developing stealth technology.

“What we had seen was that they were working on a stealth aircraft. What we didn’t know was how far along they were. They were clearly further along than our intelligence suggested,” Gates said.

Beijing may be hoping to surprise Washington once again with its sixth-generation fighters.

The US and China remain the only countries flight-testing such fighters. While there is no concrete sign of who is ahead, US Air Force Brigadier General Timothy Helfrich said last month that the US F-47 would be “the world’s first sixth-generation fighter”.

Meanwhile, China’s two tailless next-generation fighters, commonly known as the J-36 and J-50, have been observed undergoing test flights since December 2024.

General David Allvin, chief of staff of the US Air Force, said in 2025 that two prototypes of the American sixth-generation F-47 had been flying for five years. Beijing went further by releasing what is believed to be official footage of one of its sixth-generation fighters in June.

Fu said the competitiveness of China’s military aviation had undergone “earth-shaking” changes in the last 30 years, citing engines as an example.

“Compared to the US, it’s basically a tie. Even though the US is still ahead of China because it started engine installation much earlier, it has been moving quite slowly in the past few decades.”

He said China held the leading position in terms of radar, avionics and air-to-air missiles.

China’s fleet of advanced fighter jets has expanded rapidly in recent years. According to a January report by the London-based Royal United Services Institute, the PLA Air Force operated an estimated 320 to 350 J-20 fifth-generation stealth fighters by late 2025, up from around 50 in 2020.

Its fleet of J-16 advanced fourth-generation multi-role fighters grew from around 90 to 100 to around 450 over the same period, according to the report.

China is also seeking to expand its arms exports. The customer base for its fourth-generation J-10CE fighter has grown to include Uzbekistan and Pakistan, while Bangladesh is poised to become the third buyer.

Power projection

The transformation of the Chinese air force is also evident in its increasingly assertive deployments.

Xu Anxiang, then deputy commander of the PLA Air Force, said at the 2018 Zhuhai air show that China aimed to build a world-class strategic air force by the middle of the century – a goal in line with Beijing’s pledge to build a world-class military by that time.

In response to the August 2022 visit to Taiwan by then US House speaker Nancy Pelosi, Beijing mounted large-scale joint military exercises around the island.

The operations integrated PLA fighters, bombers, naval vessels and missile forces to rehearse blockade scenarios, air dominance and coordinated strikes against land and sea targets.

In October 2023, a Chinese J-11 fighter came within about 10 feet (3 metres) of a US B-52 bomber over the South China Sea, according to the Pentagon. While Washington called the nighttime encounter unsafe, Beijing countered that US military flights near China were the real cause of security risks.

According to Japan’s defence ministry, there were 1,000 take-offs and landings from China’s Liaoning and Shandong aircraft carriers in May and June last year.

The two carriers have repeatedly conducted drills in the western Pacific, the latest of which began last month and remains under way.

The PLA has also conducted long-range bomber patrols, such as when two Chinese H-6 bombers and two Russian Tu-95 bombers entered Alaska’s air defence identification zone in July 2024 in their first joint bomber operation.

China has also demonstrated its growing ability to project air power through back-to-back joint exercises with Egypt in 2025 and 2026.

For the inaugural Eagles of Civilisation exercise last year, China sent J-10C fighters, KJ-500 airborne early-warning aircraft and YY-20 aerial-refuelling tankers to Egypt, marking its first deployment of an integrated air force detachment to Africa.

Some observers, however, note that operational hurdles remain before Beijing can mount sustained global operations.

Lyle Goldstein, Asia programme director at Washington-based think tank Defence Priorities, said that while the PLA Air Force was advancing “by leaps and bounds” and was “truly formidable” close to home, it could still find it hard to operate far from China as it “lacks a robust refuelling capability or distant bases”.

“It’s very difficult to know how the US and Chinese air forces would compete in head-to-head combat.”
