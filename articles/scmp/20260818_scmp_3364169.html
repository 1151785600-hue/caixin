Nirupama Rao and Lin Minwang on the great India-China face-off

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 4130 words | 2026-08-18

Welcome to Open Dialogue, a series from the South China Morning Post where we bring together leading voices to discuss the stories and subjects occupying international headlines.

In this edition, we invited a leading Chinese scholar of South Asian affairs and a former Indian foreign secretary and ambassador to China to reflect on the deep fault lines in India-China relations. They debate whether the biggest barrier is history or politics, clash over the border, Pakistan and India’s ties with the United States, and examine whether Beijing and New Delhi can move beyond suspicion in an increasingly multipolar world.

Lin Minwang is a professor and vice-dean at the Institute of International Studies at Fudan University, and deputy director of the university’s Centre for South Asian Studies. His research focuses on international relations in South Asia, with particular emphasis on China-India relations and China’s foreign policy. He is the author of Choosing War and The Belt and Road Initiative and Geopolitics in South Asia.

Nirupama Rao is a former Indian foreign secretary (2009-11) and the first woman to serve as India’s ambassador to China (2006-09). She also served as ambassador to the United States and high commissioner to Sri Lanka. A career diplomat with more than four decades of experience, she is the author of The Fractured Himalaya: India, Tibet, China 1949-1962.

A large section of Indian opinion still regards China as an expansionist power shaped by conflicts in 1962 and Galwan in 2020. How do you respond to that view, and what can China realistically do to change it?

Lin: Part of the reason is that the history of 1962 has become an important part of India’s national identity. Indian leaders, when explaining India’s history – or even discussing the mistakes made by Indian decision-makers at that time – have often had to describe China as an expansionist power. That is why I think it is very difficult to change this perception. Because if you want to change, you have to change all your narratives, which have been deep-rooted in the national identity narratives.

Now, we are also seeing more and more Chinese people develop negative perceptions of India. One reason is that it has become much easier for Chinese people to understand how the outside world views China. Especially after Covid-19, many Chinese internet users have found it easier to access information about how Indians perceive China.

This is part of the challenge for both countries. From the Chinese side, we have made many efforts, and personally, I have also been involved in these kinds of initiatives. But ultimately, I find that what we have done over the past 10 years has not made much progress in changing the views of the Indian elite.

Rao, do you agree that the negative perception of China is part of the national identity of India and it’s opportunism on the part of politicians trying to make China look bad? As we speak, there is also a significant part of Chinese opinion that continues to see India as chaotic and unreliable.

Rao: I believe that, as Professor Lin just now said, both countries have to change the narratives about each other. That’s a fundamental requirement but a complicated issue. It is overladen with historical memory, with perhaps prejudice from both sides, ignorance and a lack of knowledge. To my mind, what is most fundamental, is a lack of curiosity about each other, a lack of curiosity about our cultures, civilisations, histories, the nature of each country. It’s very important that knowledge becomes the fundamental basis with which both countries understand each other better. That is one point I’d like to make.

You mentioned that the Chinese opinion often sees India as chaotic and unreliable and essentially anti-China. I would answer that question by saying that mutual misperceptions have become one of the greatest obstacles to our relationship. Many Chinese observers look at India’s democracy as disorderly. They look at diversity as weakness. And from the Indian perspective, these qualities are the very source of our resilience. Ask any Indian, and he or she will very proudly proclaim that the fact that we are united in our diversity is, in fact, our greatest strength. Has India’s policy towards China been one of hostility? I really wouldn’t put it that way. But I believe that there is a fundamental lack of trust between the two countries – political trust.

We engaged with each other over the years realistically after the conflict of 1962 till the clash in the Galwan Valley happened in 2020. We had some violence on the border in 1975, but by and large that border had remained quiet, and the conflict in Galwan brought it all collapsing down literally like a house of cards because since 1988 our engagement with each other had been tempered by realism. We had sought to build confidence, even as the boundary remained unsettled. But trust obviously cannot exist independently, because experience always intervenes.

For many Indians, the events of 1962 and all the histories that have been written about it have left deep scars. In fact, my book, The Fractured Himalaya, sought to address this and put it in perspective, and to say that both countries need to understand the history of what went into the tragedy of 1962 much more deeply and much better, and then you had Galwan in 2020, which again profoundly altered Indian opinion. So the change, I don’t think, was manufactured by politicians or the media. It arose because Indians at large, by and large, felt that the understandings that had painstakingly been built over 3½ decades had been disrupted.

So, how are we to rebuild confidence? Both our sides, two of the largest countries in Asia, are next-door neighbours. There is an asymmetry of power between the two countries, and China has leapfrogged ahead, literally, of India in the last few decades. But both sides must first acknowledge that perceptions matter, and strategic stability between the two countries should begin, should be founded, should be established on strategic empathy.

Question to Lin: as Rao just mentioned, we had built that understanding over the years, and Galwan kind of made it collapse in a matter of one incident. What role do you think the border issue plays in these misconceptions and mutual suspicion of each other?

Lin: I think you have mentioned the Chinese side always likes to talk about properly handling the border issues. When I was a junior diplomat and when I read in the Indian news, most of the Indian comments said China refuses to negotiate with India on the border issues. China is willing to settle the border issues, but we have a really clear understanding of the position of both sides. If the Indian position is not going to change, China will have actually no negotiation room. There is no way to find a way we can talk about resolving the boundary issue.

Rao: When they say we should properly handle the boundary question, it’s essentially what it translates into is that we should compartmentalise it. We should set it aside. Deal with other questions which can be more easily handled and keep the boundary question to be worked upon as time goes by. But it cannot simply be set aside while other aspects of the relationship flourish as far as India is concerned, because this is not merely a territorial dispute; the boundary issue has become a measure of political trust between the two countries.

For India, as a democracy that values public opinion, we just cannot shut the door on the boundary issue and say that we have decided to build common ground in other areas, and we’ll take up the boundary issue once it’s ripe for discussion. It just doesn’t work that way, and we saw that the agreements that we had painstakingly negotiated since 1993 – the various border peace and tranquillity agreements along the Line of Actual Control – had collapsed after Galwan. India had taken into account the understanding that peace along the Line of Actual Control would enable cooperation elsewhere, and we tried, we did that for three decades. But then, what happened with Galwan? It just showed that we don’t have a completely and accurately, and in a detailed way, delineated Line of Actual Control between the two countries. And there are gaps in understanding. There are conflicts in interpretation, and that’s exactly what happened with Galwan.

So the entire premise about properly handling the boundary question has been weakened in the process. The challenge is not simply just technical disengagement between the two sides, but it has to be a restoration of confidence on several political commitments. And I come back to the question of political trust.

Question to Rao: we have had several rounds of border talks. We are expecting President Xi Jinping to visit New Delhi for the Brics summit before he goes to Washington to meet President [Donald] Trump. We saw all the Chinese journalists had to leave but now are hearing that India is thinking about issuing new visas to Chinese journalists, while China has given a few visas to Indian journalists. Where do you see these efforts going? How do you see the nature of the India-China rivalry and also the recent efforts to kind of work towards a balancing of the relationship? Do you think it is primarily a regional power competition, or a deeper contest that can direct the future of the Global South itself?

Rao: I wouldn’t overread these moves. We are trying to revert to a pattern that existed for some years, for some decades, and so you have some Indian journalists now who have returned to Beijing, and there is talk of the Xinhua correspondent being posted in Delhi, and we don’t know what follows after that. The two-way passage of information, especially for the benefit of the public, for the benefit of those who access social media and the media at large, is essential. But it has to be based on information that contributes to a better understanding. There is too much bias, too much prejudice, too much misinformation and ill-informed opinions that keep circulating. So, if there is a way to clear the air on these issues, I think that is a good step.

How do you see the nature of the India-China rivalry, especially competing for the Global South leadership position? Do you think it is primarily a regional power competition, or a deeper contest that can direct the future of the Global South itself?

Rao: There is enough space for India and China to do good within the Global South. We should have an open perspective on the question of how we deal with each other as far as the Global South is concerned, and how we deal with the Global South in a world that is becoming increasingly multipolar. And I think India and China have a lot to do to contribute to stability in a multipolar world. Their bilateral differences apart.

Lin: I think actually there is a big misunderstanding here on both sides. Regarding the Global South, China and India are not in competition. China is the largest trading partner for over 114 countries worldwide. In the economic aspect, I do think China and India have some competition, but there is not really a competitive dynamic between us. And why do we have this kind of narrative? I think it’s a basic narrative which was constructed by Western media, and unfortunately, some of the Indian friends and also some of the Chinese scholars accept these narratives. Part of the reason is the [Narendra] Modi government has not invited China to join the Global South dialogue. So for some, they see India was actually trying to push China out of the Global South agendas. I think this is not true. China was part of the Global South, and India will not be able to push China out. So that’s why I say both sides actually have a really big misunderstanding on these issues.

Question to Rao: do you agree with what Lin said?

Rao: I believe India is a presence in itself. India is a country that counts as far as the world community is concerned. It is a country that is recognised in the Global South for what it stands for: its democracy, its skilled manpower, its economic potential, and the history of the contributions that it has made to progress in many countries of the Global South. So, in that sense, I don’t believe India is punching above its weight, and I don’t believe we are essentially in competition with China when it comes to the Global South. I said before that there is enough space within the Global South for both India and China. We are both countries that belong there. We intrinsically belong. Our identities and histories are so intertwined. Our interests are so intertwined with those of the Global South. So where is the need for rivalry when it comes to this? I think the rivalry comes in within the bilateral context, within the regional context.

I would like to mention the Pakistan issue, for instance. Now, China’s relationship with Pakistan causes a lot of misunderstanding within India. There’s a long-standing strategic partnership from Beijing’s perspective with Pakistan, but why should it define China-India relations? Yet it has come to define the China-India relationship. It shapes the strategic environment for us because Pakistan is not simply any other neighbour. It remains our principal security challenge, and in the 2025 conflict, we saw the extent of Chinese support and Chinese backup for Pakistan.

We’re not expecting China to abandon Pakistan. That is not what I seek to emphasise here. But I do hope China will recognise this, and this is where mutual sensitivity is added on to mutual trust – the trust factor that I referred to.

We need sensitivity for each other’s concerns. China has to recognise that military transfers, strategic coordination and diplomatic positions taken during times of crises inevitably influence Indian perceptions. So we can’t compartmentalise these things. If you understand me, you know these are all so tangled together. It’s a very tangled web.

In recent Chinese studies, there is a suggestion that scholarship in China is moving beyond a purely geopolitical lens towards examining India’s development capacity and its state power. Lin, do you think this shift is meaningful among Chinese elite? Is China gradually starting to read India differently?

Lin: Historically, the Chinese approach to India has been heavily focused on geopolitics. So when you just focus on the geopolitics lens, then you will always tend to overstate India’s strategic importance, and on the other side, be frustrated over India’s daily policy towards China, especially now the Modi government is pro-United States. After more than 10 years’ change, the recent shift is they are trying to re-evaluate India based on actual national capacity and India’s state power. It is a much more rational lens, and would make people understand India without so much emotion. It’s a good sign and I am happy to see this trend.

Rao, do you agree with Lin’s assessment that the Modi government for a long time was very pro-United States? What are some of the things that you think make India at par with China in a lot of ways when it comes to state power and development capacity?

Rao: I think Chinese take a very narrow, prismatic view about India’s relations with the US. I don’t think they understood India’s position of strategic autonomy. It’s a very fiercely independent position. We have never hitched our wagon to the Americans, in the sense that the Chinese think we did. Of course, relations between India and the US did improve significantly. But it is every country’s sovereign right to develop these relations if they are contributing to the national interest of the country, whether it’s in science, technology, defence or nuclear technology. Did China ever come forward to help or to offer to assist India in these fields? So China should not take a one-sided view of these issues.

I think China, as a big, mature civilisation, can afford to be much more inclusive and much more broad-minded in its assessment of India’s relationships with friends, with partners, with neighbours. That is where the problem lies, and I think China has to look inside itself, do a little self-correction on its own part to understand the nature of Indian foreign policy, the nature of India’s sovereign decisions to promote its national interest and to protect its national security.

But I do welcome recent Chinese scholarship that examines India beyond familiar stereotypes. That’s very important, and that there is greater recognition today that India possesses significant state capacity, technological ambition, economic potential. I think that represents progress on the part of Chinese understanding of India. I know that all the assumptions still persist, and that India is sometimes viewed through the lens of political fragmentation rather than institutional resilience and democracy. Chinese have a fundamental misunderstanding of how democracy functions. To them, democracy appears untidy.

Everything doesn’t fall into place in democracy, the way they look at it. But that should not be mistaken for weakness. Democracy should not be mistaken for weakness. The same openness that defines India generates debate. It generates adaptability. It generates resilience. Look at India. We are now approaching the 80th year of our independence.

Look at the way we have grown. Progress has reached many sections of the population. It is a very different approach from what China has taken. But China cannot claim that its approach is the only right anointed approach as far as progress is concerned. I think a little more accommodation on the part of China would be very much welcomed. I would recommend that to my Chinese friends because I’ve been working on China for 42 years from 1984. China must understand India’s capacity to adapt. That it is one of India’s enduring strategic strengths, and that is where strategic empathy comes in.

Lin, as Rao said that strategic autonomy is the reason India is growing closer to the US, do you think China can make the same argument when it comes to its relationship with Pakistan? How do you see the argument that Rao just made that China does not understand India’s democracy and views democracy as weakness?

Lin: I would first like to talk about why the mainstream opinion in China is that the Modi government is on US lines. There is no strategic autonomy now in Delhi because the Modi government actually has signed military agreements with the United States, which are cooperation between a military alliance. Also over the past several years, India took all the US positions towards China. As the United States pursues a full range of policies to contain China, the Modi administration in India has, in essence, aligned itself with US initiatives, mirroring Washington’s positions across economic, technological, and defence policies. So that’s why we say India and the United States actually were in the same position, and why India actually has no strategic autonomy, especially in the military aspect.

On China-Pakistan relations, most Chinese will say during the Cold War or after, the US was the primary military partner for Pakistan, and America has provided the most advanced weapons to Pakistan, but over the last 10 years the situation has changed. Pakistan now tries to seek more military cooperation with China. The reason is also very clear. It’s because the Modi government signs a lot of the military treaties with the US while the Joe Biden administration did not allow Pakistan to buy some weapons from the US, then Pakistan shifted towards China. So this is the background why China and Pakistan now have more military cooperation. It is part of the change of the dynamic in South Asia.

On democracy, we have different understanding about democracy and the political system. I would say China does not have any ideological bias towards democracy. But when someone says Indians have a much better political system, I think for most of the Chinese young generation, they will see it as a joke. Every time when I lead a delegation to Delhi, there are many young faces in Chinese delegations, which are good opportunities for young generations to gain more experience and exposure. But it is not the case on the Indian side, there is no young face.

So that’s why we don’t think India’s political system is better, not just because you have the general election. China and the Communist Party also have elections. We have competition within the party, but we don’t have a general election which is nationwide, so we actually have a different political system. We also have a different assessment about political systems. This is a different perception of both sides.

Rao: I may say very frankly and candidly, it is certainly not within Professor Lin’s remit to be judgmental about the state of the youth in India and the opportunities provided to them. Given the fact that we are a democratic country with an open environment, we lay out our emotions for everybody to see whether we are happy or unhappy. We are a free country in every sense of the word. That’s the first point I’d like to say. Professor Lin referred to several military treaties that we have signed with the US. I must say this is news to me because I’m not aware of any military treaties that we have signed with the US. We have a strategic partnership. It is fully within our sovereign right to transact such a partnership with the US. It’s a relationship that has had its ups and downs. That is not a perfect relationship, but it is a relationship that has delivered for India. India is not a handmaiden of the United States.

I want to make that clear to Professor Lin. We are nobody’s handmaiden. We march to the sound of our own drum. We don’t march to the sound of anybody else’s drum. We are completely sovereign in that respect. And let us look at the history of China-Pakistan relations. Professor Lin has completely compartmentalised it and said in the last 10 years they never got any assistance from the United States. But think of the last 60 years where China and Pakistan became ironclad brothers and that iron brotherhood has lasted through thick and thin. It has extended into nuclear technology. It has extended into missile technology. It has extended to all sorts of cooperation. That is the history of China-Pakistan relations.

I remember hearing 30, 35 years ago, prime minister Li Peng of China telling one of our leaders that China can never forget the good that Pakistan has done for China through the last few years – I’m talking of the early 90s – and that is that Pakistan was responsible for the opening for China to the United States. Let’s not forget history. And as far as our young people are concerned, yes, we have agitations, we have demonstrations, people are speaking their minds. I believe the government is listening to what they have to say, and I hope these matters will be resolved to the satisfaction of our young people, because our dreams, our future really rest with the young people of India. Let’s understand India. I think the Chinese do not understand complexity. Although China is such a complex civilisation, when it comes to India’s complexity, there seems to be a mental block. I hope that block is lifted because that is the key to understanding and having better relations.

Lin: It’s actually really difficult to promote the good dialogue. I supervise two Indians, PhD candidates, in the university that I am working with. Last year, the Chinese embassy in New Delhi issued visas for more than 300,000 Indian people to China, but how many Chinese have been issued Indian visas? It’s only 2,000. So that is why the Chinese public was so angry about it. It’s really difficult for them to visit Indian or open some business in India. So that’s why I partly agree with the former ambassador Rao that we have a different understanding about the political system. It’s only a political system that is good for India, but not for China.
