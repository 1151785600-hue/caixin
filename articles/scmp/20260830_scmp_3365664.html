Visa policy brings in more foreigners on short-term jobs and HK$20,000 salaries

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1514 words | 2026-08-30

About 80 per cent of the foreign nationals granted working visas under Hong Kong’s General Employment Policy (GEP) last year were hired on short-term contracts, while nearly half of successful applicants earned less than HK$20,000 (US$2,564) a month.

According to the latest Immigration Department data, 31,278 GEP working visas for foreigners were approved last year. Mainland Chinese residents cannot apply to the scheme.

While the figure marked the second consecutive year the scheme had cleared the 30,000-level, the number was still shy of the more than 40,000 recorded annually in 2018 and 2019.

The latest statistics showed that 79.7 per cent of successful candidates, or 24,929, held short-term positions or jobs lasting less than a year. Among those granted visas, 47.5 per cent, or 14,850, were earning below HK$20,000 a month.

The proportion of approved applicants holding short-term contracts last year also marked an increase from 72.1 per cent in 2024 and 64 per cent in 2023.

The data also showed 13,640 GEP visas were approved in the first half of this year, with 68.6 per cent on short-term contracts and 49.8 per cent earning less than HK$20,000.

Alexa Chow Yee-ping, managing director of human resources consultancy ACTS Consulting, attributed the uptick in GEP visa holders taking on short-term work to the government’s mega-events drive to stimulate fresh economic growth.

“As an international city, Hong Kong needs to create its own character to retain its attractiveness and charm. Not just focus on properties and finance. We need [economic] diversification,” she said.

The department said the GEP also had a new “creative industry and performing arts services” category since April 1, with 2,340 applications approved so far.

Chow said such applicants could be performers and crew for mega-events, which also brought valuable experience to local people in event production.

Despite the temporary nature of the jobs, Chow said that such talent still benefited Hong Kong economically as mega-events drove growth in tourism, catering, retail and hospitality.

Earlier this month, the government revealed that about 1.75 million visitors were drawn to more than 130 major events between January and June this year, generating around HK$5.8 billion in spending and contributing HK$3.3 billion to the economy.

In terms of industry, the sports and recreation sector accounted for the highest number of GEP applicant approvals, at 4,517, followed by arts and culture, academic research and education, financial services, and commerce and trade.

Gordon Yau Yick-chung, a sports public relations leader and chairman of concern group Hong Kong Fitness Guide, which closely monitors sports industrialisation, said the actual number of sports events could be higher than the public knew.

“I am not surprised. The figures reflected the trend of more sports competitions held in Hong Kong, to the extent that sometimes we may not notice [a match was held],” he said.

Yau said that apart from recent high-profile friendly matches featuring European football teams, there had been many small and large international sports events and competitions in the city.

Such events included baseball, wrestling and golf as well as emerging sports such as pickleball, he said, adding that some were organised by shopping centres to draw visitors.

He said many of these international competitions hired overseas judges and organisers, while global sports icons invited for commercial events would bring their managers and entourage, creating short-term visa applications.

In terms of location, South Korea accounted for the largest proportion of approved GEP applicants last year, at 17.6 per cent.

The number of South Koreans approved for the visas surpassed 5,500 annually in 2024 and 2025, representing a surge of nearly 85 per cent from 2019.

This momentum also carried into the first half of 2026, with South Korea accounting for the largest number of approved GEP candidates, at 2,226, followed by Japan with 1,974.

Korean Sky Lee Sang-ju, 25, said he came to Hong Kong in 2024 to teach taekwondo at NRG Taekwondo Korea despite not finishing his final year at university.

The 25-year-old said he chose Hong Kong over the United States and Canada because of its lower taxes and better salaries, in addition to its more systematic and well-organised working environment.

He added that his employer had provided him with professional development, including customer relationship management training, something that was rarely offered at traditional taekwondo schools in South Korea.

The vibrant local Korean community and widespread appreciation of K-pop culture among locals had also helped him adapt to life in Hong Kong, Lee said.

“I like Hong Kong, and I hope to stay here in the long term,” he said.

Lee said that after completing his university degree through distance learning, he encouraged classmate Jerry Kim Seong-hun, 26, to come and teach at the same school last year.

Kim said he enjoyed working in Hong Kong as the work culture back home was more “conservative” and stressed seniority.

Chang Hyo-suk, general manager of the Korean Chamber of Commerce in Hong Kong, said he had noticed many more Koreans coming to work in the city in recent years due to the geographical proximity, low taxes and minimal regulations.

He said that many Koreans worked in the city’s trading industry, particularly in small and medium-sized enterprises or start-ups.

Roy Ying Fai, co-chair of the Hong Kong Institution of Human Resource Management’s advocacy and policy research committee, attributed the growth to the “Dedicated Team for Attracting Businesses and Talent” government initiative.

The policy, which launched in 2022, seeks to attract talent through the city’s overseas Economic and Trade Offices, including its Tokyo branch.

He added that Hong Kong was still attractive to financial talent, pointing to the boom in initial public offerings and the growing presence of family offices.

“Hong Kong is attractive to them. First, it is easy to find a job. Second, the tax is low. Third, if they want to apply for permanent residence, it is relatively easier than in Singapore,” he said.

But the Japanese consulate in Hong Kong said it had not observed an increasing trend in the number of Japanese nationals in the city following the Covid-19 pandemic, citing its own figures.

Meanwhile, the number of approved visas for British and US nationals, the two top demographics before the pandemic, recorded significant drops.

Last year, 2,472 British and 2,121 Americans were granted GEP visas to work in Hong Kong, a fall of 53.2 per cent and 47.9 per cent, respectively, from 2019.

Australians accounted for 883 applicants last year, a drop of 48.7 per cent from 2019.

Chow, the human resources consultant, said the downward trend reflected the geopolitical tensions between China and the West.

“Under this relatively hostile sentiment, the whole situation changed. Governments’ stances changed. Media coverage [on China] changed … How would I move to [China] for development?” she said.

But the British Chamber of Commerce in Hong Kong said that the GEP figures alone failed to provide a complete picture, adding that a significant proportion of senior executives and graduates from top UK universities now entered the city under another policy, the Top Talent Pass Scheme.

The business chamber noted that the number of UK GEP approvals had increased steadily each year since 2023 and the number of UK-parented companies in Hong Kong had also grown by 20 per cent between 2023 and 2025, while the country remained the city’s largest corporate presence among European nations.

“British businesses are not just returning; they are adapting and expanding their presence to access the Greater Bay Area and wider Asia-Pacific opportunities from Hong Kong,” it said.

Government figures also showed rents for flats, as well as the number of international school applications, had increased as expatriates returned.

The private residential rental index, maintained by the Rating and Valuation Department, surged by 18.5 per cent as of June to 205.8 from the Covid-19 pandemic trough in January 2023.

Real estate services company JLL said that a primary driver of the increase was the “influx of expatriate professionals attracted by resurgent IPO activity and asset management business growth”.

The firm’s latest report, released earlier this month, showed that the overall grade A office vacancy rate fell to 12.8 per cent at the end of July, its lowest point in 31 months.

Education Bureau statistics also showed the number of places offered at international schools in Hong Kong rose by 4 per cent from 2023-24 to 2025-26, standing at 48,413.

Among those arriving in the city was Korean national Philip Jung, who was sent by his food industry company to lead the Hong Kong office in May.

The 36-year-old said he expected to spend three to five years working in the city, adding that his wife had come with him on a dependent visa.

Jung said the couple were expecting a baby next year and were considering having the child study in Hong Kong to learn English, Chinese and Korean.

“It is not easy to get in our home country,” he said.

Jung also said that while Hong Kong’s catering industry was struggling, he hoped it would gradually improve.

He added that his company expected him to help open up southern China and expand existing markets in Malaysia, Indonesia and Vietnam.

“Hong Kong is the platform for Asian markets, so there could be opportunities to make more business,” Jung said.
