From clay to cuisine: the ceramicists reshaping fine dining in Hong Kong

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2146 words | 2026-11-10

“I was thinking about something crazy like a pumpkin orange. Mustard … kind of like a terracotta orange. You’ve been to Morocco, Marrakech?”

Ricardo Chaneton, chef-owner of Michelin-star restaurant Mono, in Central, is bent over a table laden with samples of glazed ceramic tiles. Across from him is Queenie Chan Kwan-wai, a ceramicist and the founder of Ephelis, who has collaborated with him for the past five years.

“What about this kind of orange?” Chan suggests.

“No … it’s too brown,” says Chaneton. “You see the orange there. Something like that, but not that ‘screaming’.”

Chaneton and Chan go back and forth like this for a while, in Mono’s private dining room, between lunch and dinner service. In anticipation of his progressive Latin American restaurant turning seven next month, the chef has begun preparing for the anniversary with a “refresh” of the interiors, completed in early September, to make the space feel “less industrial and more like home”. He has commissioned Chan to make new tableware to match the updated look – hence the debating of colours.

It’s a scene that would have been unrecognisable to Auguste Escoffier, the French chef who served in the Franco-Prussian War (1870-71) and returned with a vision of the kitchen as a brigade. His hierarchical system, based on military structure, made haute cuisine possible at scale and standardised the fine-dining experience, allowing restaurants to expand their offerings and serve more diners with greater consistency.

But back in the 19th century, the porcelain diners ate from was not designed for beauty – it simply emerged from industrial convenience. In 1813, British potter Charles James Mason patented ironstone – a white, chip-resistant earthenware that could survive rough, repeated hotel use. It was a standardised, mass-manufactured product, cheaper to make than porcelain and easy to replace in bulk. By the 1920s, the white plate was being praised for another reason: it didn’t distract from the food, the front-and-centre priority.

Identical plates as the default changed in 2006, when René Redzepi, of Noma, commissioned custom, hand-thrown plates from KH Würtz, a father-and-son studio in rural Denmark. Described as “rough-hewn crockery in speckled earth tones” with “cracked glazes”, early buyers even thought the plates “looked like a disease”.

But when Noma was named the Best Restaurant in the World, in 2010, attitudes quickly shifted. Suddenly, the plate wasn’t supposed to be just a blank surface. Its rustic aesthetic is now a part of the restaurant’s story and a sign of locality, craftsmanship and luxury.

It is in this context that Chaneton began looking for pieces that would form part of his restaurant’s personality. On a trip to Spain for the Best Chef Awards in 2019, he noticed many of his peers buying knives, chinaware and pottery. He followed them to an atelier in Barcelona, where Chicago-born ceramicist Cara Janelle was based. He fell in love with a large oval plate she had made and asked her to make it in green for Mono, instead of brown. He still uses them today, as show plates on the restaurant counter.

But shipping tableware such a long distance was never going to be sustainable. So, in 2021, he turned to someone local – Chan.

Chan came to ceramics through fashion. She trained at Central Saint Martins in London, Britain, and worked as a fashion designer at Dior’s maison de couture in Paris, France, where she learned to think in silhouettes – how a form reads from a distance, how it feels up close, how much weight it can carry without collapsing. She returned to Hong Kong in 2017, and launched her studio, Ephelis, the following year, going full-time with it in 2020.

Chan’s first restaurant client was Mono. She was still new to producing ceramics at that scale, still figuring out how a piece that looks right on a shelf behaves under the weight of hot food. But she found her way in through the room and the environment. “When I am given complete freedom on a piece,” she says, “I create something inspired by the interior of the restaurant itself, like the stone flooring at Mono, for example.”

It’s an instinct that informs how she works with Chaneton. He tells her the purpose – a plate for the amuse-bouche, a bowl for birthday cake, a show plate to welcome guests – and she decides the rest. Size first, then the clay colour and the glaze, which is where most of the work happens. She experiments, shows Chaneton prototypes and he chooses.

“Working with clay is a bit like the process of fermentation,” she says. “We still do a lot of tests, but each stage simply takes a great deal of time. Sometimes, you have to put a piece aside and come back to it much later.”

Watching the activity of Chaneton’s kitchen – fast, high-pressure, non-stop action, with constant tasting and adjusting – makes her appreciate how different their rhythms are. His is measured in seconds; hers in weeks.

“Morocco orange” was one of those long conversations. Chan understood the direction, but not the exact tone. Over three weeks, she made more than 40 miniature samples, each a small clay base with a different colour applied. She tested them twice: first on white clay, to see the precise colour reaction, and then on terracotta, the clay Chaneton prefers. Each sample blended different pigments – “Mono blue” and Morocco orange, for example – until they landed on the one he envisioned.

For all the dissimilarities in their pace, Chan sees her craft and Chaneton’s as the same kind of work. “Whether it’s clay or food, you’re taking something from a formless, raw state and transforming it into a finished piece entirely through your own hands and creative energy,” she says.

Some of her plates are already in service at Mono, including those holding the amuse-bouche: an arepa ground-maize flatbread with Kaluga caviar and a Scottish razor-clam tart. “When I see them being used at Mono – holding a dish Ricardo has created, being lifted by a diner – I feel a quiet sense of completion,” says Chan, “because the plate becomes part of his creation and his food gives the piece a new life.”

Her Morocco-orange set is still a work in progress, but the relationship between restaurant and tableware goes even deeper than merely collaboration between chef and ceramicist.

Chan collected leftover oyster shells from Mono, incorporating what would have been tossed as food waste into her creation process, forming what she calls “a special link” between them. Oyster shells are calcified, burnt into powder and used in the glaze for the plates, “so I always feel a particular connection between my work and the restaurant”, she says.

Chaneton is already thinking about the possibilities: “Once I receive them, I’ll be inspired to create something new. I’m looking forward to seeing [the plate] for a special dessert I’ve been working on, probably something monochromatic and cold. I’m planning to use chocolate made from scratch.”

Not too far from Mono, chef-owner Vicky Lau Wan-ki, of Tate – formerly Tate Dining Room, before its recent relocation – has been having similar conversations with local ceramicists.

Lau started in the art world, working in ceramics, before she became a chef, in 2011. When she began looking for vessels for the third and latest iteration of Tate, which opened this August, on Lok Ku Road, in Sheung Wan, she was already familiar with both sides of the exchange.

“You can feel the hand of the artist, the raw earth and that magic moment when it transforms in the kiln,” she says. For the table, Lau commissioned more than 20 ceramicists, including Jess Kong and Hin Wan of Mui Wo-based Lantau Shanto Studio, who make ceramics from wild clay dug out of the island’s soil.

Kong recalls taking samples to the restaurant’s previous location, on Hollywood Road. Lau had held the plate and the ceramic leaf, and smiled. She told Kong she wanted guests to run their fingers across the tableware, to feel the texture of Hong Kong’s soil.

Her ceramic leaf is a small piece, its glaze made from wood ash collected at the HKYWCA Sydney Leong Holiday Lodge – on Lantau, near San Shek Wan village – where the camp had built a wild-clay pizza oven using earth from the site. Fired at 400 degrees Celsius, the wood leaves an ash that reacts with the wild clay in the kiln to produce a finish that resembles a dried autumn leaf, in rich orange and brown tones.

For Kong and Wan, sourcing local materials is central to their practice: “Commercial clay is chemically balanced, predictable and convenient. Wild clay requires time, patience, flexibility and physical work. We can’t just open a bag.”

She tells the story of an organic farmer who goes by the name Keung Gor, whose Grassland Farm sits five minutes from their Mui Wo studio. He let them dig two metres (6.6 feet) down into his fields, and they found what Kong calls “the ideal wild clay”, the material they had been searching for. Ever since, she says, they “have felt a deep connection between the Hong Kong clay we use for our ceramics and the local vegetables and crops that grow out of the very same soil”.

But the very soil that gives the clay its character also makes it erratic. Kong had warned Lau she couldn’t guarantee that every plate would have identical dimensions or finishes. Working with wild clay has been a lesson in unpredictability, she says. “It taught me to let go of rigid perfectionism and to stop seeing cracks in early pieces as failures.”

For Tate, Lantau Shanto Studio made a set of two plates – one slightly larger than the other, set at two heights, with the smaller of the pair bearing a ceramic magnolia-leaf adornment – to hold the amuse-bouche that opens the meal. On the larger plate sits a crispy cone of razor clam and zucchini with mussel and black bean; on the smaller, a jellyfish tartlet with steamed daikon and Yunnan ham. Each vessel is hand-thrown in raw clay with a touch of uneven white glaze – unique, impossible to replicate.

A lack of uniformity is precisely what Lau wanted. “No two hand-thrown pieces are identical,” she says. “Every mark on the clay tells a story, which gives the dining experience a soul you can’t get from a factory line.”

She brings that same philosophy to her cuisine. Ode to Pigeon, which she has served for years, used to arrive in a single bowl. Working with the new vessels, she separated the dish’s components – the protein on its own, the soup and rice together in a small bowl. Food and ceramics, working together, have transformed how guests interact with the meal.

Kong sees the same idea from the other side. “A piece should never compete with or overwhelm the dish,” she says. “Wild clay’s subtle, raw tones serve as a natural canvas, symbolising the soil, with the food placed upon it representing a fresh harvest directly from the same soil.”

Clay, for Lau, is the physical anchor of the whole project. In her restaurant’s Chinese name, Tai Di, the first character is found in both the terms for “artistic form” and “natural ecosystems” – and clay is where those two concepts meet. Clay starts in the soil, is shaped by hand, fired by flame and ends up holding the bounties of land and sea. “It is the tangible bridge connecting the earth to the guest’s hands and palate,” she says.

Tate 3.0 revolves around this instinct. Lau brought on China-born, New York-based designer Chris Shao, and they agreed that fine china and ceramics were the ultimate expression of what she calls “feminine strength, elegance and tactile precision”. He built the interiors to match: floor motifs that resemble a puzzle of fractured shards, ceramic lighting and curved lines that echo hand-thrown pottery.

If the room sets the tone, then the plates are where design becomes palpable. Lau knows exactly what she wants a diner to feel when they pick one up. “I want them to notice the weight, the subtle irregularities of the surface and the tactile imprint of the artisan’s fingers,” she says. “In an era dominated by AI, mass production and cookie-cutter dining rooms, taking a moment to feel a vessel made from local earth grounds you in the present.”

Kong carries the same hope in the vessels she and Wan make. “As [diners] enjoy every bite of each dish,” she says, “I hope they feel a connection to Hong Kong and realise that even amid the hustle of this city, nature and stillness still flourish right beneath our feet.”
