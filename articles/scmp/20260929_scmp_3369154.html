China warns Japan over WWII history as business delegation visits Beijing

SCMP | FREE | 1071 words | 2026-09-29

China’s top diplomat Wang Yi met Japan’s former foreign minister Takeshi Iwaya, who is leading a 50-member delegation of corporate executives, in Beijing on Monday, marking the highest-ranking Chinese official reception for a Japanese visit in nearly a year.

Analysts cautioned that Wang’s high-level reception should not be seen as an opening to a diplomatic thaw between Beijing and Tokyo. Instead, they viewed the encounter as a calculated exercise by Beijing in selective engagement.

During the talks, Wang warned that bilateral relations were facing a “severe situation” and had “yet to be normalised” ahead of next year’s 55th anniversary of diplomatic ties, according to a readout from the Chinese foreign ministry.

“A country that challenges its post-war ‘peace constitution’ or attempts to rewrite the history of wartime aggression … cannot face the world, let alone open up a future,” Wang said.

Wang urged Tokyo to “correct the erroneous words and deeds” of its leadership regarding Taiwan as soon as possible.

Japan must learn from historical lessons on the 80th anniversary of the Tokyo Trials, established by the Allies to try Japanese war criminals after World War II, he added.

Wang is a member of the ruling Communist Party’s elite 24-man Politburo and director of the Central Foreign Affairs Commission office. Iwaya previously travelled to Beijing in December 2024, during his tenure as foreign minister in Shigeru Ishiba’s government.

The Japanese mission’s arrival in Beijing closely followed last week’s summit in Washington between Chinese President Xi Jinping and his US counterpart Donald Trump, when the two leaders hailed their countries’ wartime alliance against Japan during WWII.

Tokyo has been watching the signs of a stabilising momentum between Beijing and Washington with unease, prompting a 20-minute telephone call between Trump and Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi on Saturday to debrief her on the talks.

Iwaya’s visit, as head of the Japan Association for the Promotion of International Trade (JAPIT), is part of an effort to help break a prolonged bilateral stalemate ignited by Takaichi’s remarks last year about Taiwan.

In November, Takaichi told the Japanese parliament that a Taiwan contingency could constitute a “survival-threatening situation” that could permit Japan to respond as part of a collective self-defence.

Beijing sees Taiwan as part of China to be reunited by force if necessary. Most countries, including the US and Japan, do not recognise Taiwan as an independent state, but Washington is opposed to any attempt to take the self-governed island by force and is committed to supplying it with weapons.

The political fallout from Takaichi’s remarks brought official ministerial exchanges between Beijing and Tokyo to an abrupt halt.

In a scathing rebuke, Chinese foreign ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun accused Tokyo of “abandoning” bilateral diplomatic agreements.

Takaichi’s Taiwan remarks “openly challenge China’s national sovereignty and renege on both the original spirit of normalising diplomatic ties and Japan’s political commitments to China”, he said.

Formal channels blocked

The depth of the chill was on display in July when Japanese Foreign Minister Toshimitsu Motegi reported a brief interaction with Wang on the sidelines of Asean-related meetings in Manila. Beijing swiftly issued an outright denial, insisting no such contact took place.

With formal channels blocked, Japanese commercial and parliamentary groups have stepped in to keep communications open.

Xu Weijun, an associate research professor at the Institute of Public Policy at South China University of Technology in Guangzhou, said Wang’s reception reflected an upgrade in diplomatic protocol, and that Beijing was beginning to selectively raise its level of engagement with Japan.

China was drawing deliberate distinctions between the Takaichi administration’s security posture and the pragmatic interests of corporates, friendship organisations, and moderate factions within the ruling party, he added.

“For Beijing, the primary function of this meeting is not a thaw, but an occasion to reiterate Beijing’s conditions for normalising China-Japan relations.”

Toru Horiuchi, a China-Japan relations expert at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, echoed this view, saying that Iwaya’s visit was a good opportunity for Wang to “reiterate the demand” that Takaichi retract her remarks on Taiwan “if she is serious about promoting better relations”.

The current JAPIT mission, originally scheduled for June, was delayed following the death of its long-time president, former foreign minister Yohei Kono, who met Chinese Premier Li Qiang in Beijing during a similar trade delegation last year.

During Monday’s talks, Iwaya acknowledged that current ties were “worrying”. He pledged that JAPIT would honour Kono’s legacy and work to steer relations back to the principles of the four foundational China-Japan political documents.

Zhang Yun, a professor of international relations at Nanjing University, said Wang’s remarks reflected a hope that Iwaya can carry forward Kono’s legacy, continue contributing to China-Japan ties, and encourage other friendly Japanese figures and groups to contribute to the advancement of bilateral ties.

He added that Tokyo’s current strategy – maintaining cordiality with the US while lacking communication with Beijing - risked leaving it diplomatically compromised.

“Out of fear of being bypassed, Takaichi rushes to meet the US beforehand to talk up the China threat, and afterwards hopes to be better informed about what was discussed. This model is unsustainable.”

Rather than worrying about being bypassed, it would be better for Tokyo to contemplate how to actually improve relations with Beijing, Zhang said.

The Wang-Iwaya meeting builds on a succession of Japanese business and legislative delegations to Beijing.

Chinese foreign vice-minister Hua Chunying received an interim four-member JAPIT mission in June, while Lu Kang, a deputy director of the ruling party’s international department, met a bipartisan group of young Japanese parliamentarians in late August.

In mid-September, another delegation of young lawmakers from the Japan-China Friendship Parliamentarians’ Union was received by assistant foreign minister Liu Bin.

Meanwhile, with Takaichi showing no signs of retracting her remarks, Horiuchi expressed doubts that the encounter would pave the way for a bilateral leaders’ meeting at the Apec summit in Shenzhen in November.

“Such a meeting without Japanese concession would be politically risky for Xi,” he observed.

In a recent nationwide survey by Japanese media outlet Asahi Shimbun, 57 per cent of respondents were of the view that Takaichi should make efforts to improve ties with China.

Among LDP supporters, 58 per cent said an improved relationship with China was necessary.
