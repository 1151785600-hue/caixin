Wall Street helped build modern China. But is it now being cast aside?

SCMP | FREE | 1019 words | 2026-08-28

For decades, Wall Street titans – from former Goldman Sachs chief Henry Paulson to Blackstone co-founder Stephen Schwarzman and Bridgewater founder Ray Dalio – were hailed as “old friends of China”, feted by senior officials and granted rare access to the country’s top leadership.

Today, while American financiers continue to visit China and maintain commercial links, those private meetings with the top leadership have largely diminished, even as Beijing gradually opens its capital markets.

The shift has raised a crucial question for global investors: can Wall Street maintain its bridge to Beijing amid a changing US-China relationship?

“The decline of such roles suggests they have stopped being useful,” said Christopher Marquis, Sinyi professor of Chinese management at Cambridge Judge Business School.

“China’s economy no longer needs foreign bankers’ capital and expertise the way it did 20-plus years ago.”

The “honeymoon period” for Wall Street and Beijing dates to China’s reform era in the late 1990s and 2000s, when Beijing relied heavily on foreign financial expertise to modernise its economy.

That era was epitomised by the 1995 establishment of China International Capital Corporation, the country’s first joint-venture investment bank, launched with Morgan Stanley to restructure state enterprises and build capital markets.

Goldman Sachs was another major beneficiary, paying US$2.58 billion for a stake in the Industrial and Commercial Bank of China in 2006 – then its largest-ever principal investment – and helping underwrite some of the country’s largest overseas listings. Others, including Morgan Stanley, built similarly close ties with Chinese banks and companies.

The partnership yielded massive returns. Alibaba Group Holding’s US$25 billion debut on the New York Stock Exchange in 2014 became the largest initial public offering in history at the time, paving the way for a wave of tech listings. Alibaba owns the South China Morning Post.

At the same time, Chinese capital was flowing into global markets. The China Investment Corporation, Beijing’s sovereign wealth fund, ploughed a combined US$8 billion into Blackstone and Morgan Stanley in 2007 alone, opening another channel of engagement between Wall Street and Beijing.

“The relationship was two-way, mutually beneficial and trusting during those years,” said Kevin Chen Kaifeng, chief economist at Horizon Financial in New York.

“Almost all of those driving factors are now gone,” he added, citing the slowdown in Chinese listings in US markets, Wall Street’s exit from major stakes in Chinese state-owned banks, and declining Chinese investment in the US.

Regulators on both sides have added friction.

Nasdaq’s tighter rules have piled pressure on Chinese issuers already facing strict US audit scrutiny, while Beijing requires mainland firms to clear rigorous national security and data privacy reviews before listing abroad.

Only two mainland Chinese companies completed US debuts in the first half of the year, raising a combined US$59.5 million – the weakest half-year showing in five years in terms of both deal count and proceeds, according to a June report by accounting firm EY.

Chinese authorities have also tightened controls on cross-border capital flows by cracking down on unauthorised offshore brokerages, expanding outbound investment rules to individual residents, and applying a 20 per cent tax on offshore family trusts and insurance structures.

“The two-way streets of deals dried up on both ends,” Chen said.

Wall Street’s leverage has waned in another way.

Marquis said its traditional role as an advocate for market access had also lost much of its currency. Banks once lobbied heavily for policies that kept the pipeline of China deals open, but with Washington’s bipartisan hawkish turn on China, that advocacy carried far less weight than it once did, he said.

I don’t foresee future ‘old friend of China’ roles for Wall Street bankers

Christopher Marquis, Cambridge Judge Business School

During the 2018 US-China trade war, top Wall Street executives – traditionally Beijing’s strongest allies in Washington – attempted to leverage their political access to broker a truce, only to find their lobbying efforts powerless in the face of US President Donald Trump’s tariff and export ban agenda.

“No amount of Goldman or Blackstone advocacy can soften export controls or investment restrictions,” Marquis said. “Wall Street can no longer deliver its half of the bargain.”

The retreat also reflected a shift in Beijing’s own financial priorities, analysts said.

Today, Beijing is pursuing national self-sufficiency to insulate its economy from geopolitical tensions, trade restrictions and the risk of decoupling from the United States and its allies, with technology innovation as a central priority.

China’s top leadership has repeatedly stressed that finance should “serve the real economy”, part of a stated ambition – formalised in this year’s five-year plan – to build China into a “financial powerhouse” on its own terms. The policy is explicitly distanced from the profit-driven model long associated with Wall Street.

Finance had been defined “as an arm of state power rather than a sector to be developed with foreign help”, Marquis said.

Yet the market has not shut. At least seven foreign firms, including Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan Chase, now wholly own mainland securities brokerages after Beijing removed foreign-ownership caps in 2020.

A high-profile business delegation – including Schwarzman, BlackRock’s Larry Fink, Goldman’s David Solomon, and Citi’s Jane Fraser – accompanied Trump on a state visit to Beijing in mid-May, where President Xi Jinping assured executives that China would expand access for foreign capital.

While some financiers continued to maintain strong links with China, analysts said those individual relationships were unlikely to restore Wall Street’s former role.

“I don’t foresee future ‘old friend of China’ roles for Wall Street bankers,” Marquis said. “There may still be a legitimate, narrower commercial role … but they should treat access for what it is: conditional and transactional.”

Chen struck a more hopeful note, arguing the bridge could be rebuilt if China reopens its financial markets further to foreign institutions and Chinese investment in the US recovers.

“The future depends on whether China can open up institutions, and revamp Chinese investments in the US,” he said. “If this happens, the future of the Wall Street bridge is bright.”
