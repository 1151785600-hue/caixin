Taiwanese leader welcomes talks between Washington and Beijing ‘to reduce risk of war’

SCMP | FREE | 1320 words | 2026-10-10

Taiwanese leader William Lai Ching-te has welcomed talks between Washington and Beijing aimed at reducing the risk of conflict.

Lai’s annual Double Tenth address, marking the anniversary of the foundation of the Republic of China, Taiwan’s official name, also called for democracies to work together to safeguard regional peace and for the Taiwanese people’s right to exist to be respected.

He also warned that mainland China was trying to put pressure on the island to change the status quo, but Beijing accused him of “stirring up confrontation across the Taiwan Strait”.

“Lai Ching-te’s speech disregarded facts ... misled the public and revealed sinister motives, constituting a performance true to form by a ‘pragmatic worker for Taiwan independence’,” said Zhang Han, a spokeswoman for the mainland’s Taiwan Affairs Office, using a label Lai had previously attached to himself.

Zhang also accused him of “stubbornly clinging” to a “separatist mindset” and playing up the alleged “mainland threat”.

She said Beijing would resolutely crack down on separatist forces, oppose interference by external forces and safeguard peace and stability across the Taiwan Strait.

She also said Beijing would promote “peaceful and integrated” cross-strait relations and “advance the great cause of national reunification”, but did not address his comments about the talks with Washington.

On Saturday, Lai said: “We welcome the US and China managing their competition through dialogue to reduce the risk of conflict and war.”

Chinese President Xi Jinping and his US counterpart Donald Trump met in Washington last month, where Xi urged Trump to handle the Taiwan question prudently and oppose independence.

Lai added that maintaining regional peace and stability required “rejecting distortions of World War II history”, respecting the Taiwanese people’s right to exist and joint efforts by democracies in the Indo-Pacific region to preserve the status quo.

During their meeting, Trump and Xi highlighted their countries’ alliance during World War II, prompting criticism from Taipei, which says it was the Republic of China government, rather than the People’s Republic of China – which was established in 1949 – that fought alongside the allies.

Beijing has described such arguments as “a betrayal of history”.

Saturday’s address was Lai’s third Double Tenth speech since taking office in May 2024. The annual address has traditionally served as a platform for the government in Taipei to outline its policies and is closely watched by Beijing and the international community for signals about cross-strait relations.

Speaking outside the Presidential Office Building in Taipei, Lai said: “China continues to exert pressure through military and grey-zone activities in an attempt to change the status quo across the Taiwan Strait.”

Faced with the “expansion of authoritarianism,” Lai said Taiwan’s resolve was not only about its own survival but also about “whether the international community still believes that force cannot override rules and that powerful countries cannot deprive people of the right to determine their own future”.

Lai also warned of the challenges posed by “red supply chains”, a term commonly used in Taiwan to refer to supply chains centred on mainland China.

“Peace cannot rely solely on wishes,” he said, adding that Taiwan would continue to strengthen its defences and use its strengths in sectors such as semiconductors to develop a more self-reliant defence system.

In last year’s address, Lai outlined plans for a missile shield dubbed the “T-Dome” to counter growing military pressure from Beijing and said Taiwan’s defence spending would pass 3 per cent of gross domestic product this year and reach 5 per cent by 2030.

On Saturday, Lai said the increase in defence spending was not intended to provoke, but to deter war. He also said Taiwan was willing to engage in dialogue with the mainland, but insisted that it must take place on an equal and dignified footing.

Beijing regards Taiwan as part of China and has not ruled out the use of force to achieve reunification.

It has stepped up its pressure on the island since Lai, whom it views as a “separatist”, took office and has since conducted several large-scale military exercises around the island, including one that came days after his first Double Tenth speech in October 2024.

Most countries, including the United States – Taiwan’s main arms supplier and international backer – do not recognise the island as an independent state. Washington, however, is bound by law to arm Taiwan for self-defence and opposes any attempt to change the status quo by force.

On Saturday, Lai avoided repeating some of his more contentious remarks from earlier addresses, including the assertion two years ago that Taiwan and the mainland “are not subordinate to each other”.

Zhu Songling, a professor at the Institute of Taiwan Studies at Beijing Union University, said Lai’s speech was largely in line with expectations, noting that he did not use other provocative phrases.

His welcome for the dialogue between Washington and Beijing reflected Lai’s “awareness of new developments in US-China relations and the situation across the Taiwan Strait,” Zhu said.

“The broader US-China relationship clearly has a significant impact on the Taiwan issue,” he added.

However, Zhu said Lai’s pro-independence position remained unchanged, and he was increasingly framing Taiwan’s security in international terms and linking it to democratic values.

“The speech placed greater emphasis on repackaging the separatist narrative around Taiwan independence in terms of democracy and international engagement,” he said.

Wang Kun-yi, head of the Taipei-based Taiwan International Strategic Study Society, said Lai had maintained a hard line with his comments accusing the mainland of pressurising Taiwan.

While the speech broke little new ground on cross-strait policy, it suggested that improving US-China relations could make Lai more cautious, Wang said.

He said Lai had showed “greater strategic flexibility” than in previous speeches and may indicate that “Taipei must take into account the impact of US-China interactions on the situation across the Taiwan Strait”.

“The Lai administration supports efforts by Washington and Beijing to reduce tensions, while stressing that Taiwan’s interests must not be sacrificed in negotiations between major powers,” he said.

Wang Jianmin, a cross-strait specialist from Minnan Normal University in Fujian, also said Lai’s most significant comments were those about the talks between Washington and Beijing.

“To some extent, he is endorsing the idea of the US and China jointly managing regional stability,” Wang said, adding that they should be understood in the context of Taiwan’s pro-US stance rather than a shift in his view of Beijing.

Wang added that Lai had not mentioned cross-strait exchanges, which have stalled under the Democratic Progressive Party’s rule.

“He is not a pragmatic Taiwan independence advocate, but a radical one,” Wang said.

Tang Shao-cheng, a research fellow at the Institute of International Relations at Taiwan’s National Chengchi University, said there was “no sting” in Lai’s comments, but they still fell far short of what Beijing wanted to see.

“The new international environment shaped by Xi Jinping’s visit to the United States is highly unfavourable to Taiwan independence forces, prompting Lai to make some adjustments to his rhetoric,” Tang said.

He said Lai appeared to be adapting to this environment, describing it as a “positive development, but it is far from enough”.

Tang added that Beijing would look at Lai’s actions, such as his policy on cross-strait tourism, rather than his words.

During Saturday’s speech, Lai also highlighted Taiwan’s economic performance, saying the island’s annual economic growth was expected to exceed 11 per cent, its highest rate in 39 years.

He also pledged to make Taiwan a “city upon a hill” – invoking a biblical metaphor widely used in the United States to invoke a model for others to follow.

Lai also outlined a range of policy priorities, including expanding social benefits, tackling drugs and strengthening Taiwan’s advanced technology industries.
