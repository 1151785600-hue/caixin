Analysis: China Seeks Private Capital to Help Fund Its Infrastructure Push

Caixin Global | FULLTEXT | 726 words | 2026-10-16 10:46

1. China has begun deploying 800 billion yuan ($119.2 billion) in fresh policy-backed financing for investment projects, as the government intensifies efforts to draw private capital into the space amid a persistent decline in investment. According to data from the National Bureau of Statistics, fixed-asset investment fell 7.2% year-on-year in the first eight months of the year, while private investment dropped 10.1%, although the decline narrowed to 6.4% when real estate development was excluded.

2. Against this backdrop, the government is seeking to bring private companies into a massive infrastructure push centered on "six networks," covering water, power, logistics, computing power, next-generation communications, and urban underground pipelines. The main challenge is to create a financing and regulatory framework in which companies participate because projects offer genuinely attractive and stable conditions for investment, operation, and returns. Beijing is trying to pivot from the country's past reliance on government debt. A person from the China Development Bank stated that all projects backed by the 800 billion yuan policy-backed financing instrument must now make repayments from their own cash flow, as government budget funds cannot be used for repayment to avoid adding to local governments' hidden debt.

3. Policy banks began disbursing the policy-backed financing last month. On Sept. 10, the National Development and Reform Commission's (NDRC) private economy bureau promoted 36 projects in transportation, logistics, energy, and water conservancy. These projects are expected to require 61.4 billion yuan in total investment, with plans to attract 15.6 billion yuan in private capital. This initiative forms part of a broader push to stabilize investment, with multiple meetings held since mid-August regarding the six networks, major national strategies, and major projects under the 15th Five-Year Plan. NDRC Chairman Zheng Shanjie said in March that preliminary estimates suggested investment in the six networks and other infrastructure would exceed 7 trillion yuan this year, while market institutions estimate it could reach 20 trillion to 25 trillion yuan in the five years through 2030. However, government funding alone will not be enough, as analysts estimate the 800 billion yuan (up from 500 billion last year) could leverage roughly 9.6 trillion to 10 trillion yuan in total project investment, but only if other capital sources participate.

4. The policy-backed instrument can provide equity investment or shareholder loans to projects, or act as bridge financing for projects using special-purpose local government bonds. The first batches deployed this year have largely involved private investment, but Beijing is trying to bring private capital into infrastructure projects that have traditionally involved a strong government role, rather than just guiding financing to private companies. This creates a fundamental question about how risks and returns are divided between the government and the market. Luo Zhiheng, chief economist at Yuekai Securities, said the core of improving mechanisms lies in clarifying government-market boundaries and establishing a reasonable return mechanism. The government can then guide private investors by lowering entry barriers and providing investment or loan-interest subsidies.

5. A senior investment researcher told Caixin that private investors in some sectors still face difficulty entering and operating projects — some getting "entry without permission to operate" — and have an even harder time exiting investments. These obstacles are particularly significant in utilities and other sectors where private investors cannot freely determine how they generate returns. An infrastructure consultant noted that two factors currently restrict private capital's enthusiasm: the return on investment and whether investment returns can be fully recovered on time. This makes the pricing mechanism for utilities a central issue. Some six-network projects, particularly underground pipelines and other municipal utilities, require large upfront investment while generating relatively low returns, so their attractiveness depends on the prices charged for services.

6. A researcher who follows government investment said that if pricing mechanisms cannot be improved, these projects may lack attractiveness to private capital. Public-service prices should be adjusted according to the principle of "sustainable for projects, affordable for residents, and bearable for public finance." Recent investment-stabilization meetings have repeatedly mentioned improving pricing and fee mechanisms, and an article in the Communist Party newspaper People's Daily also called for steady and gradual price reforms for utilities. Yang Fan, chief macro and policy analyst at Citic Securities, said low profit margins at utilities underscore the need for pricing adjustments.
