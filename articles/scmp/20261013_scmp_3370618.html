AI fuelling growth while putting Asia’s young workers at risk, IMF meeting hears

SCMP | FREE | 1158 words | 2026-10-13

An investment surge into artificial intelligence may be helping prop up a global economy battered by energy supply shocks, but the technology’s potential to cull jobs requires swift mitigation if Asia’s youth are to secure a place in the future workforce, delegates were warned at the IMF–World Bank annual meetings in Bangkok on Monday.

Soaring energy prices, persistent inflation and mounting debt framed the opening of the week-long summit, which was last hosted in the Thai capital in 1991.

Unusually, the US has not sent its Treasury Secretary to attend the gathering, despite triggering the energy crunch following its war against Iran, taken alongside Israel, in February.

Since then, oil prices have oscillated around US$100 a barrel thanks to the partial closure of the Strait of Hormuz, driving up inflation and forcing governments to drain reserves or borrow heavily to cushion consumers from high costs.

Yet the multi-trillion-dollar global plunge into AI and its supporting infrastructure – from data centres to printed circuit boards – had given a 3 per cent boost to a global economy bruised by successive crises, according to the International Monetary Fund (IMF).

“AI – love it, hate it or fear it – it is here, and we are already seeing the first wave of impact,” said IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva, citing the US, China and India as the main drivers of growth in the technology.

“That shift in [to a] higher gear is what is keeping the world economy growing at 3 per cent, despite the fact we’re being dragged down by the energy shock,” she told delegates, including finance ministers from across the world.

But the same technology serving as a sticking plaster for a wounded global economy risks undermining its workforce over the longer term unless policymakers act quickly to prepare their populations for the changes taking place.

“It is a slow-moving tsunami,” Georgieva said of the impact on employment. “We estimate 40 per cent of jobs will be augmented, downgraded or eliminated. In advanced economies, [that figure is] 60 per cent. [In] low income countries, [around] 23 per cent.”

“We cannot today imagine what exactly the world of work will be five years from now … but we can be disciplined, draw lessons and act on them.”

Highlighting the upside of a technology whose capabilities and speed have thrilled and horrified people in equal measure, Georgieva said Asia’s ageing societies could offset shrinking workforces and declining tax bases with AI-driven productivity gains.

Advanced Asian economies could see an annualised growth increase of up to 1 percentage point from AI adoption, she said, while slower-adopting countries could gain between 0.2 and 0.8 percentage points.

To realise those gains, governments were warned to take a long-term view – especially in reforming education systems to equip graduates with skills needed to survive and thrive in the coming years.

“This is a whole new era,” Singapore President Tharman Shanmugaratnam told the same panel on how smaller countries might also gain from scaled AI adoption.

“We should start with the recognition that growth is going to be critical to secure good jobs, but growth is not itself going to be sufficient. There is already a divergence between growth and job creation, and AI is going to widen that divergence if we are too passive.”

Global AI development remains heavily concentrated in the US and China, where leading foundation models are trained, hoovering up talent and capital as tech giants expand.

The resource-intensive infrastructure like data centres is frequently outsourced to developing nations, which often lack access to the higher-paid roles and training opportunities created by the technology.

To narrow that gap, Shanmugaratnam said Asian governments and private businesses must collaborate to ensure students acquire adaptable skill sets that can be continually upgraded.

Education systems would have to be retooled urgently to ensure young people entered the workforce with relevant skills, he added.

“The silver lining in job displacement is it’s going to happen task by task,” Shanmugaratnam said. “That gives us time to adapt, learn new skills and stay agile.”

Host country aspirations

The IMF was set up after World War II to stabilise the international monetary system, provide policy guidance and emergency loans to nations in crisis.

But critics say its bailouts have sometimes pushed borrowing countries deeper into hardship by imposing tough spending rules. Others condemn the organisation for maintaining Western economic dominance, noting that the US retains effective veto power over core decisions.

The IMF–World Bank annual meetings are held in Washington for two consecutive years before rotating to one of the institution’s roughly 190 member states every third year.

Thailand, which spent nearly US$35 million to host this year’s summit, is using the event as a showcase for its own economic ambitions.

The kingdom’s economy has logged growth of 1.5 to 2.5 per cent in recent years, struggling to overcome the lasting shock of the Covid-19 pandemic and trailing its regional peers such as Indonesia, Malaysia and Vietnam.

Host officials are framing the summit as an opportunity to position Thailand as a stable investment destination in Southeast Asia, highlighting its regional connectivity, regulatory streamlining and readiness to attract foreign direct investment – especially in data centres and core AI supply chains.

Thailand relied on billions of dollars in IMF emergency funding during the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis to pull it back from the brink. The lifeline came attached to structural reform requirements that eventually helped Thailand, alongside Indonesia and South Korea, stabilise and rebound.

Yet the country faces stark challenges, notably an education system that currently struggles to produce enough technical graduates to meet the demands of expanding AI firms.

Similar challenges face countries across the world as an estimated 1.2 billion young people turn 18 over the next 10 to 15 years. Across Asia, vast disparities in income, education, digital literacy and internet infrastructure persist.

These divides separate developed and developing nations, as well as cities and rural areas, leaving tens of millions of young people vulnerable to white-collar job displacement and linguistic isolation from English-dominated large language models.

“The most important thing is to remove the gap between youth and the system that shapes their future,” Sarah Rauzana, Chairwoman of the Asean Youth Organisation, told a separate panel. “We need to make sure youth are included in all of the policymaking process … from design to monitoring [and] implementation.”

Other panellists urged governments to keep pace with innovation by cutting bureaucracy and updating regulations to support young entrepreneurs launching start-ups.

“We see a lot of companies able to go regional [or] global from day one,” said Mei Chel Tan, global managing partner at the venture capital firm 500 Global. “Companies and entrepreneurs that grow with that global vision are the ones going on to create new jobs and new segments of the economy.”

But government leadership was critical to ensure the benefits of AI were widely shared, she added. “In Southeast Asia, we see incredible talent … but it has to be harnessed.”
