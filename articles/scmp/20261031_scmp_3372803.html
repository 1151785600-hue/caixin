New Everest film hopes to nudge US-China relations into a higher altitude

SCMP | FREE | 1182 words | 2026-10-31

The film Everest: The Other Side, a collaborative US-China project that opened on Friday in 515 US cinemas and that will premiere at 700 IMAX theatres in China on November 6, starts with a majestic shot of the world’s tallest mountain. Then the music builds and more challenging weather rolls in, foreshadowing what is at stake.

The film invokes a well-trod narrative for fans of the mountaineering genre: a mix of the grit and sacrifice driving world-class climbers, along with no small amount of tragedy among these extreme humans driven to take on epic risks.

What is different is that this ascent finally succeeded on its third attempt by master climber Jim Morrison, not on more conventional Nepal and Tibetan routes, where we are told that 13,000 climbers have reached the pinnacle.

Rather, it took place on China’s Hornbein Couloir route, which only five climbers have reportedly ascended, with a twist. Morrison was also the only one ever to ski back down, a feat that took over four hours on incredibly steep slopes with no margin of error.

Morrison is driven by grief and harrowing memories as we learn not only that his wife and two children crashed and died in a plane that she was piloting in 2015.

But after meeting fellow world-renowned ski mountaineer Hilaree Nelson four years later, he then watched her die in 2022 in an avalanche as they prepared for their shared Everest attempt.

“I hope that it inspires people to get through their Everest, their hardship, whether that’s a big goal in life or a sickness of a family member,” Morrison said at a premiere at the Asia Society in New York. “We’re all going to go through difficult times.”

Much of the film is focused on how to cope with the death of loved ones, the cost of embracing life on the edge and his desire to memorialise Nelson by successfully carrying out their shared dream.

Interwoven is also the cold calculus of life at the extreme as Morrison skis down to get experts – who ultimately took two days to recover Nelson’s body after bad weather set in – rather than try and dig her out. This is followed by his resolve a few months after her death to resume his quest, convinced she would have wanted him to forge ahead.

The documentary, backed by National Geographic and the Walt Disney Company, was directed by award-winning filmmakers and climbers Jimmy Chin and Elizabeth Chai Vasarhelyi, friends of Morrison and Nelson, who are both directors and characters in the narrative.

The film took six years to make, weathering personal setbacks, the pandemic, two failed attempts and very narrow weather windows before Morrison’s successful ascent.

“This movie is a miracle on top of a miracle on top of a miracle,” said Janet Yang, the film’s producer, in an interview in New York’s famed Chelsea Hotel, briefly the city’s tallest building that has hosted the likes of Bob Dylan, Mark Twain, Janis Joplin and Andy Warhol over the years.

Yang was the project’s principal bridge between the US and China, the problem solver and cultural whisperer, overseeing the endless coordination required to secure permits and approval during years when the two nations grew increasingly wary of each other.

But she had an ally, however unfortunate, in the Covid-19 outbreak.

“We had more time than we thought we would,” she said. “There were a lot of Zooms and a lot of different departments that had to get involved. It just is not that interesting to talk about. It’s also a lot about the cultural sensitivity.”

Yang has been here before, in a long career introducing Chinese film to the West and Hollywood productions to China.

In 1986, she brought Chen Kaige’s Yellow Earth to the US. The following year, she worked with director Steven Spielberg on Empire of the Sun, one of the first major productions filmed on the Mainland since 1949, before partnering with Oliver Stone on The Joy Luck Club in 1993, with its hallmark all-Asian cast.

Along the way, the native New Yorker became the first Asian-American president of the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences before assuming the chairmanship of the Committee of 100 earlier this year.

Yang sees film as a way to bridge the enormous gaps separating the US and China. The US side often has a highly simplistic, monolithic and inherently suspicious view of China, she said, although the recent personal ties between President Donald Trump and President Xi Jinping have helped.

“This kind of reductive, absurd mentality about China is what I’ve been fighting,” she said. “It’s much more multidimensional and complex and layered and human.”

China, meanwhile, could benefit by acting with greater confidence, she added, rather than reacting in ways that can be misunderstood overseas where there is little knowledge of its history of exploitation by Western powers.

Everest: The Other Side has incredible footage and a heartwarming narrative. Nature as a lowest common denominator can act as a cultural bridge far from politics or polemics.

But the decidedly safe topic contrasts with some of Yang’s past projects that grappled with history, rural poverty and crumbling dynasties.

“There’s again nothing controversial, nothing sensitive, not because we were going out of our way to avoid it, but just because, by the nature of the subject, it just transcends national boundaries, cultures,” said Yang. “It’s not political truth. It’s about people’s dreams, but also the lure of nature.”

Films involving foreign partners in China are either classified as official co-productions, which Beijing generally funds and distributes as Chinese films, or assisted productions like Everest that allow more leeway, though their content is still carefully vetted.

At one point, one of the Nepalese climbers gets hurt and fellow Sherpas balk at continuing, their internal differences and criticism played out on the screen.

Yang said the production team debated at length before ultimately deciding to leave this in, a rawness that depicts the power and economic imbalance in a climbing team.

“I’m very happy,” she said. “It’s very real.”

The technical challenges of shots, equipment and filming in brutal conditions were significant, and there were no second chances, said Chin, who, along with his wife Chai Vasarhelyi, shot the 2018 Academy Award-winning documentary Free Solo.

“Every extra person or extra camera up there creates a lot more exposure to risk,” he said.

“Every single one of those shots on the ski descent, those are all single takes because we weren’t going to make him go back up there to recreate it.”

At a time when US-China tensions are omnipresent, spanning trade, security, technology, Taiwan, misunderstanding and the ever-present risk of a crisis, Yang still believes film can help ease the distrust.

“This is my Everest. I want to climb it. I have to believe it’s possible. Am I deluding myself?” she said, before concluding that the needle has moved over the decades, however unquantifiable the progress often is by definition.
