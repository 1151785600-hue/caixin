Asean urged to take on Big Tech after Meta’s record payout

SCMP | FREE | 1118 words | 2026-08-31

Calls are growing for Asean to collectively crack down on Big Tech platforms that spread scams and harmful content, spurred on by Meta Platforms’ landmark US$18 billion US settlement last week.

Meta agreed on Wednesday to enact sweeping changes to Instagram and Facebook – including default time limits and content restrictions – as part of the settlement with several US states, which had alleged the platforms damaged children’s mental health.

Within hours of the deal being announced, advocacy group Scam Watch Pilipinas was urging the Philippines, as this year’s chair of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, to put platform accountability squarely on the bloc’s agenda.

“For too long, countries like the Philippines have been dealing with the world’s biggest technology companies individually,” said Jocel de Guzman, co-founder of the group, in a statement.

Because an individual nation state’s enforcement reach was limited, cooperation on removing scam ads, fake accounts and harmful content often came down to “the willingness of the platform”, he added, further calling for an Asean Big Tech accountability framework to be introduced.

Analysts who spoke to This Week in Asia said the US settlement strengthened the case for Southeast Asian governments to demand comparable safeguards, though they cautioned it was not a template the region could simply copy wholesale.

Still, it “most certainly helped the Philippines get agreement from Meta on the safety protocols that were requested” last week, said Asha Hemrajani, a technology policy expert and senior fellow at the S. Rajaratnam School of International Studies, referring to commitments extracted from Meta and Roblox executives in Singapore.

These included age verification, parental controls, faster takedowns and closer cooperation with law enforcement, according to Manila’s Information and Communications Technology Secretary Henry Aguda.

Those talks followed a school shooting in the southern port city of Zamboanga on August 18 that officials said was live streamed. It was the second fatal school shooting in under two months.

While Hemrajani said the effectiveness of Meta’s new safety protocols “remain to be seen”, she called the latest developments “a turning point” that were likely to spur other governments into demanding change.

Elina Noor, a non-resident scholar at the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, said the Meta settlement showed “there is more to be done in holding Big Tech accountable for a range of harms”, affecting children in particular.

But she said the picture was complicated by the fact that many of the same companies being pressed to pay billions in damages were also providing technical expertise and underwriting capacity building efforts to help the region fight scams.

Yesterday’s rules

Asean’s existing digital cooperation mechanisms were not built around platform accountability, focusing instead on phone calls and SMS.

The bloc’s current guidelines “expressly exclude scams conducted through social media and OTT services such as WhatsApp”, said Alejandro Reyes, chief strategic officer of non-profit organisation AI Safety Asia, referring to services delivered directly to users over the internet.

“Yet much of the problem has shifted onto precisely those platforms,” he told This Week in Asia.

As an example, Reyes pointed to Singapore, where social media accounted for roughly 30 per cent of scam cases last year, with messaging apps responsible for another 23 per cent.

Digital governance analyst Hafizh Nabiyyin said Asean leaned too heavily on scam prevention and non-binding guidance, such as public education campaigns or knowledge-sharing exercises, rather than holding platforms to account.

This was a function of the bloc’s non-interference principle, consensus-based decision-making approach and tendency to take “a middle-ground position”, he said.

Even if it expanded its guidelines to cover platform accountability more explicitly, “they would just be guidelines and not regulations or legislation”, Hemrajani said.

Asean was not the European Union and it could not feasibly introduce comparable legally-binding rules for Big Tech companies, she added.

‘Coalition of the willing’

If bloc-wide legislation is seemingly off the table, then what can Asean do?

Existing regional frameworks set baseline measures, backed by information sharing and joint investigations, for individual members to adopt.

Asean’s Guideline for Digital Platform Regulation, for instance, recommends that member states verify the identity of all online sellers on digital platforms.

When Taiwan adopted a similar measure, it saw scam advertisements drop sharply as a result, said David Lam, a visiting fellow at the ISEAS – Yusof Ishak Institute, digital economy and technology policy.

“As effective as this measure is, however, Asean leaves it to member countries to individually enforce it,” Lam said.

Enforcement, not ambition, is where the region keeps stumbling. Malaysia’s social media licensing requirements – later codified under its Online Safety Act – were largely ignored or rejected by social media companies when first introduced.

Meta had also reportedly ignored Philippine requests to take down deepfake videos of President Ferdinand Marcos Jnr used to promote an investment scam, Lam said.

Collective bargaining power against Big Tech platforms could be consolidated via an Asean-wide framework, Noor argued, enabling the bloc to take on companies “whose market valuation and even annual revenue rival Southeast Asian economies”.

But she said the obstacles to enforcement were numerous. Not only are these the same companies that underpin much of the region’s digital economic growth, member states also have differing exposure to online harm and uneven levels of digital maturity.

On top of that is the ever-present risk of US trade retaliation against governments seen as targeting American tech firms unfairly.

Yet at least a subset of Asean member states clearly wanted tougher Big Tech regulation, Lam said, driven by the twin pressures of scams and child safety.

He pointed to Project Nexus – a regional instant-payments system built by a founding group of countries around binding operation standards, with the door left open for others to join later – as a template for how a “coalition of the willing” might operate on platform accountability too.

Reyes said pushing platform accountability up the Asean agenda would fit “naturally with Manila’s stated priorities on digital transformation and protecting vulnerable groups”.

But as chair, the Philippines is merely a convenor.

“The chair cannot impose a regional regulatory regime,” he said. “There is no Asean equivalent of an EU legislature and regulator imposing and enforcing a single platform law across the region.”

A more realistic ambition, Reyes said, would be establishing a “common regional floor” of shared minimum standards on scam advertising, account verification, fraud reporting, evidence preservation, child safety and cooperation with law enforcement.

“If the Philippines wants to lead this at Asean, the question is not only what regional framework it can broker, but what enforceable obligations it is prepared to put in place at home,” he said.
