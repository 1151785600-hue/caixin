Young Malaysians brew goal for traditional tuak drink to become national success

SCMP | FREE | 1436 words | 2026-09-06

At Raffly Langi’s family longhouse in Sarawak, on Malaysian Borneo, the tuak rice wine did not come in a branded bottle or a tasting flight.

His aunt has brewed the drink for Gawai, the harvest festival celebrated by Dayak communities, an umbrella term for indigenous groups in Sarawak whose longhouses have long been centres of family, rituals and communal life.

She made it from steamed glutinous rice and a cake called ragi, prepared with rice flour, yeast and spices to kick-start fermentation. Once ready, the tuak was drawn from a jar and served without flavouring.

Years later, 33-year-old Raffly has carried that family recipe to the Klang Valley, the urban region around Kuala Lumpur, where he produces tuak under the brand Tuak Alus and develops modern flavours for drinkers who are unfamiliar with the traditional brew.

Tuak traditionally comes in sweeter indu, or female, and drier laki, or male, versions, but artisan brands now also offer flavours derived from pandan, pineapple and roselle.

The drink can still be bought in Sarawak for US$5 to US$7.50 in an unlabelled plastic or glass bottle, while at Klang Valley pop-ups, producers sell premium bottles and promote brand collaborations and tasting flights, with prices around US$25 or more each.

But as it moves from longhouses into mainstream markets, brewers are confronting a persistent question: how much can tuak be changed before the culture that gives it meaning fades?

Keeping tradition alive

For Raffly and Kuin John, the 39-year-old founder of Tuak Sun Borneo, their craft is inextricably linked to the women who have taught them the drink’s brewing process.

“The basic method of making tuak is something I will always keep because it was taught to me by my mother,” Kuin said.

She makes a traditional ragi from rice flour, ginger, jicama root and pepper, and lets the drink ferment for at least three to four months to give it a deeper character.

“Most importantly, I still respect the traditional taboos and practices passed down from the older generations, [because] even as I modernise the business, these are the parts I don’t want to lose,” Kuin told This Week in Asia.

In Raffly’s case, his aunt is the main inspiration for the drink.

“The way I learned it throughout all the years, it’s always from my auntie. She’s the one that was always making the tuak in the house,” Raffly said.

But preserving that foundation does not mean reproducing every part of her process. Raffly now uses a commercial rice steamer and dehydrator, after batches of ragi dried in the sun repeatedly failed. The machine, he said, was “the key to making the ragi consistent”.

For Raffly, authenticity does not require every ingredient to come from Sarawak.

Alus sources glutinous rice in West Malaysia, where supplies are more readily available, but continues buying Sarawak’s nipah palm sugar, or gula apong, for indu. Unpredictable gula apong stocks have spurred Alus to develop its pandan and sour-plum versions, which require less palm sugar than indu.

“We try to get all the resources from Sarawak, but it’s not efficient for us to maintain production every month,” Raffly said.

His aunt has not insisted that authenticity depends on a particular rice or sugar. “It’s the feeling and the taste of the tuak that make it different,” he said.

Raffly’s business partner, Ayden Chong, 31, was emphatic that their business should not be rigid in adhering to traditional tuak-making.

“There’s a sacrifice and there is adjustment, that we need to make hard decisions … but it is what it is,” he said.

Chong, who hails from Perak, said his first taste of tuak came during a visit to a Sarawak longhouse over a decade ago.

Mindful of tuak’s cultural significance, he said Alus had tried to hire Sarawakians to work in the Klang Valley and create a community around the drink, instead of just marketing it as alcohol.

“I always tell people we are sharing the culture,” he said. “I’m not trying to sell you things.”

With tuak’s rising popularity and commercial potential, some Sarawakian politicians have embraced the drink as a symbol of their state.

At the launch of Borneo Tuak Festival 2026 in the state capital of Kuching last July, Snowdan Lawan, Sarawak’s deputy tourism, creative industry and performing arts minister, described the drink as a “living heritage”, where a family recipe could become a source of income, brewers could build brands and young people could see a future in inherited knowledge.

At the same festival, Gerald Rentap Jabu, the state’s deputy youth, sports and entrepreneur development minister, said building tuak into a recognised Sarawakian product required an ecosystem with quality rice production, research into tuak production and opportunities for young entrepreneurs to develop new products without compromising authenticity.

Licensing gap

As more tuak entrepreneurs seek to expand their business, they say that current legislation poses a stumbling block.

Malaysia’s excise law distinguishes domestic tradition from commerce, defining “native liquor” as customarily made by natives in Sabah and Sarawak for their own consumption and not for commercial use.

Work like fermenting, bottling or adding ingredients to liquor intended for sale is treated as manufacturing, requiring a customs licence tied to specified premises. Retail sales are separately licensed.

For small producers such as Alus, this leaves a gap between home brewing and conventional commerce.

“Pop-ups are still the way because we can’t afford retail,” Chong said. Establishing an approved brewery would require equipment, specialist employees and – by his estimate – an eight-figure investment even before compliance audits.

Alus and other tuak brewers are seeking to partner with established Sarawak distilleries, according to Raffly.

The Royal Malaysian Customs Department has not responded to This Week in Asia’s questions about excise application and licensing requirements for small commercial tuak producers.

Consistency challenge

Micky Vincent, a fermentation researcher who lectures at Universiti Malaysia Sarawak, said the composition of yeast and other microorganisms in ragi would typically affect tuak’s alcohol content, aroma and acidity, making quality results difficult to reproduce.

“This batch might be good and you become the best brewer for the year. But next year … you buy the ragi from the same auntie, it could be a bad one, it could be masam [sour],” he said.

A bioethanol specialist by training, Micky and his students have collected ragi from across Sarawak and isolated about 20 yeast species, retaining five for their ability to produce alcohol and aroma, and suitability for food use.

His method directly applies the selected yeast strains to cooked rice instead of using a traditional ragi cake. His own label “Tuak Bioteknologi Borneo” includes novel flavours like coconut and vanilla.

Micky said commercial producers should document their fermentation and hygiene protocols and analyse alcohol, residual sugar and organic acids before bottling. But standardisation should not require having a single recipe, he added.

“It’s my protocol; it’s their mother’s recipe or tradition. But what we can fight for is the same product.”

SAGO Group, a food innovation platform that organises the Borneo Tuak Festival, convened brewers and other stakeholders in 2024 to draw up a tuak manifesto, proposing reforms covering licensing, food safety, research and market access.

In a joint written response on Tuesday, SAGO founders Arif Abdul Rahman, Ehon Chan and Evelyn Teo said they were unaware of any policy change arising directly from the manifesto.

Because the issues could involve multiple government and other agencies, SAGO had called for coordinated discussions among stakeholders and different licensing tiers proportionate to the scale of producers and risks.

“The most immediate issue remains the absence of a clear and proportionate pathway for small producers to move from home or community-based production into legitimate commercial activity,” they said.

Malaysia’s new generation of tuak producers are not content with just making it big at the national level and are aiming for the global market.

Chong said the Alus sour-plum variety had proved popular with Japanese and Korean customers at the brand’s events, as they were already familiar with plum wines as part of their drinking cultures.

“If you have the skills, the right people, the right place and more equipment, this is actually going global,” said Chong, adding that he and Raffly had not decided on their export plans.

Kuin said: “Exporting tuak is one of my hopes and ambitions, where I can introduce Sun Borneo’s tuak and send it overseas, so more people get to know about tuak as part of Sarawak’s heritage and culture.”
