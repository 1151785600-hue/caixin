Will China’s 4 peace principles for tackling African conflicts work?

SCMP | FREE | 1003 words | 2026-10-25

Beijing has a formula for tackling African conflicts and hot-button issues, and during a meeting this month with Togolese leader Faure Gnassingbe, Chinese President Xi Jinping outlined it in four principles linking peace to development.

The principles entail focusing on African-led security free from outside intervention; upholding sovereignty and UN Charter principles; rejecting force and sanctions and favouring ceasefires through dialogue; and focusing on both immediate security concerns and long-term development.

Asked on Monday whether the principles could be applied to northern Ethiopia and heightened tensions with neighbouring Eritrea, Chinese foreign ministry spokeswoman Mao Ning said they offered “practical guidance” for the Horn of Africa.

Days later, a joint statement following a meeting between Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi and his Djiboutian counterpart, Abdoulkader Houssein Omar, said China would continue to play a constructive role in mediating regional disputes through its existing Horn of Africa peace-and-development initiative, subject to the wishes of the countries concerned.

Beijing has financed major infrastructure projects in the region under the Belt and Road Initiative, its China-centred trade and investment scheme. These include the Addis Ababa–Djibouti Standard Gauge Railway, as well as oil, fishing and maritime interests across Sudan, South Sudan, Somalia and Djibouti.

Yet, Beijing remains reluctant to intervene directly, opting instead to support African-led diplomacy to preserve its long-standing doctrine of state non-interference.

As conflict in Ethiopia’s Tigray region threatens to destabilise the Horn of Africa, China seeks to strike a delicate diplomatic balance advocating for peaceful, African-led solutions that align with Xi’s conflict-resolution principles.

The idea is to safeguard China’s billions in regional infrastructure and economic investments while carefully maintaining its core foreign policy pledge of non-interference.

But experts said lasting peace would ultimately depend on regional actors addressing internal factionalism, respecting state sovereignty and fostering inclusive dialogue alongside broader international peace efforts.

Yun Sun, a senior fellow and director of the China programme at the Stimson Centre, a Washington-based think tank, said Beijing’s response reflected concern about what renewed fighting could mean for its interests in the Horn of Africa.

“I think it first and foremost points out China’s concern and aversion to instability,” Sun said. “War and conflicts fundamentally undermine China’s interests on the ground.”

China also faced a diplomatic constraint, she added, saying it seeks to influence how conflicts are resolved without abandoning its stated principle of non-interference in other countries’ affairs.

Rather than lead a peace process itself, Beijing tends to support approaches led by countries in the region and their regional organisations.

“The balance they strike is through siding [with] and supporting [a] regional approach and the involvement of regional organisations,” Sun said. “That confers legitimacy and credibility to the Chinese approach.”

Ethiopian federal forces and fighters from the Tigray People’s Liberation Front (TPLF) are battling again in northern Ethiopia, putting the 2022 Pretoria Peace Agreement under severe strain.

Ethiopian federal forces entered Mekelle, Tigray’s capital, in early October after advancing on the city and prompting TPLF fighters to withdraw.

Ethiopian Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed’s government announced a six-month caretaker administration in the Tigray region to restore services, return displaced people and prepare for elections.

The conflict has also escalated Ethiopia–Eritrea tensions, with Addis Ababa accusing Asmara of backing the TPLF and occupying its territory.

Eritrea denies the claim and accuses Ethiopia of threatening Red Sea access, critical for landlocked Ethiopia, which relies on Djibouti’s port.

According to Seifudein Adem, a visiting professor at Doshisha University in Kyoto, Ethiopia must address its internal divisions if it is to resist outside intervention.

“President Xi Jinping is correct to suggest that Ethiopia should put its house in order by resolving internal disagreements through dialogue because disorder is the ultimate justification for external intervention in Africa,” he said.

Adem argued that the obstacle was not an inability to resolve disagreements peacefully.

“Rather, these disagreements often take the form of militarised factionalism,” he said. “This, in turn, is because the Ethiopian state does not enjoy a monopoly over the means of violence in the society.”

“The problem is that some of these neighbouring countries seem to view their interference as a kind of African solution to African disorder, consistent with an ‘African-led’ solution,” Adem added.

Xi’s emphasis on sovereignty shifted the focus from Africa as a whole to the rights of individual states, he said.

According to Costantinos Berhutesfa Costantinos, a public policy expert at Addis Ababa University and former United Nations policy adviser, China has an interest in calming the fighting but must use its influence carefully.

“China is a friend and major trade partner and investor in the Horn,” Costantinos said, citing Beijing-backed roads, power-generation and transmission projects, and industrial estates in Ethiopia.

Those investments could not deliver their intended economic benefits without peace, he added.

Costantinos said China’s diplomatic initiative, together with its influence through the Forum on China-Africa Cooperation, raised expectations that Beijing would engage with countries in the region to help resolve their long-standing conflicts.

China appointed Xue Bing as its first special envoy for the Horn of Africa in 2022 and convened a conference on peace, governance and development in Addis Ababa that year.

By November last year, Hu Changchun succeeded Xue. Hu has continued to promote Beijing’s regional initiative.

Costantinos said governments in the Horn should put Xi’s principles into practice through diplomacy to reduce tensions, protect trade routes and secure local ceasefires.

“No conflict can be considered resolved if any group whose interests are affected has been left out of the negotiation or mediation,” he added, observing that the parties would not enter talks with equal influence.

As Costantinos saw it, China should work alongside existing peace efforts.

“China will only add to the praiseworthy efforts by the [African Union], UN, US and [European Union],” he said. “Its initiative will in no way gainsay the work of these venerable institutions and countries.”
