Can Xi and Trump use their leverage to actually make progress?

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1756 words | 2026-09-23

When the presidents of the United States and China meet in Washington, the world will be watching to see whether their two countries can make good on their agreement to work towards “constructive strategic stability”. In the fifth part of a series, Fan Chen and Orange Wang look at the negotiating chips and areas where progress might be made.

There will be some powerful bargaining chips on the table when Xi Jinping and Donald Trump meet in Washington this week.

Both sides have economic and geopolitical leverage, and according to one analyst the negotiations will be as much about what they have chosen not to do to avoid retaliation.

Others pointed to critical areas where the two powers are locked in fierce competition but could instead be working together – from artificial intelligence and military dialogue to trade and biosafety.

But analysts agreed that keeping bilateral ties on a stable track would be a priority at the summit, and that was likely to be helped by a “tacit understanding” between Beijing and Washington on sensitive issues such as Iran and Taiwan. North Korea is also expected to be on the agenda.

Since Trump visited China in May, both countries have seen that the leverage they hold actually “works”, according to Taiyi Sun, an associate professor of political science at Christopher Newport University (CNU) in Virginia.

He said rare earths and critical minerals remained a key bargaining chip for China, while America still had leverage from its advanced semiconductors and related technologies.

Sun noted that Washington still had options, including another tariff increase based on its Section 301 trade investigation and its delayed US$14 billion arms sale to Taiwan.

Likewise, he noted that Beijing had room to adjust its commitments to buying American oil, planes and agricultural products.

“What looks like goodwill today can become leverage tomorrow,” Sun said.

But he said that while both capitals had powerful tools at their disposal, “neither can use them without expecting significant retaliation”.

Expectations have been low for the presidential talks – the second face-to-face encounter between Xi and Trump this year.

Still, Sun said artificial intelligence “could produce one of the summit’s least expected outcomes”, suggesting the US and China might share the strongest interest in preventing “catastrophic misuse or loss of control” of the emerging technology.

AI governance has become a prominent part of the strategic rivalry between the world’s two largest economies.

The issue has been in the spotlight in recent days, with warnings that the technology could create an extinction-level event for humanity and heated debate over slowing down development of advanced models.

In marathon talks in New York on Sunday, US Trade Secretary Scott Bessent and Chinese Vice-Premier He Lifeng agreed to set up a dialogue on artificial intelligence with a “shared vision of common goals and common threats”. The US said a bilateral Board of Trade was also operationalised during the talks.

Strengthening communication on AI will be a key goal of the Xi-Trump talks, according to Zhao Minghao, deputy director of the Centre for American Studies at Fudan University.

He said the two sides were likely to discuss domestic regulatory approaches and notification mechanisms for shared threats such as terrorism and transnational criminal groups using AI for malicious purposes.

“They may also explore communication protocols for major AI risk events, including a US-China emergency hotline for critical AI incidents,” Zhao said, adding that these proposals were already under discussion through unofficial channels.

Earlier this month, the Trump administration accused Chinese firms of stealing and copying American models through “industrial-scale distillation campaigns”. In response, Beijing said Washington was seeking a “monopoly” on the technology and warned of countermeasures.

Huang Ping, assistant dean of the Chinese University of Hong Kong- Shenzhen’s School of Public Policy, said that given the timing, the US accusations would boost its leverage in the discussions on AI.

He said a US-China dialogue mechanism on AI was of “very high” urgency, noting that they had clear differences over the definition of security and the purpose of regulation.

But he remained “cautiously optimistic” about outcomes of the AI talks, saying he expected a statement on principles, a follow-up working mechanism and preliminary arrangements in some areas.

According to Huang, Beijing and Washington were likely to discuss the rules, pace and boundaries of their AI rivalry. But he noted that their technological advantages, market interests and industrial growth remained the biggest issues – highlighting the clash between China’s low-cost, open-source ecosystem and an American commercial model that relies on closed-source, high-priced services.

Ahead of the summit, China’s top diplomat Wang Yi on Thursday urged Washington to stick with its commitment to “constructive strategic stability” in the relationship during a phone call with US Secretary of State Marco Rubio.

Zhao from Fudan said reinforcing and acting on that commitment would be Beijing’s “primary goal” for Xi’s US trip – his first state visit to the country since 2015.

Both sides agreed to the concept in May, though the White House in its statement included US demands for “fairness and reciprocity”.

Political scientist Zhu Zhiqun, from Bucknell University in Pennsylvania, said both the US and China “genuinely desire a stable and constructive relationship”.

He also noted that Trump valued his personal relationship with Xi, seeing their rapport as key to stabilising bilateral ties.

“When facing serious internal and external challenges such as rising gas prices and a prolonged war in Iran before the November midterm election, the last thing Trump wants is a rocky relationship with China,” he said.

Ali Wyne, a senior research and advocacy adviser for US-China relations at the International Crisis Group, expected Trump to “express further support” for constructive strategic stability when he met Xi.

“With the Russia-Ukraine war grinding on and the Middle East morass expanding, Trump aims to preserve a tactical thaw between the two countries,” he said, adding that Xi was also likely to avoid a personal rupture with Trump.

According to Sun from CNU, China has gained leverage since the Iran war began in February by avoiding using its “enormous purchasing power in the global energy market” and not adding more pressure on international oil prices.

“I see that restraint partly as a favour to Trump – reciprocity for Trump’s restraint on issues that Beijing cares about,” he said, pointing to the US president putting the latest Taiwan arms sale on hold.

“There is a tacit understanding between Trump and Xi on this front,” Sun added. “Neither side needs to publicly announce a quid pro quo for such an understanding to function.”

Taiwan has long been “the first red line” for Beijing in managing its ties with Washington.

Beijing sees Taiwan as part of China to be reunited by force if necessary. Most countries, including the US, do not recognise Taiwan as an independent state, but Washington is opposed to any attempt to take the self-ruled island by force and is committed to supplying it with weapons.

The South China Morning Post reported on Saturday that the US was considering postponing major arms sales to the island until after November’s Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen, citing Beijing’s strong opposition to the deal and US defence industry constraints.

The SCMP also reported on Thursday that the two sides were discussing possible announcements to strengthen military ties during Xi’s visit to the US.

A potential trip by Trump to China to attend the Apec gathering could provide an opportunity for another face-to-face meeting with Xi, as well as a chance to meet Russian President Vladimir Putin and North Korean leader Kim Jong-un.

The SCMP reported earlier this month that China was willing to broker a leaders’ meeting between Pyongyang and Washington and would support all credible efforts leading to resumed dialogue.

Zhao from Fudan said it was possible that Trump and Xi had already discussed this in May, and that there had been further talks between Beijing and Pyongyang during Xi’s subsequent visit to North Korea in June.

“As a result, I think Trump himself remains very interested in pushing for a new summit with North Korea’s top leader – and Shenzhen is a very likely location,” he said, adding that Pyongyang would be looking to improve its international standing with such a meeting.

But he noted that China’s influence on its neighbour was limited and that friction between Washington and Seoul – following the recent cancellation of joint military drills – could stall diplomatic progress with Pyongyang.

Wyne from the International Crisis Group noted that some of Trump’s advisers would likely be “wary of making China a diplomatic intermediary”.

“Trump himself, though, would likely have little – if any – objection given his affinity for strongmen, his frustration that South Korea did not assist US efforts to reopen the Strait of Hormuz, and his hope that another tete-a-tete with Kim could burnish his peacemaking credentials by helping de-escalate tensions across the Korean peninsula,” Wyne said.

According to Sun, North Korea is not a Chinese bargaining chip. “China can help open the door, but it cannot simply deliver Kim,” he said.

There have been efforts to build positivity ahead of Xi’s trip to Washington, with the two governments signalling progress in discussions on reciprocal tariff cuts covering some US$30 billion in trade on each side and on counternarcotics cooperation.

Zhao said extending the trade truce reached in Busan, South Korea in October was likely to be the “most realistic deliverable” from this week’s talks.

“This summit is vital for how China and the US coordinate their economic and trade relations and operationalise mechanisms such as the trade and investment councils,” he said.

Meanwhile, growing warnings over AI-enabled biosecurity threats have fuelled calls for the two powers to work together in the health sector.

Yanzhong Huang, a senior fellow for global health at the Council on Foreign Relations, said health issues were “unlikely” to be a summit focus, with fentanyl a possible exception.

“That does not mean there are no urgent issues between the two sides. Pharmaceutical supply-chain resilience, China’s rise in biotech, dual-use research, and AI’s application to health are increasingly treated as health-security challenges,” Huang said.

“US-China health cooperation is possible, but it will remain narrow and ad hoc,” he said, adding that given the lack of trust, formal cooperation in sensitive areas was unlikely and “joint R&D even more so”.
