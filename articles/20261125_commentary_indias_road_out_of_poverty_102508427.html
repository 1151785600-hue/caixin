India has achieved dramatic poverty reduction over the past decade. The World Bank's latest Poverty and Equity Brief shows that between 2011 and 2023, the share of Indians living below the international extreme poverty line of $3 a day fell from 27.1% to 2.6%. The share living below $4.2 a day, the poverty line for lower-middle-income countries, dropped from 57.7% to 16.1%, with approximately 496 million Indians rising above this threshold in just 12 years.

This trajectory echoes China's own experience as the only other country with over a billion people to confront poverty on such a massive scale. China lifted nearly 800 million people out of poverty over four decades and announced the elimination of absolute poverty in rural areas in 2021. India is pursuing its own path and pace toward the same destination.

Historical context underscores the scale of progress. At independence in 1947, nearly 90% of India's 340 million people lived in poverty, fewer than one in six could read, and life expectancy was about 31 years. Even in 2011, more than one in four Indians lived in extreme poverty, but by 2023 fewer than three in 100 did. Broader measures confirm the trend: India's multidimensional poverty rate fell from 53.8% in 2005-06 to 16.4% in 2019-21, and the World Bank's own multidimensional measure put the figure at 15.5% in 2022-23. Today, roughly 1% of India's 1.4 billion people lack electricity and 6% lack adequate drinking water.

Economic growth did much of the heavy lifting. India has been among the fastest-growing major economies, creating about 134 million new jobs between 2017 and 2023, nearly twice the increase in the working-age population. Rural women entering paid work for the first time also supported growth.

Public policy complemented growth. Bank account ownership rose from 35% of adults in 2011 to 78% by 2021, with the Jan Dhan program alone opening 590 million accounts—56% held by women and 78% in rural and semi-urban areas. These accounts, linked to digital identity and mobile phones, enabled direct transfers of pensions and support payments. Rural sanitation access rose from 39% in 2014 to universal coverage following the construction of 120 million household toilets. Nearly 40 million poor families moved into permanent homes, over 100 million households now use clean cooking gas, and women's workforce participation has nearly doubled in six years.

The challenge of implementing these programs across a federal democracy of diverse states was met with remarkable success, and the social and political structure proved no impediment to development. The author acknowledges the job is not complete and that lifting people above poverty lines is only part of the journey toward a developed India by 2047. The harder task ahead is ensuring reliable, better-paid work and rising incomes.

Future priorities include creating better jobs in manufacturing and modern services; equipping young people with relevant skills for technological changes; bringing more women into the workforce through safe transport, equitable pay and nearby opportunities; and securing better prices, affordable inputs and market access for farmers. Families also need protection against economic shocks through public health coverage and food security.

India and China, home to more than a third of humanity, have achieved development successes that have reduced global poverty and offer adaptable lessons for other Global South countries. Following the August 2025 meeting in Tianjin and September 2026 meeting in New Delhi, President Xi Jinping and Prime Minister Narendra Modi agreed that the two countries are development partners. Sharing poverty-reduction lessons can help put this understanding into practice and accelerate progress toward the U.N. goal of ending poverty worldwide by 2030.
