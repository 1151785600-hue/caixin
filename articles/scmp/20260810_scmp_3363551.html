Why Taiwan is conducting its first-ever mobile network throttling exercise

SCMP | FREE | 1024 words | 2026-08-10

Taiwan is conducting its first-ever mobile network throttling exercise this week to test how the public responds to wartime communications disruptions, a new civil defence measure that has sparked criticism over the government’s preparedness.

The exercise, held in central Taiwan on Monday and planned for northern Taiwan on Thursday, will simulate severe mobile network congestion by reducing 4G and 5G bandwidth for 30 minutes.

About 17 million people, or 73 per cent of the island’s population, are expected to be affected, according to the National Communications Commission (NCC).

Rather than a complete internet shutdown, authorities said voice calls, SMS messages and emergency cell broadcasts would continue to function.

Services relying on mobile data, including video streaming, video calls, mobile payments, online banking, e-ticketing, food delivery, QR code verification, cloud synchronisation and app authentication, may become temporarily unavailable.

The first phase of the exercise in central Taiwan on Monday prompted complaints from users who said the throttling had effectively left them without mobile internet access.

Some reported that 4G and 5G connections had slowed to the point where they could not send messages online or even load a browser homepage.

“What throttling? It’s basically a complete shutdown,” one user in Taichung said, while others described having “a signal but no internet speed”, saying the disruption felt more like an internet blackout than reduced speeds.

The drill is part of this year’s expanded Urban Resilience exercises, which replace the long-running Wan An air-raid drills and reflect Taiwan’s growing focus on preparing civilians for network outages caused by war, cyberattacks or natural disasters, according to the defence ministry.

The NCC said northern and central Taiwan were chosen because they had the island’s highest population density and telecommunications traffic, adding that the exercise would eventually be extended island-wide.

Before the tests, Premier Cho Jung-tai stressed that the exercise would be “network throttling, not an internet shutdown”.

He said it drew on the experience of democracies – including Japan, South Korea and the Nordic countries – to test the government’s ability to operate under degraded communications while encouraging people to prepare alternative means of staying connected.

“We hope this exercise will help the public build the ability to cope with communications disruptions through backup measures,” Cho said at a cabinet meeting late last month.

He described communications as a critical pillar of resilience, saying natural disasters, large-scale cyberattacks and other emergencies could all disrupt telecoms networks.

The exercise has nevertheless drawn criticism online and from opposition lawmakers.

Many internet users questioned the practicality of the government’s civil defence handbook, which directs readers to scan QR codes for additional information.

“If the internet is unavailable during wartime, what exactly are we supposed to scan?” one widely shared comment asked.

Others criticised the NCC’s advice to postpone important online activities or switch to fixed broadband during the drill, arguing such recommendations were unrealistic for commuters, street vendors and people working outdoors.

Ko Ju-chun, a legislator from Taiwan’s main opposition Kuomintang (KMT), said reducing mobile bandwidth to about 1 per cent of normal capacity amounted to a large-scale internet shutdown.

Ko disputed the government’s claim that the drill followed international practice, arguing that countries such as Japan and South Korea generally conducted smaller-scale or scenario-based exercises.

“Real communications resilience means backup systems immediately take over when the primary network fails,” he said. “The exercise should test whether backup systems work, not whether the government can cut off people’s internet.”

Fellow KMT legislator Weng Hsiao-ling said the government should compensate the public for any financial losses or damage caused by the exercise.

Ruling Democratic Progressive Party legislator Chen Kuan-ting defended the drill, arguing that a real attack could disrupt communications for an entire day rather than the 30 minutes being simulated.

However, he warned Beijing could exploit the exercise through a false-flag operation by blaming any simultaneous cyberattack or communications disruption on technical failures by Taiwan’s authorities.

Beijing has dismissed the exercise, saying that no matter how many drills the island’s authorities conduct, in the face of the People’s Liberation Army they were “little more than political theatre and an attempt to bolster morale”.

“They cannot alter the doomed fate of ‘Taiwan independence’ or stop the historical trend towards inevitable reunification of Taiwan with the motherland,” Zhang Han, a spokeswoman for the State Council Taiwan Affairs Office, told a press conference last month.

Beijing sees Taiwan as part of China and has not ruled out the use of force to achieve reunification. Most countries – including the United States, Taiwan’s main international backer – do not recognise the self-ruled island as an independent state. However, Washington is opposed to any attempt to take it by force.

Analysts also offered mixed assessments of the tests.

Su Tzu-yun, a senior analyst at the government-funded Institute for National Defence and Security Research, said the exercise reflected lessons from recent natural disasters as well as overseas resilience drills that simulate communications congestion.

“It gives people an opportunity during the 30-minute exercise to think about how they would stay in contact with family members if internet access became unavailable,” Su said.

He suggested future exercises could recruit volunteers for closed trials, minimising disruption while allowing authorities to collect more detailed technical data.

Taipei-based military analyst Lu De-yun, a former defence ministry press secretary, said the government had failed to adequately explain the exercise beforehand, unnecessarily alarming the public.

While agreeing that degraded communications were a realistic scenario during war or major disasters, Lu said the authorities “have focused on telling people to prepare their own backup communications without demonstrating what contingency measures the government itself has in place”.

He also questioned why Taiwan was not making greater use of leased low-Earth orbit (LEO) satellite communications services, such as Britain’s OneWeb, as a backup if terrestrial mobile networks are disrupted.

“The government often talks about developing Taiwan’s LEO satellite capabilities but none of that seems to benefit ordinary people. Instead, people are simply told to fend for themselves,” Lu said.
