Hong Kong-style cha chaan teng cafes thrive globally as Gen-Z embraces nostalgic dining

SCMP | FREE | 1200 words | 2026-08-17

When a cha chaan teng in Busan, South Korea, made it into this year’s Michelin Guide, it was a sign of how far the Hong Kong tea restaurants have come since the 1950s and 60s. Both at home and abroad, cha chaan teng are hotter than boiling-hot Ceylon tea poured through a milk-tea cotton “stocking”. In Hong Kong, long queues form outside Milk Cafe branches, while social-media buzz surrounds the ramshackle Lan Fong Yuen on Gage Street, Central. Overseas, these cafes have spread to cities such as Seattle and New York.

A popular working-class mealtime fixture since the mid-20th century, cha chaan teng have long been a Hong Kong mainstay. So why, in 2026, are they drawing fresh attention in culinary circles online and off?

A marriage of Cantonese and Western fare, cha chaan teng can ply a hungry guest with a macaroni soup starter, a satay beef noodle main and an egg tart for dessert, blending cuisines from far-flung places in one meal. And then there’s the speed at which the food is served. Hong Kong’s diners-cum-cultural landmarks are still as fast, furious and familiar as they ever were. Some, such as the Jordan institution Australia Dairy Company, are so brisk, in fact, that they have drawn criticism from tourists (notably, rarely from unfazed locals) for rushing customers into seats, through their order and back out the door.

And yet, despite the complaints, the number of tourists adding cha chaan teng to their itinerary grows. The draw, perhaps, is less the fare than the feeling of sentimentality and the yearning for simpler times.

It’s likely that the “Chinamaxxing” social media trend has had a hand in this, adding fuel to the proverbial fire and increasingly pushing Gen-Z audiences into the bosom of Chinese nostalgia. With their wooden booths, mosaic floors and colourful melamine tableware, cha chaan teng are catnip to influencers, and their authentic vintage interiors make for the perfect background in just about any frame. With the wide availability of ring lights, garish, washed-out lighting is no longer a problem. So popular are cha chaan teng among content creators that staff at Mido Cafe in Yau Ma Tei have had to restrict photography so that they can keep serving – rather than swerving selfies.

As a symbol of Hong Kong’s East-meets-West fusion, as well as of the resilience of local culture and egalitarian social spaces, the cha chaan teng embodies the softer side of a city often thought of as sharp-edged. Theirs is a social-media-friendly aesthetic – think idiosyncratic and worn at the edges – and businesses stand ready to capitalise.

Brands such as Adidas and Marathon Sports have launched cha chaan teng-themed clothing lines, keeping this strand of wistfulness on the right side of chic.

Last October, a photography exhibition at K11 Art Mall featured five artistic takes on the tea restaurant. Airside, too, has taken note: an exhibition curated by architectural historian Charles Lai Chun-wai and product designer Kay Chan Wan-ki – running until the end of July – examines the cha chaan teng from table to tile.

Its visual appeal, it turns out, travels. Spreading through the Chinese diaspora to the United States, Canada, Australia and Britain, modern interpretations of the Hong Kong-style cafe are attracting young customers worldwide. Fok Noodles in New York has become the go-to spot for Hong Kong cart noodles, and Cafe No 12 in Penang’s George Town often sees queues out the door for its egg tarts.

Following waves of migration, cha chaan teng have become cultural touchstones for Chinese communities everywhere. So far is their reach and influence that a cha chaan teng pop-up opened at Art Basel in Paris in 2024.

Home-grown cha chaan teng without an online presence – but keen to cash in on the whims of “food-fluencers” or film aficionados eager to recreate their own Wong Kar-wai scene – are tapping their young customer base for social-media savvy.

Tai On Coffee and Tea Shop was a Kowloon landmark for more than 50 years, before closing in 2019 when its owners emigrated. The current owner, Tiffany Yeung Chiu-yi, bought and reopened the cha chaan teng in 2022, harnessing social media to amass a new following.

“We have had a lot more tourists in the past year, mainly due to the internet. Instagram is now a key source of marketing for us,” says Yeung. “All kinds of dining options dropped off over the past two years in terms of demand in Hong Kong. But through Instagram, we found out what customers expected from us and were able to adjust our offerings to meet those expectations. It’s about differentiating from others, and Instagram helped us to do that.”

The desire for nostalgia increases in places going through rapid social change, says Dr Veronica Mak Sau-wa, associate professor of anthropology at Hong Kong Shue Yan University. It’s no secret that the city has gone through monumental shifts in recent years, so this craving for all things old school could be seen as a reaction and the younger generations’ way of connecting with the city’s history, while forging a new cultural identity in the process. From an anthropological standpoint, Mak says, “it’s a very human craving, one that helps us to feel connected to other people and establish a clear sense of self. For some, it can even be a form of escapism”.

For ArChan Chan Kit-ying, head chef of modern Cantonese restaurant Ho Lee Fook, nostalgic dining is a celebration of local culture: “With fewer heritage restaurants left, there is a growing need for spaces that preserve the essence of Hong Kong while continuing to create new memories.”

Mak goes further, suggesting that eating at a cha chaan teng is not just a means of honouring the past, but of expressing what we would like to see in our future. “Dining out is always a form of identity, a differentiator,” she says. “Whether that’s the place we go after work or class to build community, a type of food that speaks to our heritage, or just a place to see and be seen, it’s all doing that very same thing: helping us to define and individualise ourselves in society.

“The noisiness of a cha chaan teng establishes an informality that allows people to speak more freely, in ways they couldn’t at a fine-dining restaurant. If you go to these places a lot, they remember you and they remember your order. A sense of community comes from that.”

Snapping photos of our food before we tuck in, or shooting a video of a plate, embeds it with meaning of our own making. A young person posting content from a traditional cha chaan teng is, perhaps unknowingly, lending it a modern significance. As Hong Kong’s F&B market continues its unpredictable roller-coaster ride, that significance may be key to the institution’s survival.

Now well into their seventies, cha chaan teng show no sign of slowing, even establishing themselves as a major tourism draw. How’s that for off-menu?
