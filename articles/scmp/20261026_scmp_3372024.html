Will AI-powered open-door vending machines reshape Hong Kong’s retail landscape?

SCMP | FREE | 1304 words | 2026-10-26

Sherry Shum has embraced a new shopping routine after finding a smart vending machine near her home in Chai Wan in Hong Kong.

The 37-year-old retail worker uses her phone to scan a QR code, opens the smart machine’s door, picks up a bottle of Coca-Cola, an oolong tea and a pack of potato chips, closes it and walks away with HK$60 (US$7.70) worth of goods. The whole process is so seamless that the bulk of the time is spent deciding what to buy.

The moment Shum takes an item from the shelf, the machine detects the product based on its appearance and debits her account. Once she shuts the door, the bill is settled via the payment method she selected before opening it.

“I buy things basically every day … I can grab multiple items at once after just opening the door,” she said. “The open-door machines are more convenient because you do not have to wait for items to drop one by one from traditional machines that sometimes get stuck.”

Over the decades, vending machines have evolved from simple dispensers of bottled or canned drinks into smart retail points that combine digital payments and data-driven consumer insights. They now offer a low-cost retail channel for third parties such as local charities, selling products like flowers and daily necessities – from disposable underwear to sanitary towels.

These artificial intelligence-powered open-door vending machines have been growing in popularity in Shenzhen in the past couple of years, but they remain uncommon in Hong Kong.

Some operators have pledged to install more of them across the city in the coming months, with one reporting higher sales revenue during the trial stage.

IVM Technology is one of Hong Kong’s largest operators, managing about 800 AI vending machines alongside 1,500 traditional ones in Hong Kong, the firm’s founder and managing director Leo Lui told the South China Morning Post.

Hong Kong had an estimated 15,000 to 17,000 vending machines, with the number expected to increase as the lower capital costs of AI machines attract new market entrants, Lui said.

“It will not surprise me if there are 30,000 to 40,000 machines later,” he said.

Lui pointed to Hong Kong’s exorbitant logistics, labour and parking costs – such as paying HK$200 just to park a delivery van for restocking at major commercial sites – as a major bottleneck.

Unlike mainland Chinese operators who could rely on low delivery expenses to sell low-margin goods, Hong Kong vending businesses faced fixed transport costs of about HK$2 per item, forcing them to focus on higher-priced, premium inventory, Lui said.

While the new open-door models generated 20 to 30 per cent more revenue in closed environments such as offices, traditional machines still outperformed them by 30 per cent in transient public spaces like bus stops, Lui explained.

“On the streets, some consumers might find the new machines are too slow, whereas with an Octopus card on a traditional machine, the product drops in a second after a purchase is made,” he said.

While nearly 90 per cent of new vending machines deployed on the mainland were AI open-door models, Hong Kong’s adoption remained significantly slower due to distinct local economic factors, said Rex Leung, general manager of IVM Technology.

The offline nature of the Octopus card contactless payment system presented a significant hurdle for AI machines, which required post-purchase settlement after the door was closed, Leung said.

“The physical Octopus card is an offline payment method where users tap and leave immediately, which is incompatible with this machine that charges users only after they finish shopping,” Leung added.

To resolve this issue, Octopus Cards was expected to launch an integration allowing users to scan a quick response (QR) code and settle payments online via the Octopus application, Lui said.

Leung added the company aimed to evolve into a platform that allowed a wider range of organisations to sell goods in their machines.

FreshUp, another local vending machine operator with a network of more than 5,000 machines, had set up more than 500 smart coolers that allowed customers to open the door, take products and be charged automatically through visual recognition technology, said David Lam, the group’s founder and CEO.

The smart models were generating 10 to 20 per cent more revenue per transaction than traditional push-button machines because the open-door design encouraged consumers to buy multiple items at once, Lam explained.

“People might have pressed buttons for individual items in the past, but now, since they have already opened the fridge to buy a drink, they might take chocolate or potato chips if they find them interesting, increasing the overall basket value or unit items per transaction,” Lam said.

The AI models required an upfront hardware cost of HK$20,000 to HK$30,000, which was similar to the price of traditional machines, he added.

While the local smart retail sector was projected to grow steadily at an annual rate of 5 to 10 per cent, operators had to pay a recurring fixed back-end computing fee to software providers for data processing every time the cabinet door was opened, Lam said.

Beyond commercial retail, the elimination of mechanical coils and rigid size restrictions has allowed open-door cabinets to serve as low-cost “micro-retail platforms” for non-governmental organisations and local brands seeking physical exposure without traditional overheads.

The new technology provided a low-cost retail alternative for charities such as the Po Leung Kuk, which sold products made by people with disabilities, said Choi Kai-yin, a social worker and service manager at the organisation.

Listing items in physical retail stores incurred high rental and administrative costs, making it difficult for charities to generate sufficient revenue to support their disabled creators, Choi said.

“Renting a shop is [costly], as we are selling bottled drinks for about HK$10 each, and it is hard to calculate how many bottles we need to sell to cover the rent,” Choi added.

By selling bottled tea at HK$12 through vending machines, the Po Leung Kuk hoped to create a reasonable profit margin, which would directly benefit individuals with disabilities in recognition of their contributions and participation in vocational training, Choi said.

Swire Coca-Cola has been rolling out smart vending coolers on the mainland in recent years, as retail formats incorporating digital payments increase in popularity following the pandemic. Its Hong Kong bottling business has now begun introducing these coolers, which allow customers to open the door and select drinks, adapting technology and operating processes proven on the mainland.

The coolers are easy to operate: shoppers select a payment method before opening the door, choose their products and close the door. When the door is closed, the bill is settled.

“Consumers today expect to see it, want it and get it almost instantly, and Hong Kong is no exception. People want a cold drink close to where they live, work, study and play, and they want paying for it to be quick and simple,” said Hunter Jin, Greater China CEO of Swire Coca-Cola.

“Smart vending coolers meet that expectation by combining cashless payment with product-recognition technology, so the whole purchase feels seamless.”

Swire Coca-Cola has set up more than 150 coolers across sports and entertainment venues, workplaces, residential communities, educational institutions and industrial settings to reach locations unsuitable for conventional machines.

The new coolers would not completely replace the company’s network of about 6,000 conventional vending machines in Hong Kong, but would serve as a smart alternative offering a seamless digital experience, Swire Coca-Cola said.

“As Hong Kong continues to welcome major events and visitors, vending can do more than sell drinks,” Jin said. “We are exploring Hong Kong-themed vending and digital experiences at venues, transport hubs and tourist destinations that showcase local culture and add to the city’s vibrancy.”
