Politics aside, young Vietnamese are warming to China

SCMP | FREE | 1097 words | 2026-09-14

Vietnam is often held up as proof of China’s underwhelming soft power in Southeast Asia: a millennium of resistance against Chinese domination, a border war within living memory, unresolved maritime disputes and elite surveys that consistently show Vietnamese respondents’ deep distrust of Beijing.

Yet beneath these strategic tensions, a quieter shift is under way. Young Vietnamese are warming to Chinese culture, language, lifestyles and economic opportunity. From crowds queuing for Labubu toys to a scramble for Chinese-language jobs, and from Chinese content going viral on social media to record numbers of Vietnamese students choosing to study at Chinese universities, China is being woven even more tightly into the everyday lives of young Vietnamese.

Four converging forces underpin this shift: cultural attraction, new-media connectivity, facilitation by both governments and economic incentives.

China’s cultural appeal rests on a mix of cultural familiarity and contemporary trendiness. Chinese dramas have long been a fixture of Vietnamese television and Vietnam is now among Asia’s largest markets for them, their reach amplified by digital distribution. Content from major Chinese streaming platforms such as iQiyi, Tencent Video and Youku is carried on Vietnamese platforms such as VieOn and FPT Play, delivering China’s entertainment industry directly into Vietnamese homes and onto Vietnamese smartphones.

Meanwhile, some Vietnamese celebrities have found wider fame on Chinese reality shows, while hit Chinese formats have been adapted for Vietnamese audiences.

China’s appeal has also spilled into Vietnam’s shopping and lifestyle. Many Chinese brands have won over young Vietnamese with trendy designs and affordable prices. Mixue, the Chinese ice cream and tea chain, had expanded to more than 1,300 Vietnamese outlets by late 2024, making Vietnam its second-largest overseas market. Chagee, Cotti Coffee, KKV and Pop Mart have followed suit, turning shopping centres in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City into showcases of “Made in China” cool.

Algorithmic ambassadors

None of this cultural traction would travel as far, or as fast, without new media. TikTok counts more than 70 million monthly active users in Vietnam, out of 460 million across Southeast Asia, and sits within a broader Chinese digital ecosystem that channels content on Chinese culture and daily life to regional audiences. Vietnamese-language content on TikTok and Facebook – including from Vietnamese influencers living in or travelling through China – offers viewers a window onto Chinese cities, landscapes and the rhythms of life and work in the country.

This bottom-up, peer-driven pull is unfolding alongside a renewed official push for closer people-to-people ties. Since the 75th anniversary of diplomatic relations and the designation of 2025 as the “Year of Vietnam-China Humanistic Exchange”, officials on both sides have promoted youth engagement. The joint statement during President Xi Jinping’s state visit in April 2025 invited Vietnamese youth to join “Red Study Tours”. More than 1,000 have done so, visiting sites commemorating the two countries’ shared revolutionary history – among them the former base of the Vietnamese Revolutionary Youth League in Guangzhou – alongside hi-tech hubs that showcase China’s modern development and technological achievements.

Two-way travel has expanded too, supported by growing rail and air links. Since August, Vietnamese citizens have been able to transit China visa-free for up to 10 days, and the Vietnam–China Tourism Cooperation Years 2026–2027 initiative aims to further strengthen people-to-people ties. The numbers are impressive: Chinese visitor arrivals to Vietnam surged 41.3 per cent to 5.3 million in 2025, making China Vietnam’s largest source of tourists, while Vietnam had become China’s fourth-largest source market by mid-2026.

Education diplomacy tells a similar story: the number of Vietnamese students in China has more than doubled, from 11,300 in 2018 to about 25,000 today – still trailing the numbers in South Korea (43,300), Japan (36,300), Australia (33,000) and the United States (31,300), but expected to climb further as Beijing promises at least 1,000 government scholarships and 1,000 Chinese-language teachers to Vietnam.

Mandarin pays

Underpinning all this is economic pragmatism. Vietnamese attraction to China rests not only on what China represents, but also on what engaging with it offers. As Chinese investment has poured into Vietnam, Mandarin has become a sought-after skill for young Vietnamese. Vietnamese recruitment platform Joboko reported roughly 13,000 openings for Chinese-speaking professionals in 2025, up 50 per cent on the year before, with fluency commanding better pay in industrial estates and Chinese-invested companies. LinkedIn, TopCV and VietnamWorks each list thousands of Chinese-language roles in Vietnam, with monthly salaries ranging from US$500 to US$2,000 – attractive pay by Vietnamese standards.

Demand is such that Vietnam recorded the world’s highest number of registrations for the HSK Chinese-proficiency test in the first quarter of 2025. At some Vietnamese universities, Chinese-language programmes now post higher admission scores than English ones, reflecting surging youth interest. English remains the default foreign language taught from primary school and is moving towards compulsory status by 2030, while Chinese is increasingly picked up later for its practical economic value. But anecdotal evidence points to growing parental demand for early Chinese learning and Vietnam has said it will pilot Chinese, Japanese and Korean lessons for preschoolers to prepare for future workforce competitiveness.

Young Vietnamese people’s growing embrace of China coexists with deep-rooted patriotic sensitivities

All this unfolds as Hanoi emphasises the more productive dimensions of its relationship with Beijing, while keeping long-standing South China Sea tensions in the background. This favourable climate may have helped, but young Vietnamese people’s growing embrace of China coexists with deep-rooted patriotic sensitivities. The 2025 boycott of Chagee after its app and website displayed China’s disputed “nine-dash line” and the removal of some Chinese dramas displaying the same map show how quickly those sensitivities can resurface. Yet such episodes have merely interrupted rather than reversed the broader trend. Chagee is back in Vietnam, just as bilateral ties have recovered from the turbulence of the 2010s, when the maritime dispute overshadowed the relationship.

Whether this societal warming can cushion or withstand the next downturn in relations remains to be seen. Vietnam’s history suggests that affinity with China and resistance to its power have never been mutually exclusive. A younger generation is now living that old paradox on new terms: drawn more directly and intimately into China’s orbit of culture and opportunity, while retaining the instinct to push back when national interests collide.
