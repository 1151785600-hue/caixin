Reviving Kuala Lumpur: can Malaysia modernise without pricing out its past?

SCMP | FREE | 1226 words | 2026-08-30

A yellowing receipt, framed behind glass near the entrance of Abdul Razak Gold House, records an address that no longer officially exists: “63 Ampang Street, Kuala Lumpur (Malaya)”.

The shop’s modern glass frontage, polished counters and gleaming bullion cases give little away about its history. But step inside and that simple receipt serves as a small window onto pre-independence Malaysia.

For six decades, this family business has reinvented itself in step with the city around it. What began as a modest goldsmith’s counter beneath a row of old shophouses is now a brightly lit jewellery and bullion store.

Today, skyscrapers and overhead train tracks loom over the capital’s commercial core, and many of the businesses along Lebuh Ampang – the modern name for Ampang Street – that the shop’s proprietor remembers from his childhood have long since vanished.

Mohd Razalie Abdul Rasul, 37, is the newest custodian of Abdul Razak Gold House. He has expanded the business into bullion trading, bought the property next door and is, ultimately, preparing for a handover to the next generation.

“My dad is still a mentor, but the way he thinks and I think is totally different,” he said. “We have to think very modern. We have to stay one step ahead.”

Their family drama is a microcosm of the soul-searching currently sweeping Kuala Lumpur: how do you hold on to the past while still moving forward?

Muddy origins

Long before Malaysia existed as an independent nation, a small trading post emerged at the junction of the Klang and Gombak rivers. This geographic meeting point gave the future capital its name, which translates from Malay as “muddy confluence”.

Today, those humble riverbank origins stand in sharp contrast with the towering glass and steel of the Petronas Twin Towers and Merdeka 118. Yet cradled beneath this hyper-modern skyline lies Kuala Lumpur’s historic heart.

Here, amid old shophouses, traditional temples and bustling markets, the city’s earliest trading streets still endure, even as the tides of modern commerce have slowly drifted away from them.

Reversing that decline is the aim of Warisan KL, a heritage-led regeneration programme working to restore landmarks, upgrade public spaces and improve transport links across the old city.

Its ambition is to turn heritage into an economic asset without emptying it of the traders who make it feel authentic, or dressing it up as a backdrop for tourists rather than a place where people actually live and work.

Tourist footfall is the wrong measure of success, according to Syed Redza Algadrie Syed Hassan, CEO of Prasarana Integrated Development (Pride), a government entity tasked with revitalising Malaysia’s urban public transport network.

What mattered more was whether visitors lingered and spent money at local businesses, he said.

“The discipline is to let commerce follow authenticity rather than replace it,” Syed Redza told This Week in Asia.

“If you over-polish a heritage district, you risk removing the very qualities that made it distinctive and valuable in the first place.”

Izwan Ibrahim, CEO of PNB Merdeka Ventures, which oversees the Merdeka 118 precinct, takes a similarly hard line on what counts as success.

Growth that comes at the expense of the communities already living in a heritage enclave is not growth worth having, he argues – pointing to the restored Merdeka Stadium, which now hosts not only sports events but also concerts and community programmes, as a case in point.

“It is not enough to restore heritage buildings or bring more visitors into an area if the surrounding community does not also see improvements in its everyday environment,” he said.

Masjid Jamek, one of the city’s busiest transport interchanges, illustrates the approach: the station has been deliberately positioned as a gateway, funnelling commuters into the historic quarter around it rather than simply moving them through.

Pride reports that its pilot projects in transport hubs have generated more than 624,000 social media impressions, while the digital heritage trails on its MyRapid PULSE app drew over 22,600 engagements in the first three months since launch.

“The early signals are encouraging,” Syed Redza said.

Heritage for show?

Not everyone is convinced that physical improvements alone can revive a neighbourhood’s soul, however.

“You can’t contract out authenticity or buy it through municipal budgets,” said Azry Bruce Aziz, a Kuala Lumpur-based independent history researcher.

For him, what makes a place authentic has little to do with the buildings themselves and everything to do with their function. He points to the hardware stores and Chinese medicinal halls of Jalan Tun H.S. Lee, the gold and textile traders of Lebuh Ampang and the temples, grocers and eateries that still pull people into the city centre.

“When planned regeneration focuses purely on surface makeovers, you end up with heritage for show,” he said, warning that “treating historic streets like a pretty backdrop for weekend photos” would only result in higher rents that “kick out the very shops that made the area real in the first place”.

Azry is not opposed to the idea of regeneration. Better lighting, pavements, drainage and public space can make a district safer and more inviting, he says – provided the change does not come at the expense of the businesses that already operate there.

What concerns him more is the inevitable ripple effects of rising property values, which incentivise landlords to swap out low-margin tenants such as repair shops, tailors and stationery stores for glossier, higher-rent lifestyle brands.

“What planning should be doing instead is acting as a protective shield, instead of trying to invent fake charm,” he said. “Top-down planning only works when it protects the real community that’s already there.”

Adapting to survive

There is a compelling economic case for getting this right. Heritage-led regeneration is about more than just urban renewal or cultural preservation; it can drive tourism’s share of Malaysia’s gross domestic product beyond its current 15 per cent.

The commercial returns on such projects tend to be modest, according to Yeah Kim Leng, director of Sunway University’s economic studies programme, but there are other cultural and heritage benefits that do not show up neatly on a balance sheet.

“Integrating with local arts, crafts, food and entertainment will be important to maximise tourist spending along with enhancing visitors’ experiences and return visits,” Yeah said.

He sees no tension between old landmarks and new ones: Merdeka 118 and the regeneration of Kuala Lumpur’s historic districts can complement each other, both attracting investment into hotels, restaurants, commercial property and transport infrastructure.

True success, Yeah argues, is not measured by raw visitor numbers. Instead, it is found in the jobs created, the wages lifted and the living standards raised for everyone the work touches.

Back on Lebuh Ampang, Razalie knows this balance all too well, without needing an economist to explain it to him.

His family’s business has survived not by staying the same but by adapting – what it sells, how it sells and who it sells to – while holding onto something intangible that has not changed: the trust built up over generations of customers.

He hopes his son will one day take the reins, even if he doubts the business will look anything like it does now under his management.

“Nothing is going to be easy in the coming years,” Razalie said. “We have to stay one step ahead.”
