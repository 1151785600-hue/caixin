US-led Luzon corridor in Philippines faces growing scrutiny over real local benefits

SCMP | FREE | 1105 words | 2026-09-15

While Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos Jnr was pitching the Luzon Economic Corridor to a 600-strong delegation of foreign investors and officials last week, protesters outside were clashing with police, accusing the US-backed initiative of exploiting the country’s resources.

The backlash mirrored opposition against the Pax Silica AI supply chain drive – a separate proposal but again, American-led. It underscored mounting tension over the ambitious corridor, since expanded from a transport and logistics project into development plans for energy, digital infrastructure, critical minerals and advanced manufacturing.

Analysts say the pushback does not necessarily signal a wider rejection of Washington in a country where public trust in the US remains relatively strong. But the latest stirrings point to scepticism over local benefit from such foreign investments.

The inaugural Luzon Economic Corridor Investment Forum on Thursday and Friday at the Grand Hyatt Hotel in Bonifacio Global City was sponsored by the US Trade and Development Authority and co-hosted by the Philippines, the United States and Japan – countries that had announced the initiative during a summit in 2024.

Tensions erupted outside the venue, with human rights group Karapatan claiming that over two dozen supporters were injured and five paralegals arrested while protesting against what the group called a “US-backed open house for the plunder of Philippine resources”.

Initially envisioned as a project linking three main ports and two international airports across four cities on the main island of Luzon, the corridor is now also drawing investments in energy, digital systems, cybersecurity, shipbuilding and advanced manufacturing.

Marcos said in his speech that it had drawn “wider support” from other like-minded nations. Canada, Australia, Denmark, France, Italy, South Korea, Sweden and the United Kingdom pledged support in May while Spain and the EU announced their participation at the forum.

“This growing interest reflects confidence in the Philippines as a reliable partner and an attractive destination for long-term investment,” the president said. “Across all these sectors, our goal is not simply to attract investment. We want investment that strengthens our economy and expands Filipino capability.”

The protesters also rallied against the proposed Pax Silica hub, a 1,620-hectare (4,000-acre) industrial zone that will sit in New Clark City, part of the Luzon Economic Corridor.

Pax Silica is a US-led coalition aimed at countering Chinese dominance in critical supply chains such as AI and semiconductors. The Philippines and Singapore are the only Southeast Asian countries to join the 13-member bloc. It has seen online backlash due to concerns over resource extraction and the widespread displacement of farmers and indigenous communities.

Younger dissent

Net trust among Filipinos for the US, though, remains “good”, according to Pollster Social Weather Stations. The June survey of 1,200 Filipinos found that 60 per cent were still positive about the US compared with 18 per cent who voiced distrust.

Dissent towards the two US-led projects likely came from Gen Z, experts said.

Sonny Africa, executive director of Manila-based non-profit think tank IBON Foundation, called the backlash “extremely significant politically”.

“They show that reported positive views of the US don’t translate into public acceptance of every US economic or military initiative, and that the public is receptive to seeing these for what they really are beyond predictably glowing US and Philippine government hype,” Africa said.

“If this leads to greater understanding of US historical dominance of the Philippines and of worsening global US economic and military aggression today, the Pax Silica protests could eventually snowball into a generalised anti-American turn.”

Online access allowed people to decide for themselves. “Reports on the displacement of indigenous peoples strengthen the sense that these projects are more exploitative than beneficial,” said Gino Trinidad, a political science instructor at Ateneo de Manila University.

“It does not help that a considerable number of Filipinos – especially the youth – have had access to reports of how the building of data centres in the US and elsewhere have contributed to the depleting of natural resources and polluting of water.”

A Momentum Research survey of 1,487 Filipinos from August 18 to 23 found that while 33.9 per cent decried the Pax Silica project, opposition was highest among Gen Z compared with others at 43.2 per cent, with adequate power and water supply for data centre operations topping their concerns.

Gen Z Filipinos were also the most concerned about environmental effects and the displacement of farming and indigenous folk, the polling firm said.

“For a country that has locations still facing problems of accessing potable water, the potential ill-effects of the initiatives become harder to ignore,” Trinidad said.

While initiatives such as the Luzon Economic Corridor promise “1 million jobs” for Filipinos, their critics are sceptical that these would provide higher value for the country and offset resource exploitation.

“My sense is that the main promise of these initiatives is job creation … Jobs will indeed be created, but what kinds and if these jobs are high-value are questions that must be addressed clearly,” Trinidad said, pointing out that Central Luzon was already in the top three regions for total gross domestic product.

“The level and potential effects of the exploitation of the Philippines’ natural resources by these projects should be clearly addressed. Furthermore, the redistributive potential of such projects must be scrutinised. Will these projects really lead to a more equitable distribution of economic growth?”

‘Minor’ benefits

Africa called the expected benefits for the Philippines “shallow, minor and incidental at best if the overriding logic of the initiatives is another country’s strategic purposes”.

“There will be construction activity, some foreign-oriented infrastructure with limited domestic use, and perhaps some foreign-owned mineral processing aside from how the government can report additional exports, jobs and foreign investment,” he said. “However, there is not likely to be much in terms of the Filipino firms, technology, profits and strategic productive capacity that constitute real Philippine industrial development.”

US desire for technological dominance in semiconductors and AI would result in the Philippines staying in its “subordinate role in US-led value semiconductor chains”, Africa said, adding that it had been the case for decades despite billions of dollars in investments by American firms.

“There’s also the opportunity cost. Philippine infrastructure, minerals and public resources will be directed towards the requirements of US semiconductor, AI and military supply chains instead of the country’s own national industrialisation and development priorities.”

He warned that increased economic dependence on the US “becomes another mechanism to reinforce strategic alignment with the US, while excluding potentially productive relations with countries the US seeks to isolate such as China”.

“National industrialisation and development is difficult enough as it is without these major external constraints,” he said.
