‘A pack of lies’: Abby Choi’s mother rejects claims Kwong family repaid her debts

SCMP | FREE | 1036 words | 2026-10-14

The mother of slain Hong Kong socialite Abby Choi Tin-fung has denied in court that she was repeatedly in debt because of gambling, calling her former son-in-law’s claim that he had financially supported her “a pack of lies”.

Cheung Yin-fa accused Alex Kwong Kong-chi on Monday of acting unscrupulously by demanding the return of HK$150,000 in shopping expenses she owed him, while failing to repay any of the more than HK$70 million he had received from Choi for investment.

Wrapping up her five-day testimony in the High Court, Cheung also rejected the defence’s suggestion that she was so addicted to mahjong that Choi had to pledge a property she received as a wedding gift to secure a HK$4 million mortgage to repay her mother’s debts.

Cheung appeared emotional as she responded to the defence’s contention that Kwong, who said he had run a restaurant in mainland China and worked as a parallel betting agent in Macau, had helped repay her debts and supported her over the years.

“This Kwong family of brutes repaid my debts? Impossible,” she said. “How can he tell such a lie? Is he human? It’s all a pack of lies.”

She also denied ever receiving HK$7.5 million from Kwong through a luxury watch company, saying the money was simply a return of funds she had given him via a currency exchange shop.

Kwong, 31, is accused of conspiring with his father and elder brother to murder his ex-wife after parts of the 28-year-old’s dismembered remains, including her head and legs, were found at a flat in Tai Po’s Lung Mei Village in February 2023.

He is standing trial on a joint murder charge alongside his father, Kwong Kau, 68, and elder brother, Anthony Kwong Kong-kit, 35.

Kwong Kau, a retired police sergeant, has pleaded not guilty to preventing the lawful burial of Choi’s body, while his two sons pleaded guilty to that charge before the trial.

Prosecutors argue the family feud stemmed from the proposed sale of a HK$72 million (US$9.17 million) flat at the Kadooria residential estate in Ho Man Tin. The father, the property’s registered owner, allegedly resented Choi for trying to sell the luxury home and enlisted his two sons to help kill her so that he could keep it.

Under cross-examination, Alex Kwong’s counsel, Andrew Raffell, said his client did not oppose the sale and had been asked by Choi to persuade his father to sign a power of attorney allowing her to negotiate with prospective buyers.

Cheung reiterated that the entire Kwong family had been reluctant to sell the flat until she threatened legal action.

Raffell cited a series of text conversations that he said showed Alex Kwong had previously given his former mother-in-law more than HK$500,000.

Cheung rejected that interpretation, saying she owed Kwong only HK$150,000 for shopping expenses. She added that he had given her daughter HK$370,000 in profit from the sale of a car that his ex-wife had bought.

On her gambling habits, Cheung acknowledged that she occasionally visited mahjong parlours with friends and gambled up to HK$100,000 for “entertainment”, but stressed she had never run up debts so severe that debt collectors harassed her family.

Raffell suggested that Choi helped her mother clear her debts by taking out a second mortgage of up to HK$4 million from a high-rise property at the Manhattan Hill residential estate in Lai Chi Kok, which she received from the father of her partner, Chris Tam Fong-chun, as a wedding gift.

The counsel noted that the white Toyota Alphard that Cheung had been using to travel to court in the present case was splashed with red paint in February this year, which he said was indicative of her financial difficulties.

Messaging records showed that Cheung complained to Detective Constable Ko Yan-ki, the chief investigating officer of the murder case, that she had been harassed by a woman she met in a mahjong parlour in Mong Kok over a monetary dispute she thought had been resolved seven years ago.

Raffell also highlighted text exchanges between Cheung and Choi, in which the witness acknowledged she would occasionally play mahjong “till daybreak” and that what she owed her daughter “cannot even be repaid in this life”.

Cheung dismissed Alex Kwong’s contention as smearing and stressed she did not have any outstanding debt. She also denied that her gratitude towards Choi was because she had repaid her gambling debts, saying she was simply thankful for her daughter’s support.

Raffell suggested that Alex Kwong had co-owned a restaurant in Guangzhou and knew an agent who sold Richard Mille watches in Macau.

Cheung admitted she had no direct knowledge of her former son-in-law’s income, apart from what Choi had told her about his failure to contribute to the support of their two children.

Separately, Raffell argued that his client had been angry with Cheung for calling police after Choi disappeared on February 21, 2023, despite knowing that he was wanted over his role in a HK$6.3 million theft case after jumping bail in 2015.

Cheung said she was unaware that authorities were still pursuing her former in-law. She reiterated that she believed the matter had been “settled” by Kwong Kau years before Choi’s killing.

During re-examination, prosecutor on fiat Charlotte Draycott SC sought to counter the defence’s suggestion that Cheung’s family was not as wealthy as she had claimed. She asked Cheung to explain the jewellery and luxury items she had worn or brought to court during her week-long testimony.

They included a luxury watch set with diamonds and rubies, a pair of bracelets, a diamond necklace and a pair of earrings.

Cheung said the valuables – excluding the watch, which was a gift from Choi – were worth at least HK$1 million.

She added that the handbag she carried to court on Monday was worth about HK$50,000, while another Hermes Birkin bag she had previously brought to court had been bought by Alex Kwong for more than HK$100,000 using her money.

The trial continues before Deputy Judge Brian Keith and a nine-member jury.
