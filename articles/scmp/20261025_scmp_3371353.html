Hong Kong’s Huanggang crossing absorbs 366% surge as travellers stream back

SCMP | FREE | 1138 words | 2026-10-25

Inbound crossings at Hong Kong’s revamped Huanggang Port surged by 366 per cent on Monday afternoon over comparable periods in September, as the first long weekend since its reopening came to a close, with authorities expecting an even greater rush later in the evening.

A steady stream of travellers returned to Hong Kong through the checkpoint on Monday, with crowds growing noticeably by 5pm, peaking at around 6.15pm, and tapering off by 7pm, as observed by the South China Morning Post.

Unlike Sunday, when departing travellers complained on social media about long wait times during peak hours, Monday’s inbound flow remained orderly.

“It’s a very nice port – the bathrooms are clean, it’s huge and spacious … what a great thing to see in our lifetime,” said 66-year-old retiree Adam Ko, who was with his wife, Mary.

The couple said they were aware of the large crowds at the crossing over the weekend.

“It’s still early in the night, so we decided to come here hoping to skip the peak hours. It was very smooth and we just breezed through,” said Ko.

However, he noted minor traffic congestion while approaching the port by taxi in Shenzhen at around 4.30pm. “We were stuck on the roads for maybe 10 minutes. Acceptable, but I’d think twice if it was any later in the day.”

A Shenzhen resident in her forties who works in the garment industry and gave only her surname, Tang, said she preferred the revamped port for its convenience.

“This new arrangement saves me about 20 minutes crossing the border,” she said.

According to the Security Bureau, 9,401 travellers entered Hong Kong via Huanggang Port between 2pm and 4pm on Monday, a 366 per cent surge from the average number of inbound crossings through the former Huanggang/Lok Ma Chau control point during the same hours across the four weekends in September.

It expected the number of inbound travellers to rise further in the evening.

With the expected surge in crossings, all immigration clearance channels were opened, while authorities prepared crowd-control measures and designated waiting areas for passengers using public transport.

The holiday marked the first major test for the revamped crossing between Hong Kong and mainland China, which reopened on October 12 with a new joint immigration clearance system designed to cut processing times from about half an hour to as little as five minutes.

Travellers can complete clearance by presenting a single identity document and passing through an automated channel with three gates.

Yung Ka-wai, deputy commander of the Immigration Department’s Lok Ma Chau Division, said clearance at Huanggang had been smooth on Monday and expected the afternoon-to-evening period to be the busiest for foot traffic.

“We will continue to monitor the situation at the control points in real time and flexibly deploy manpower according to passenger flows to speed up the clearing of crowds,” Yung said.

“Travellers are advised to plan their journeys early, choose a suitable control point and avoid crossing the border during peak hours.”

He also reminded eligible residents to register in advance if they wished to use the new clearance model.

Commissioner for Transport Winnie Tse Wing-yee inspected Huanggang Port on Monday morning, instructing officers and public transport operators to closely monitor passenger and traffic flows and deploy resources flexibly. She also asked bus companies to run additional short-haul special services to clear travellers more quickly.

The Transport Department said about 120 franchised buses, 40 minibuses and 130 cross-border buses had been deployed for service and standby on Monday.

Headways for franchised buses would be shortened to two to three minutes during peak hours, and to three to four minutes for minibuses and cross-border buses.

Huanggang handled more than 152,000 crossings on Sunday, with some travellers waiting up to two hours amid congestion that lawmakers attributed to unfamiliarity with new clearance procedures, a surge in traffic and computer system reboots.

Immigration Department figures showed the port handled 152,068 crossings, the highest daily total since it opened on October 12. These comprised 77,166 outbound trips and 74,902 inbound journeys.

Hongkongers accounted for 133,265 crossings, while mainland visitors made 16,832 trips.

The facility is designed to handle up to 300,000 travellers and 15,000 vehicles a day, with daily passenger numbers initially expected to range from 100,000 to 200,000.

As the Chung Yeung Festival break fuelled a surge in cross-border travel over the weekend, the Security Bureau issued a “busy” advisory for Huanggang on its “Easy Boundary” portal on Sunday afternoon, warning that clearance could take up to 45 minutes. Some mainland visitors, however, reported queuing for as long as two hours.

Lawmaker Ben Chan Han-pan, chairman of the Legislative Council’s transport panel, said travellers’ unfamiliarity with the new “collaborative inspection and joint immigration clearance” model was the main cause of congestion on Sunday, with some attempting to use the automated channels without registering beforehand.

He added that the sudden surge in passenger numbers had also overwhelmed the control point.

Chan said computer systems at the immigration counters had slowed after running for extended periods and needed to be rebooted, further lengthening queues.

Lawmaker Chong Ho-fung said the long queues at the newly reopened port over the weekend were inevitable given the large number of travellers crossing the border during the Chung Yeung Festival peak.

He said that close to 40,000 people crossed at Huanggang between 10am and 2pm on Sunday, averaging nearly 10,000 an hour, and even with each passenger taking just a minute to complete clearance, queues could easily result in an hour-long wait.

“The government’s projections and my own both forecast this would happen. It is a chaotic situation that was within expectations,” Chong told a radio programme.

He urged authorities to step up publicity about registration procedures and eligibility requirements for the new “joint clearance” automated channels.

Children under seven cannot use the automated channels and must be accompanied by their parents to staffed counters to complete clearance. Those aged seven to 17 can use the “joint clearance” channels once their parents have registered them.

Chong said clearer information would help prevent families with young children from reaching the front of the queue only to discover they were ineligible and had to move to staffed counters, adding to their clearance time.

“There were too many different reasons why a passenger could not complete clearance, and staff on the ground may not be able to identify the exact reason immediately, causing a chaotic situation,” he said.

He also suggested designating more clearance lanes for use by both foreign travellers and Hong Kong residents to speed up the process.

Chong said he had also observed system reboots on Sunday in the “joint one-stop” vehicle clearance lane because of mismatches between Hong Kong and Shenzhen data.

But he said the congestion was unlikely to persist, as clearance should become smoother once more travellers had registered properly.
