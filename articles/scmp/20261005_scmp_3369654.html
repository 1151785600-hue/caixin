Chinese art takes long march home from US museums

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1746 words | 2026-10-05

Christie’s auctioneer Rupert Banner leans forward with practised showmanship as bidding stalls on a rare Yongzheng bowl near New York’s iconic Rockefeller Centre. He pauses as if willing offers from thin air, scanning dozens of in-person buyers and phone banks connecting with China, London, Texas.

It will be a bad look if the frenzy abates for this delicate peach-decorated bowl highlighted on Christie’s glossy Chinese sale catalogue and online advertising. But bidding soon resumes with a realised price of US$2.05 million, the sale’s top porcelain result.

The bowl is among over 100 items from the Art Institute of Chicago, part of a broader generational transition as rarefied relics amassed by 19th century American Gilded Age “robber barons” return to Chinese hands after gathering dust for more than a century in Western museums.

Then it is the turn of lot 1032, an archaistic zun-form vase once owned by finance magnate Charles Schwab. “They’re off and running,” says Banner, before a Chinese man snags it for US$40,960.

“China lost many relics to the West because of corrupt officials,” the new owner says. “We started losing our best relics to the West after the Song dynasty, lost on the Maritime Silk Road, with Guangzhou among the key ports. I want to bring this back to Guangzhou.”

The “wealthy farmer” from Jiangxi, who is in his 40s and dressed in black sportswear and trainers, does not give his name like many in the discreet art trade.

“The West is in decline, and it’s only fitting,” he says, adding that he will donate it to a Chinese museum. His next stop: France and Italy to repatriate more treasures.

Institute sales from the two-day event total US$17.3 million, led by a waistless Huanghuali daybed at US$3.2 million.

For Chinese art collectors, items from US museums hold special cachet, given their prestige and assurances against counterfeiting.

“The pieces that have museum provenance are absolutely the focus of any collector,” said Angela McAteer, Chinese works of art department head at rival auction house Sotheby’s. “In a category that’s plagued by not only fake pieces but fake provenance, fake catalogues, fake labels, you name it, a de-accession piece with that provenance is watertight.”

Provenance – ensuring items are not fake or stolen – and de-accession – essentially selling unneeded stuff from your attic – are among the rarefied art world’s special language, along with good title, burned art and white-glove sales.

US museum auctions also offer a recent workaround. After US President Donald Trump’s 2025 tariff bender, imported Chinese art faced up to 12.5 per cent taxes – and in some categories double – evidence that even art cannot avoid geopolitics, spurring US-sourced sales. A few months ago, after intense lobbying, the US Trade Representative agreed to exempt fine art.

Recent auctions include items from the Metropolitan Museum of Art, Brooklyn Museum, San Francisco Asian Art Museum and Honolulu Museum of Art.

“Every major museum in America has had to grapple with this. These American tycoons were buying all this Chinese art and donating thousands of objects,” said Vicki Paloympis, Christie’s Chinese department head, navigating half-opened crates ahead of the public viewing. “Insurance is high, storage is expensive, and they have all these pieces that are duplicates and triplets.”

Another factor, adds McAteer, is that museums display fewer objects these days, leaving more in storage.

Asian museums tend not to de-accession given strict heritage-protection laws, although private or corporate museums with underfunded endowments sometimes do. Japan’s Fujita Art Museum faced a cash crunch before its hugely successful 2017 US$262.8 million sale.

And last month’s heavily subscribed Okada Museum of Art Hong Kong auction of rare Chinese ceramics yielded HK$174.7 million (US$22.3 million) after its founder, pachinko magnate Kazuo Okada, faced a US$50 million legal dispute involving casino mogul Steve Wynn. Hong Kong faces fewer art-sale restrictions than mainland China.

But museum sales are hardly exempt from criticism.

“Some people were nationalistic. They wrote saying, look, American museums are getting rid of Chinese art because they don’t like Chinese culture,” said Tao Wang, the Chicago institute’s Chinese art curator, after months spent teeing up the sale. “But we made statements, and people realised what the de-accession purpose is, to upgrade and enhance.”

De-accession at accredited US museums follows exhaustive legal, ethical and curatorial standards, including restrictions on how proceeds can be spent after some early scandals.

“There are some people who dig in their heels and say museums shouldn’t sell their treasures, blah, blah,” said Eric Zetterquist, an artist and gallery owner. “I­t’s a much more natural process than people realise.”

Rising prices have given US museums further incentive to houseclean. The global Chinese fine art auction market rose from US$200-300 million in 1995 to US$5 billion in 2015 before settling at US$1.75 billion last year, according to industry estimates, with huge upside. A single Andy Warhol image of Brigitte Bardot sold in May for US$24.8 million, more than the entire Okada collection.

Long wary of the trade, Beijing has increasingly welcomed collecting while keeping a close eye on corruption, capital flight, tax evasion and political dissent.

“This is not a flashy Rolls-Royce, so Chinese people can still buy and not feel like the government’s going to come down on them,” said Zetterquist. “And the government wholly encourages people to buy Chinese masterpieces and bring them back to China.”

Wang’s career mirrors the dramatic arc of China’s art market.

Born in Yunnan in 1962, he lived through the 1966–1976 Cultural Revolution, watching art and books burned in the river and the anti-“Four Olds” campaigns that saw museums closed.

His early formal education was patchy. But he was a voracious reader, helped by an uncle who slipped him two “huge” Chinese literature books from a boarded-up library and by private calligraphy lessons.

“The artist was professional. But not like a professional painter. He drew cinema posters,” Wang said. “It was far from the capital, so artists had more freedom … There was no payment. You just had to know someone.”

Provincial universities reopened in 1977 and his clandestine reading helped him get into college at 16 alongside 30-something classmates.

Graduating from Yunnan Normal, he headed to Beijing for further studies and then to London in 1986 where he saw his first proper museum. After receiving an art history PhD in 1993, he taught at SOAS University of London and University College London before moving to Sotheby’s in 2012 and Chicago in 2015.

“At the bottom of my heart, I’m an academic,” he said, adding that he never imagined Chinese art would reach such heights.

In 2011, China’s Hurun Rich List listed 271 mainland US-dollar billionaires, though Wang says that 600 was probably more accurate. That year, Chinese art accounted for nearly half of global art auction revenues. Since then, curators say, Chinese collectors have become increasingly knowledgeable.

“The nouveau riche are now people who made their fortunes in the 80s and 90s and are sending their kids to Vassar,” said Zetterquist. “Their taste is much more sophisticated.”

Porcelain holds a particular fascination, especially items like the peach bowl made in imperial kilns. Chinese buyers last month pored over items in Christie’s pre-auction galleries, sometimes holding them for extended periods.

“Chinese people have a special relationship with ceramics because they in many ways invented them,” Wang said.

The rarity of top-end pieces and the centuries-long struggle to crack the recipe saw Westerners similarly fixated.

American shipping fortunes after 1800, some built on the illegal opium trade, spurred early interest. But demand exploded among Gilded Age steel, oil and banking magnates. The focus was initially on decorative Qing dynasty ceramics as political instability opened Chinese imperial collections and tomb excavations. Later decades saw their tastes refined towards older, rarer Song, Tang and Zhou dynasty items.

“These things were considered to be what aristocrats, old money, collected, and so that’s what the robber barons wanted,” Zetterquist said.

The original Morgans, Rockefellers and Fricks eventually died, leaving their collections to museums. More than a century later, some of those objects are passing to wealthy Chinese collectors.

But even timeless art weathers trends.

Chinese collectors tend to shun Tang dynasty horses and other funerary items favoured by Westerners.

“There’s the idea that the tomb has been invaded, so to speak, and the spirit of the deceased has been left without things that he or she needed,” said Robert Mowry, a senior consultant and Harvard Art Museums’ curator emeritus.

“The ironic thing is, a lot of other things that Chinese collectors are interested in, like ancient bronze vessels, like ancient jades from the Zhou dynasty, came out of tombs as well. But no one worries about that in terms of the spirit of the deceased seeking revenge.”

Chinese have also frequently sniffed at “export” Chinese art, often larger items in bold colours, some even adorned with lavish gold frames targeted at foreigners after the 16th century.

“People were just filling up crates and bringing them home,” said Zetterquist. “Think about the aesthetics of the Gilded Age. It was a lot of googly gook, shiny things with cachet.”

Of particular interest for new Chinese buyers are items associated with past emperors, especially Qianlong’s 1735–1796 reign.

“The nouveau riche collectors, it’s all about Qianlong. If Qianlong liked it, it must be good,” said Mowry, even though some imperial items were made in lots of 100,000.

“If somebody visits the White House, they’re going to leave with some kind of gift,” he added. “When it’s made for the palace, everyone thinks it’s made for the emperor. Well, not everything was.”

But imperial provenance provides a certain assurance, curators said.

“The brand new fakes can be very convincing,” said Mowry. “Qianlong? Come back tomorrow, we’ll have some more for you … There’s the idea: if we buy it in the US or Europe or wherever, and it’s been out of China for a long time, like 40 years, it’s probably OK.”

While patriotism may drive some Chinese bidders, others less so, he added. “They’re virtually all dealers,” said Mowry. “There’s nothing wrong with that. But to mask it all as repatriation is part of the truth, but not the whole truth.”

Wang said museums should treasure their collections as a mother does her children, even when they’re banished. “They’re going to a better home,” he added.
