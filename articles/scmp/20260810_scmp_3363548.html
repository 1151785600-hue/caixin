As US marks 75 years of defence pact with Philippines, can it stay the course?

SCMP | FREE | 1009 words | 2026-08-10

The Philippines and the United States are marking the 75th anniversary of their defence alliance with a renewed pledge to step up joint drills as geopolitical tensions in the region continue to simmer.

While the allies are moving towards more frequent planning for maritime security cooperation and deterrence, there are still questions about Washington’s long-term commitment towards the security of the Philippines and the broader region, according to analysts.

At an annual meeting to strengthen security engagement in Manila on Saturday, Admiral Samuel Paparo, commander of the US Indo-Pacific Command, and Armed Forces of the Philippines chief of staff General Antonio Nafarrete reaffirmed their commitment to the mutual defence treaty signed in 1951 and signed a memorandum to map out the next phase of cooperation.

While Paparo and Nafarrete did not disclose details, US Pacific Command said the plans included expanding on large-scale military exercises and specialist exchanges.

In recent years, the US and the Philippines have taken part in joint and multilateral military drills with partners such as Japan, Australia and New Zealand, including in contested waters near the Southeast Asian country.

Expanding ties with other allies

The alliance is stepping up such cooperation as the Philippines sits at the intersection of the South China Sea and the Taiwan Strait – two theatres that could spark a potential conflict between major powers, according to analysts.

Developments near Taiwan would have major implications for Philippine security and US regional planning, said Vina Nadjibulla, co-founder and chief executive of the Centre for Strategic Statecraft, a Canadian foreign policy think tank.

Beijing sees Taiwan as part of China to be reunited by force if necessary. Most countries, including the US, do not recognise Taiwan as an independent state, but Washington is opposed to any attempt to take the self-governed island by force and is committed to supplying it with weapons.

“The next phase should be about networked deterrence: stronger Philippine defence capabilities, deeper US-Philippines planning and closer cooperation with Japan, Australia and other partners,” Nadjibulla said.

“The objective should be to make coercion more difficult and give Manila more strategic options, not fewer,” she said.

Nadjibulla noted the increasing sophistication of the joint drills through bilateral planning and real-time information sharing in areas such as air and missile defence, maritime offence, unmanned systems and cyber cooperation.

As such, the treaty between Manila and Washington is becoming “less of a passive insurance policy and more of an active deterrence architecture”.

China’s growing military capabilities and aggressive activities in the South China Sea had accelerated cooperation between the two allies, Nadjibulla said, citing Beijing’s use of water cannons and dangerous manoeuvres during confrontations around Scarborough Shoal and Second Thomas Shoal.

“For Washington, the Philippines now matters not only because of the South China Sea disputes but because its geography is increasingly consequential to preserving deterrence across the first island chain,” she added.

Washington’s island chain strategy dates back to the Cold War. The first island chain stretches from the Kuril Islands through Japan, Taiwan, and the Philippines down to Borneo, with the second island chain including the major US base at Guam further east, extending through the Marianas to Palau and New Guinea.

Next stage of alliance

Muhammad Faizal Bin Abdul Rahman, a research fellow at the S. Rajaratnam School of International Studies in Singapore, said the evolution of the Manila-Washington alliance reflected a shift in the geopolitical landscape.

“While it was important to the US during the Cold War as part of the strategic competition between the Western and communist blocs, it has now become a key strategic and operational frontline in the competition between the US and China,” Faizal said.

Sylwia Monika Gorska, a political analyst at the University of Central Lancashire, said the alliance had moved from the Americans having a large permanent military presence in the Philippines during the Cold War to rotational deployments and counterterrorism cooperation followed by cooperation focused on maritime and territorial defence.

Gorska cited the Enhanced Defence Cooperation Agreement signed by the allies to rotate US troops in the Philippines for longer stays and deploy American equipment in Philippine territories, such as northern Luzon, as central to the shift in defence cooperation.

To further enhance the alliance, Washington and Manila should build resilience in the areas of logistics, communications and command-and-control networks, according to Gorska.

“The next stage of the alliance is therefore not simply geographical expansion of access, but whether distributed forces can remain supplied, connected and operational when the network supporting them is under pressure,” she added.

Chris Gardiner, chief executive of the Institute for Regional Security, said that while the memorandum was another step in strengthening the bilateral partnership, the alliance should be further enhanced by expanding defence cooperation with Japan and Australia.

“The treaty is strong and strengthening because there is a clear strategic confluence of interests,” Gardiner said, adding that the Trump administration viewed closer alignment with the Philippines as more important than its strategy over Taiwan.

However, doubts still linger about Washington’s commitment to the long-term security of the Philippines and the region, according to analysts.

Faizal cited the US aversion towards being drawn into another conflict amid the ongoing war against Iran and signs of Gulf allies reassessing their security ties with Washington.

“This makes it necessary for the Philippines and other US allies to work more closely to ensure that the hub-and-spokes alliance system continues to function by strengthening the spokes [allies] to compensate for the hub [US] becoming less credible,” he explained.

Gorska said while Manila’s other regional allies were stepping up to the plate, they would not be able to replace the US as the anchor of regional security.

“Japan and Australia already give Manila additional sources of surveillance, logistics, equipment, training and operational support, reducing its reliance on Washington for some functions,” Gorska explained.

“What they cannot substitute is the treaty guarantee itself, or the scale of high-end military capability that sits behind it,” she said.
