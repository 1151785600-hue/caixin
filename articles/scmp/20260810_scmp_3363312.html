Chip off the old bloc: Thailand looks to Siam Silica to propel it to top Asean player

SCMP | FREE | 1261 words | 2026-08-10

Thailand launched its Siam Silica framework last week, a national road map that stamps the Southeast Asian country’s stake in the semiconductor race by building a domestic ecosystem and positioning it as a key hub within an integrated supply chain among Asean member states.

However, analysts say that the country will have much ground to cover as it trails behind neighbours Malaysia and Singapore.

At the unveiling by the Ministry of Higher Education, Science, Research and Innovation (MHESI) on July 27, minister Yodchanan Wongsawat called semiconductor development “a national priority”.

Siam Silica will target five areas: photonics fabrication, advanced packaging, silicon design, quantum photonics and power devices. Thailand aims to establish eight integrated circuit design companies, one semiconductor fabrication plant, and two advanced semiconductor packaging facilities by 2030.

The MHESI also announced it was launching a Higher Education Sandbox degree programme for universities to fast-track deep-tech expertise and develop advanced competencies.

STEMPlus, the ministry’s human resources development and planning platform, will recruit 152 lecturers and scientists, 950 researchers, experts and senior engineers, 553 highly skilled engineers and 1,330 technicians for the programme.

Bangkok’s ambitions also include creating an Asean Talent Observatory to forecast demand in the workforce, incorporating photonics and printed circuit boards into the Asean Semiconductor Roadmap, and growing its Thailand Skill Bridge platform to collaborate on research and development with other countries in the region.

To achieve that, Thailand should push for closer Association of Southeast Asian Nations integration so the bloc could strengthen its resilience and regional bargaining power amid disruptions in the global supply chain, Surin Khomfoi, vice-president of the Office of the National Higher Education, Science, Research and Innovation Policy Council, was quoted as saying by the Bangkok Post.

Thailand could also look into producing power control chips, photonics chips and sensor chips, as there is a high market demand and low number of players in this sector, according to Surin.

Appropriately ambitious

The recently unveiled plans reflect Thailand’s long-standing ambitions to move into higher-value segments of the semiconductor manufacturing chain, such as chip design and wafer fabrication.

In 2024, Thailand established the inter-ministry National Semiconductor Board to promote its semiconductor and advanced electronics industries, and deepen its foothold in global value chains.

However, analysts say the Southeast Asian country faces an uphill battle.

“It’s a tall order, for two reasons. First, Thailand is a latecomer to the semiconductor value chain compared with Singapore and Malaysia. Announcing a road map to build, by 2030, an ecosystem that can rival what those two neighbours have spent decades assembling is very different from actually delivering it,” Pavida Pananond, a professor of international business at Thammasat University’s Thammasat Business School, told This Week in Asia.

“Second, Thailand also lags in the breadth of semiconductor activities it can perform.”

In 2024, Thailand’s semiconductor exports reached 436 billion baht (US$13.8 billion). Malaysia’s total that year was US$95.6 billion, nearly seven times that.

“Thailand exports a much smaller value in semiconductors each year than Malaysia and Singapore. It would have to increase exports by orders of magnitude to catch up, which is unrealistic in the near term,” said Robert Walker, a research fellow at the Lowy Institute’s Indo-Pacific Development Centre.

Thailand’s traditional role in the semiconductor chain has largely been confined to back-end work such as assembly, packaging and testing, according to Pavida.

“Expanding into front-end chip manufacturing and design will take a lot more than financial incentives and a government push to attract investment,” she said. “Both design and front-end manufacturing demand highly specialised technology and skills that simply aren’t as readily available in Thailand today as they are in Singapore and Malaysia.”

While Thailand’s policy appeared to be “well-designed” and “appropriately ambitious and realistic”, it would have to be a “decades-long strategy” – particularly if it wanted to become a leading player in the region, Walker said.

“Siam Silica’s focus on skill pipelines, rationalising government support mechanisms, and trying to leverage regional cooperation all look positive. Thailand is unlikely to overtake leaders like Malaysia and Singapore but can still make itself indispensable to regional supply chains,” he said.

Managing expectations

Thailand would have to be realistic in its expectations, Pavida said.

“Thailand is trying to build this ecosystem in 2026, when Singapore and Malaysia already began that journey some 50 years ago – and neither of them is standing still,” she said.

“So there is a lot of catching up, and even leapfrogging, that Thailand will need to do if it wants to become a central player in an integrated regional supply chain.”

Siam Silica’s success will depend on the effectiveness of its implementation, according to Pradittha Werasakun and Piriya Piriyakrit, senior analysts at BowerGroupAsia.

While Thailand has established its strengths in the downstream semiconductor segment, it needs to solve key issues such as limited wafer manufacturing capability, technology transfer, and high-skilled talent to build the ecosystem required to support its goal as a central hub in the region.

The greater challenge might be political rather than technical, Werasakun and Piriyakrit told This Week in Asia.

“The initiative builds on momentum generated under the previous Pheu Thai-led government, which demonstrated its ability to mobilise investment behind strategic sectors such as data centres. Siam Silica is now being advanced by Deputy Prime Minister and MHESI Minister Yodchanan as a national priority. However, semiconductor ecosystems require sustained policy support over many years,” they said.

“Given Thailand’s changing coalition dynamics, investors will likely pay close attention to whether the initiative remains a consistent cross-government priority or becomes associated with a particular political leadership or ministry.”

The country’s approach to work with Asean could also come up against nationalist ambitions.

“The six Asean countries with a stake in the semiconductor value chain all want to hold on to the highest value-creating segments for themselves. That dynamic may end up making their relationship more competitive than complementary,” Pavida said.

With a bit of patience, though, Thailand could become a leading player in the region, Walker said.

“Thailand has a strong manufacturing ecosystem and pre-existing semiconductor capabilities, with a relatively well-educated workforce. Combined with a competitive wage level, Thailand is well positioned to push a strategy to become a larger semiconductor hub in the region,” he said.

Rather than meeting its competition head on, Thailand could be a helpmeet, Werasakun and Piriyakrit said.

“Thailand’s competitive advantage lies in complementing, rather than replicating, the strengths of other Asean semiconductor hubs,” they said.

Siam Silica was drafted “to attract companies to help Thailand build a semiconductor ecosystem, and not to compete across the entire value chain in this early stage”.

By building on its existing strengths, Thailand could carve out a niche contributing to the broader value chain of the digital economy, Pavida said.

“One bright spot is the country’s dominance in hard disk drives, which are becoming an integral part of the AI ecosystem, as hyperscalers and data centres increasingly need cheap, high-capacity storage at scale,” she said.

“It may not be as glamorous as advanced chipmaking, but it gives Thailand a differentiated path – becoming an essential, if unglamorous, link in the AI supply chain, while building up a more highly skilled ecosystem and workforce over time.”

Ultimately, a more realistic benchmark is whether companies view Thailand’s contributions as indispensable, according to the BowerGroupAsia analysts.

“If Thailand can deepen its strengths in selected segments while continuing to attract strategic investment and develop skilled talent, then it can become an important regional semiconductor node.”
