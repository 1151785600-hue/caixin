Why small quakes in Malaysia’s Sabah could be sounding seismic alarms

SCMP | FREE | 1079 words | 2026-09-23

A series of small earthquakes beneath Keningau, an inland district of Sabah in Malaysian Borneo, is drawing attention to a part of the state where scientists say the faults are poorly understood and the consequences of a larger quake could be devastating.

The latest, a magnitude-3.4 tremor on September 3, was felt in several parts of Keningau, prompting staff at the district office to suspend a meeting and move to a designated safe area. No serious damage was reported.

The tremor was one of several weak earthquakes recorded around Keningau since 2023, raising questions about efforts to strengthen monitoring, development and disaster planning in the area.

A stronger earthquake in the region could trigger landslides and debris flows, cutting off roads and other vital infrastructure, experts say.

Sabah has focused much of its earthquake monitoring and preparedness on Ranau, a district near Mount Kinabalu where a 2015 earthquake killed 18 people, triggered landslides and caused widespread damage.

But about 100km southwest of Ranau, the similarly mountainous Keningau and Tambunan valleys were identified as seismically active in federal planning guidance in 2018. Geologists say both lie within the Crocker fault zone, a network of geological faults in Sabah’s mountainous interior associated with earthquake activity.

Yet neither Keningau nor Tambunan appears on the government’s published list of mapped active fault zones or areas where developers must check for earthquake risks before building.

It is unclear how extensively the two districts have been mapped and monitored for seismic risks, as the Minerals and Geoscience Department (JMG) did not respond to questions from This Week in Asia.

“Sabah’s seismic hazard is broader than Ranau alone, our scientific understanding of Sabah’s earthquake sources has not yet caught up with the level of development and exposure that now exists in the state,” said Felix Tongkul, a geologist at Universiti Malaysia Sabah (UMS), who has studied faults in the state for more than three decades.

The September 3 tremor was not an isolated event. Mohd Hisham Mohd Anip, director general of the Malaysian Meteorological Department (MetMalaysia), listed six weak earthquakes in the Keningau area since April 2023, ranging from magnitude 1.4 to 3.4.

“What makes Keningau scientifically interesting is not its magnitude, which was small, but the fact that several small earthquakes have occurred there over recent years,” Tongkul said.

Mohd Hisham told This Week In Asia on Friday that the sequence showed local seismic activity “associated with the release of tectonic energy”, with the “possibility of recurring earthquakes”.

Of MetMalaysia’s 80 seismic monitoring stations, 28 are in Sabah and concentrated around the Mount Kinabalu area. Keningau has one at the local meteorological station.

“For now, the current station network is sufficient for monitoring seismic activity on a regional scale,” Mohd Hisham said.

The 13th Malaysia Plan, a national development blueprint covering 2026 to 2030, does not list any new stations in the works. Rather, MetMalaysia will upgrade one station in Sandakan and four others across Sabah.

By contrast, when a series of tremors rattled Segamat in Johor – a state with no history of damaging earthquakes – last August, two new stations were announced in November, along with four to be upgraded under the plan’s first implementation phase.

Seismic records

In November 2025, Johari Abdul Ghani, then acting Natural Resources and Environmental Sustainability minister, said the JMG had mapped 11 active fault zones nationwide between 2016 and 2020, four of them in Sabah: Lobou Lobou and Mensaban in Ranau, and Lahad Datu and Tabin in Lahad Datu.

“Ranau is relatively well studied, whereas places such as Keningau, Tambunan and parts of the interior are much less well characterised,” Tongkul said.

Six fault-movement benchmark stations had been installed in Sabah, Johari said. No fault in Keningau or Tambunan appeared on the list.

This contrasts with the 2018 guideline from PlanMalaysia, the federal town planning department under the Ministry of Housing and Local Government, which names both Keningau and Tambunan as seismically active.

Arthur Joseph Kurup, who took over the portfolio in December 2025, announced 18 additional early-warning systems for Sabah in June. He said four of the 34 systems nationwide were in Sabah, two of them in Kundasang.

The ministry also planned to table a proposal earlier this year to strengthen the 2017 hazard map underpinning Malaysia’s seismic building standard, including new active fault-mapping.

Sabah has held disaster-preparedness exercises since the 2015 earthquake, but publicly posted programmes have largely centred on Kundasang and Ranau. In October 2024, the National Disaster Management Agency (NADMA) and the Sabah government held drills on landslides in Kundasang involving more than 70 personnel from various agencies.

In June, a community disaster-risk initiative in Kundasang included an earthquake simulation, early-warning and evacuation procedures, and landslide-risk mapping.

NADMA and the Sabah Fire and Rescue Department did not respond to questions regarding earthquake contingency plans, preparedness and public education.

Hidden faults

Rodeano Roslee, dean of science and technology at UMS, said the geology of the interior left little doubt about the source.

According to Rodeano, Keningau and Tambunan lie within the Crocker fault zone and normal faults have been documented cutting young river gravels near Keningau with displacements of several metres. Some faults may also be hidden beneath valley sediments.

“We know enough to recognise Keningau and Tambunan as fault-controlled and potentially active areas, but not yet enough to quantify the hazard of individual faults with the confidence required for detailed risk-based planning,” he said.

This uncertainty, he said, had consequences for things such as fault setbacks (a legal buffer zone between a known active geological fault and human structures), predicting how violently the ground would shake in future earthquakes, and the design of critical infrastructure and high-occupancy buildings.

Rodeano said this did not mean development in these two districts should stop, but rather that “the level of geological investigation should be proportional to the consequences of failure”.

The potential risk to Sabah’s mountainous interior is not limited to structural damage from an earthquake itself but also includes secondary hazards like landslides and debris flows, which can cut off vital road and infrastructure links.

Rodeano said the 2015 Ranau disaster showed that Sabah’s danger could extend well beyond the initial shaking, with destabilised slopes and rockfalls causing immediate casualties and loosened debris later capable of being swept into debris flows by heavy tropical rain.

He urged Sabah authorities to shift to statewide fault mapping, better earthquake monitoring and checks on vulnerable areas, and stronger infrastructure planning.

“The recent Keningau earthquakes are not grounds for alarm, but they are grounds for investigation,” Rodeano said.
