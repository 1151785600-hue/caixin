Indonesia’s haze crisis becomes rallying point for student anger at Prabowo

SCMP | FREE | 1163 words | 2026-10-28

Yosafat Menteng travelled from Kalimantan on Indonesia’s Borneo island with five fellow students to join Wednesday’s protest in Jakarta against the government’s handling of forest fires that have blanketed parts of Indonesia and neighbouring countries in toxic haze.

Back home in Palangka Raya, one of the worst-affected cities by the haze, he heads the students’ council at the University of Palangkaraya’s Faculty of Social and Political Sciences.

“We in Kalimantan strongly urge the government to declare the forest fires happening in Sumatra, Kalimantan and Papua a national disaster,” he said.

The protests, taking place in several spots around the capital, have been organised by student leaders from the University of Indonesia along with a coalition of student organisations from universities across the country and civil society groups.

Hundreds of security personnel were deployed in Jakarta ahead of the demonstrations, with a heavy police and military presence and armoured vehicles visible in busy parts of the capital.

The rally is a follow-up to a demonstration on October 20 and is timed to coincide with Youth Pledge Day, a national commemoration of a 1928 declaration that helped lay the foundations for Indonesia’s independence movement.

Organisers have framed the protest around five broad demands: declaring the fires a national disaster and holding responsible companies to account; redirecting public spending towards people’s needs; reducing education costs; tackling corruption and abuse of power; and protecting civil liberties and human rights.

The government has resisted calls to declare the fires a national disaster, arguing it can handle the crisis itself. Critics say the reluctance may reflect concerns about the financial burden of a national disaster designation and greater scrutiny it could bring to the government’s response.

Yosafat said he would like to invite central government officials to spend time working from disaster zones. “It will help them understand the scale and severity of the destruction involved and their consequences for human lives.”

Syahganda Nainggolan, head of Indonesia’s Government Communication Agency, struck a conciliatory tone in response to Wednesday’s protests, saying the government respected students’ right to demonstrate and urging them to hold their rallies “in an orderly, safe and peaceful manner”.

“The government will continue to be open to public input, and this interaction is a positive space that needs to be safeguarded together,” he said in a statement, adding that security forces should adopt a “persuasive and humane approach”.

The students at Wednesday’s protest were also pressing the government to reconsider its spending priorities, including some of Prabowo’s signature welfare programmes.

“We also call for the immediate termination of white-elephant programmes such as the Free Meals and the Red and White Cooperative and channel the funds into free education instead,” Yosafat said.

Prabowo’s free nutritious meals programme aims to provide food to 83 million schoolchildren and pregnant women but has faced mounting criticism over its cost, logistical failures and food-poisoning incidents, with at least 57,000 cases reported.

His Red and White Cooperative initiative aims to establish 80,000 village cooperatives, with 240 trillion rupiah (about US$13 billion) in state-bank loans earmarked for building stores and related facilities. The government will repay the loans, raising concerns among analysts about pressure on public finances and the risk of bad debts.

“Today’s public pressure goes beyond the forest fires alone,” said Nicky Fahrizal, a researcher at the Centre for Strategic and International Studies Indonesia. He said the protests reflected accumulated frustration with government policies “that are out of step with public sentiment and common sense”.

“Calls for the government to act swiftly to tackle the forest fires and declare a national disaster have been voiced by the public for some time,” Nicky noted. “Unfortunately, the government appears to have lacked focus, allowing the crisis to drag on without a dedicated response, firm law enforcement or an evaluation of the officials responsible.”

Whether the protests could translate into policy changes would depend partly on the authorities’ handling of the demonstrations, said Wasisto Raharjo Jati, a political analyst at Indonesia’s National Research and Innovation Agency.

He warned that a crackdown on protestors could lead to further backlash, referring to the death in August last year of a 21-year-old motorcycle taxi driver who was killed after an armoured police vehicle ran him over during a demonstration in Jakarta, triggering outrage and demands for police accountability.

Wasisto said another incident like that “would lead to broader political liability for Prabowo”.

He also argued that if public anger were to escalate into riots, it could “trigger negative sentiment among economic actors, which could lead to real policy changes”.

On top of the domestic outcry, Prabowo’s government is also facing pressure from neighbouring countries affected by the haze, amid criticism of Jakarta’s reluctance to seek further foreign firefighting assistance while insisting it could contain the fires itself.

On Tuesday, Indonesia and Singapore agreed to establish a joint task force to coordinate investigations into companies linked to forest and land fires. The initiative is intended to strengthen information sharing, financial intelligence and cross-border evidence gathering, with the possibility of recovering assets where appropriate.

Indonesian Foreign Minister Arrmanatha Nasir said Singapore had offered to share data on companies linked to the fires, while Singapore Foreign Minister Vivian Balakrishnan said the two countries would coordinate their actions closely.

Wasisto described the task force as a “stepping stone” for both countries, particularly Indonesia, to address neighbouring countries’ concerns. But he said it was “still too early” to conclude that Jakarta’s approach had fundamentally changed, as the government’s commitment to follow-through remained uncertain.

“The protests may attract regional attention to Jakarta’s responses and, again, to Jakarta’s approach to regional cooperation, depending on whether national elites lower their nationalistic tone to achieve common ground,” he added.

Fahrizal was also sceptical that public pressure alone would change Prabowo’s position, given the emphasis on political, national and economic sovereignty within the ruling circle.

“This closed-off stance risks making regional cooperation more difficult,” he said, arguing that governments should open channels of communication to find common ground in tackling the haze.

The call for a stronger response to the haze comes amid reports of its severe health impact on the country. Indonesia’s Health Ministry has reported more than 333,000 cases of acute respiratory illness across seven affected provinces since mid-August.

Katherine Hasan, a researcher and analyst at the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air, welcomed the students’ action.

“The escalating air pollution from blazing forest and land fires in Kalimantan and Sumatra, alongside chronic pollution across Java, must serve as a wake-up call for all,” she said.

Clean air was a fundamental human right and the government had a duty to protect it, she added. Through the student protests, “we have a crucial window and opportunity right now to put citizens’ rights and public health back at the top of the national agenda — without compromise”.
