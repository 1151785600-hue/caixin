Syria’s US$216 billion rebuild offers a Hormuz alternative

SCMP | FREE | 1177 words | 2026-07-27

For years, Syria was a wound the world watched fester: a country carved up by war, sanctioned into isolation and reduced to a byword for state collapse.

Its economy needs US$216 billion to rebuild, according to World Bank estimates. This month, for the first time since the fall of the Assad regime in December 2024, it began to look like it might actually get there.

On July 8, the government of Ahmed al-Sharaa – a former jihadi commander turned president – established a supreme constitutional court. Four days later, it convened the new national parliament.

Together, these acts set in motion the process needed to build a post-Assad state, spurring Washington to announce on July 8 that it would lift its long-standing designation of Syria as a state sponsor of terrorism, following a third face-to-face meeting between Sharaa and US President Donald Trump on the sidelines of a Nato summit in Ankara.

That decision and the impending removal of associated US sanctions clear the way for Syria to rejoin the global financial system, without which the job of national reconstruction cannot begin in earnest.

After 14 years of civil war, the country’s infrastructure lies in ruins. Its institutions are embryonic and its politics still combustible. What it has, instead, is its location at the ancient crossroads of trade between Asia and Europe.

Two forces have revived interest in these long-dormant trade routes: the Strait of Hormuz crisis and a planned logistical corridor connecting the two continents via the Gulf states and Israel that stalled amid the political wreckage of the wars on Gaza and Iran.

Into that vacuum has stepped Syria, offering its eastern neighbour Iraq, the second-biggest oil exporter in Opec, a sorely needed outlet for its fuel.

On July 14, Damascus and Baghdad – with encouragement from Washington – struck a deal to rebuild a moribund pipeline connecting Iraq’s oilfield in Kirkuk to the Syrian port of Baniyas on its Mediterranean coast. US energy giant Chevron is set to lead the project, with mammoth multinational investment bank J.P. Morgan arranging the requisite financing.

Meanwhile, Iraq has signed a separate memorandum of understanding with Jordan to explore reviving a pipeline from Basra to the port of Aqaba, near the Red Sea. Combined, these projects could reroute practically all of Iraq’s oil exports and, in the words of US special envoy Tom Barrack, “make the Strait of Hormuz an afterthought”.

A third project – a rail corridor connecting Saudi Arabia’s Red Sea coast to Turkey via Jordan and Syria – is also in the works, following the signing of an agreement in June by Ankara and Riyadh.

“Syria has got a lot of problems, but its location isn’t one,” said Aron Lund, a senior analyst at the Swedish Defence Research Agency, calling the country “perfectly placed” to serve as a link between Europe, Turkey and the Gulf.

“But to capitalise on that geographic position, Syria needs a stable and welcoming political and legal climate, which is not the case today,” Lund said.

Get it right and the pay-off “could be hugely significant for Syria’s future”, he added. Get it wrong and “it’s far from certain” these long-term projects will ever be finished.

Caroline Rose, senior director of the New York-based Soufan Centre think tank, agreed. The flurry of pipeline and railway deals “definitely show that leaders and business stakeholders are seeing Syria as a serious alternative” to Hormuz, she said.

But it remains to be seen whether the current political momentum and push to diversify trade routes can be sustained.

“These projects will take many years to conduct,” Rose said. “It won’t be an immediate result, that’s definitely clear.”

A similar asterisk hangs over sanctions relief. Their lifting by the US removed some legal hurdles to trade and investment, said Omer Ozkizilcik, a senior Syria fellow at the Atlantic Council think tank, but “just like in any other post-conflict nation, many economic and administrative obstacles remain”.

‘Scared’ money, raw risk

Though analysts agree that the removal of Western sanctions is likely to accelerate reconstruction, it still “won’t be easy or rapid”, according to Bill Park, a senior lecturer in defence studies at King’s College London.

The scale of the destruction and dislocation means many of the roughly 4.5 million Syrians still living abroad – three-quarters of the 6 million who fled the war – are unlikely “to return in a hurry”, as Park put it.

“There will need to be confidence that Syria has stabilised, and maybe greater stability in the region,” he said.

It will also take time to convince Western financial institutions that Syria is a safe place to invest.

Despite J.P. Morgan’s decision to open a branch in Iraq to support the Baniyas pipeline, Rose said New York bankers she had spoken to were still “scared” of engaging with Syria’s new government.

Lenders have good reason to be circumspect. Trump continues to muse about the prospect of Syrian forces intervening in Lebanon to disarm Hezbollah, despite Sharaa’s denials that any such plan exists.

Israel, meanwhile, continues to occupy parts of southwestern Syria, seized amid the Assad regime’s collapse, having unilaterally declared the area a demilitarised zone – one policed by Druze separatists whom Israeli forces arm and protect, and who run what amounts to a mini-narco state there in cahoots with former Assad loyalists.

Trump’s Hezbollah idea took on new life on July 15, when he told Fox News that Sharaa could target the Iran-allied group “much more precisely” than Israel could – enabling Israel to redeploy its forces and focus on “the bigger issue, which is Iran”.

Israel rejected the notion a day later, but Damascus got the message. It has since announced three major seizures of Iran-supplied weapons, including drones and missiles, that were bound for Hezbollah in a bid to placate the Trump administration.

Tehran’s Revolutionary Guards responded by firing several missiles into the Syrian desert: a reminder of the country’s vulnerability to attacks by Iran’s proxies.

Damascus has tried to defuse the situation. Earlier this month, Foreign Minister Asaad al-Shaibani travelled to Beirut, offering reassurances of non-interference to both the government and parliamentary speaker Nabih Berri, a Shia community leader and political ally of Hezbollah.

The landmark visit yielded a border security pact aligning the two governments against the shared threats posed by Israeli occupation and Hezbollah, along with plans to revive business and trade ties and rebuild a railway line connecting Syria to the Lebanese port of Tripoli.

Lebanon. The Iraq pipeline deal. Even overtures to Assad’s former ally Russia, which is negotiating a deal to repurpose its former naval base at Tartus into an agricultural commodity trading hub – all point to Syria rebuilding relationships on every front and hedging against overreliance on any one patron.

As for Washington? Damascus knows it must “keep a very positive face” with the Trump administration, according to Rose – fully aware that this most improbable of relationships could “turn on a dime”.
