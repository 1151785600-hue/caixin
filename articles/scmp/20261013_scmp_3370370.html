How shadow banking feeds Myanmar while starving its military

SCMP | FREE | 1168 words | 2026-10-13

On a recent Friday night in a Bangkok dive bar, a group of young Myanmar exiles sang softly along to a mournful ballad played on an acoustic guitar.

None among the crowd had any plans to leave, either for their homeland or home for bed. They fled war, a broken economy and forced conscription into a military that seized power in the 2021 coup.

While their future in Thailand is uncertain, their ties to home are kept alive by the hard currency they send back each month to parents and siblings left behind in devastated village economies.

Aung*, a 31-year-old from the government-controlled Bago region, used to work at a state-owned bank. But since fleeing Myanmar he has relied on a shadow banking system to bypass state-sanctioned remittance channels that take a hefty cut.

That means most of the 3,000 to 5,000 baht (US$90-US$150) he sends home every month reaches his mother and sister.

“I only use the agent in my home village. The exchange rate is better and the military does not get the money,” he said.

Village-level agents are the front end of Myanmar’s “hundi” system, a network built on kinship, trust and community that can deliver money to remote hamlets within a day or two, even amid conflict.

“My agent is like my brother, I trust him 100 per cent,” Aung said.

Rules enacted in 2024 require migrant workers to send at least 25 per cent of their earnings home through Myanmar’s official banking channels, with non-compliance punishable by travel bans and passport confiscation.

Last year, such remittances totalled US$5.6 billion, or about 38 per cent of foreign inflows, according to non-public Finance Ministry data cited by Bloomberg.

That pales in comparison to the US$8 billion that Myanmar’s government estimated was remitted in 2015, though this did include money sent through both official and unofficial channels.

A decade ago, total remittances were thought to account for as much as 13 per cent of the country’s gross domestic product. It is not known what that percentage is today, but it is almost certainly higher given the millions based abroad sending money back home.

Foreign direct investment, by comparison, barely topped US$1 billion last year, by Myanmar state media’s own admission.

The government, desperate for foreign currency to pay for essential imports, sees remittances as a quick and reliable source of cash.

Money sent by relatives abroad is also a lifeline for those left behind in a nation where inflation spiked to nearly 25 per cent in June, according to the World Bank.

The kyat, Myanmar’s currency, has lost about three-quarters of its value against the US dollar since the coup, its worth battered by conflict, natural disasters and economic mismanagement.

Giant currency swaps

Money sent home by Myanmar’s migrant workers through the unofficial hundi system rarely, if ever, crosses the border as physical cash.

Instead, it operates more like a giant currency swap. In Thailand, small sums of baht collected from migrants are pooled in border towns like Mae Sot and Mae Sai to pay Thai exporters directly for goods. Myanmar importers then hand over the equivalent value in kyat to hundi agents, who distribute it to migrants’ families while charging a transaction fee of around 2 to 2.5 per cent.

In Myanmar, a country that has been at war with itself for generations, this informal system has been in operation, in one form or another, for decades. It now dwarfs official banking channels and works, by comparison, like clockwork. Customers get a better deal too, enjoying an exchange rate that can be twice as high as the official rate of around 2,100 kyat to the US dollar.

It works just as well for Myanmar nationals living and working in Malaysia.

Ju*, a migrant from Yangon, said the hundi system was as easy to use as walking into a shop in Kuala Lumpur’s Pudu district or elsewhere in the Klang Valley, with the money arriving fast, at a better rate and without the daily withdrawal limits imposed by conventional banks.

Some people “have been cheated before”, Ju conceded. “So usually, we test an unfamiliar agent by sending a smaller amount first. Usually it arrives within a day.”

Chasing foreign cash

In Naypyidaw, former military chief Min Aung Hlaing’s government would like some of that money.

The coup leader swapped his fatigues for the title of president in April and his administration – civilian in name but military in character – needs foreign currency to pay for imports.

Thailand, meanwhile, wants the influx of Myanmar migrants in the country, nearly all of whom work, to be regularised, moving millions from the shadow economy into one that is taxable, or at least more visible.

As many as 4.6 million Myanmar nationals were based in Thailand as of September last year, according to the International Organization for Migration – forming what Thai government-linked think tank the Institute for Strategic Policy called “the largest single migrant-worker corridor in Southeast Asia” in a report published last month.

Their remittances “are a critical source of household income and national foreign exchange for Myanmar,” the report said. “Yet the overwhelming majority of these flows continue to move through informal channels – principally the hundi system – rather than through banks or licensed money transfer operators.”

Some Thai banks have partnered with Myanmar digital payment platforms such as KBZPay and AYA Pay to offer cross-border transfers at rates that now compete with the black market.

But opening a Thai bank account requires a passport and other documents issued or notarised by the Myanmar embassy, creating an impossible hurdle for exiles who crossed the border illegally or fled conscription into the military.

Thai authorities would also like the legions of migrant workers from Myanmar to pay into social security or health schemes so they do not place a strain on public services intended for Thai citizens.

“The issue for the Thai side is we have to find a way to pay for their health, education and social facilities,” said Pana Janviroj, executive director of the Institute for Strategic Policy, suggesting that employers could start paying a small monthly contribution to welfare funds for legal employees.

In the meantime, some of those who did use the hundi system “may be charged more, some are taken advantage of”, Pana warned, adding that both governments had a security interest in monitoring cross-border money flows because “a lot of grey money goes into this system”.

None of this is persuading the exiles back at the bar in Bangkok. Mistrust of Myanmar’s military-backed government just runs too deep.

“If I register, the government will know about me,” said Mark*, using an anglicised nickname. “Then I will have to fight in the army or they can cause trouble for my family.”

He had a more fundamental objection as well. “I don’t want to give the government any money to buy more guns and bombs to use on us.”

*Name changed to protect interviewee’s identity
