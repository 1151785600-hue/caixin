How Japan’s innovative Nakagin Capsule Tower is finding new life

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1707 words | 2026-09-21

In 1988, upon moving to Tokyo, British architect Mark Dytham made a pilgrimage to Ginza to see one building he believed encapsulated everything he had imagined Japan to be: Nakagin Capsule Tower. “It was one of the first proper hi-tech buildings in the world, like a machine or a robot, and represented a vision where things don’t have to be static,” the co-founder of Klein Dytham architecture says. “I thought it was the future.”

So in 2022, it was with mixed feelings that Dytham stood on a street in the Japanese capital and watched the Metabolist building come down. After cranes gingerly hoisted capsules off the two interconnected towers that accommodated them, roughly one in five were loaded onto trucks destined for temporary shelters, where they would be rehabilitated and shipped to new homes.

Given that Metabolism was an architectural movement founded on the ability of buildings to move with the times, its radical poster child seemed to be exhibiting normal vital functions. Or was it metastasis? In the past two years, newly revitalised since the dismantling of their 50-year-old mother ship, Nakagin cells have spread throughout Japan and beyond. Hong Kong is part of that advance.

Nakagin’s scattered course would have pleased its avant-garde architect Kisho Kurokawa (1934-2007), who conceived the design to emancipate what he called Homo movens (mobile humans). Many fans still demonstrate their unbridled enthusiasm for the building by donning zany get-ups inspired by its form – often described as a stack of front-loading washing machines.

In my mind’s eye, however, Nakagin had always been a colony of barnacles. That analogy borrows from the free movement of these crustaceans before anchoring themselves in place. But unfortunately, it was the unexpected immobility of Kurokawa’s capsules during their first phase of life that ultimately thwarted his vision that they be modular, interchangeable and recyclable.

That idea of impermanent permanence remains irresistible, however – at least in Japan. Which was one reason Metabolism captured the imagination of many during the country’s buoyant post-war expansion, making its name and ethos heard at the 1960 World Design Conference in Tokyo. Adaptable buildings and cities – with renewable nodes fed by circulation systems – sparked interest. Adding excitement was the promise of Expo ’70, whose theme was “progress and harmony for mankind”.

At that landmark event in Osaka, futuristic pavilions, including several by Kurokawa, allowed the forward-thinking to see for themselves the biomimetic potential of a movement whose proponents included other eminent architects, Kenzo Tange and Fumihiko Maki among them.

Nakagin, however, earned the kind of cult status accorded few other buildings in Japan. One reason was the charisma of Kurokawa, also a media personality and, towards the end of his career, a politician. His building was among the few examples of Metabolist architecture ever realised.

In late 2022, Ikko Yokoyama visited a warehouse in Chiba, near Tokyo, where salvaged Nakagin capsules, resembling pet Cyclops, awaited adoption. As lead curator of design and architecture at M+, she was there to see one of 23 pods that had been saved by the Nakagin Capsule Tower Preservation and Restoration Project, founded by former resident Tatsuyuki Maeda.

Hong Kong’s museum of contemporary visual culture was prepared for this inevitable moment, according to Yokoyama. Debate had raged for years about whether to preserve Nakagin whole, with Kurokawa himself shifting from yes to no. Ultimately, land value outweighed legacy, says Dytham, who had tried without success to persuade developers and hospitality groups to turn Nakagin into a boutique hotel. “It wasn’t sustainable to keep it there,” he says.

The decision to tear it down was made despite its uniqueness. Unlike anything the West had produced – except, perhaps, in the minds of Britain’s Archigram group of architects – Nakagin consisted of capsules “plugged” into the building’s towers, or cores, one 11 storeys high, the other two floors taller. Tight spaces between each pod impeded access and meant that repairs were difficult because “you couldn’t get in”, says Dytham. Worse, owners were unwilling to pay the high cost of detaching their capsules for upkeep every 25 years, as Kurokawa had warned they must. Decay set in, leaks appeared and damage from Japan’s frequent earthquakes saw the building’s exterior netted for safety. Additionally, the presence of asbestos complicated matters.

But M+’s capsule speaks volumes about their enduring appeal. A806 now stands beside another of the museum’s inspired acquisitions, Kiyotomo (1988), the sushi bar designed by Shiro Kuramata. Unlike that 656 sq ft restaurant – which was disassembled in Japan, shipped to Hong Kong and later put together again by the original contractors and an M+ team of restorers – Kurokawa’s capsule was purchased fully reconditioned.

The 2.78 metre x 2.69 metre x 4.21 metre capsule in the East Galleries is one of six standard units, complete with their famous round window at one end accommodating a circular blind that folds open like a hand fan. Also included within the 121.9 sq ft space are mod cons and a dinky unit bath, those tiny waterproof rooms sold as a single product in Japan.

“Like organ donation”, as Yokoyama puts it, parts for the capsule were harvested from others in the building because none of the pods survived with all of its original fittings intact. Kiyotomo’s neighbour now also boasts a new blue carpet and mattress, in addition to a fold-down desk, television and reel-to-reel tape deck, among other integrated features. Combine the service and payload modules of a spaceship and you get the idea.

Of the capsules plucked to safety by Maeda’s group, 14 have been sold to companies and museums, including M+ and the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art, which purchased Kurokawa’s prized personal unit. Of the nine remaining, he says, four are to stay in Japan while five will head abroad.

Some have already been turned into display modules and mobile units and, next year, hotel-room conversions are expected, which should come as no surprise: in 1979, Kurokawa’s ideas also gave birth to the world’s first capsule hotel. New Japan Umeda continues to offer budget accommodation in Osaka.

Maeda’s is not the only group intent on giving the (in) famous corner building a second life. The Nakagin Capsule Tower A606 Project is named after the pod that another ex-resident helped restore several years before the building came down. This group, which owns seven units, has even more ambitious, albeit firmly parochial, aims.

“We plan to reconstruct a mini-tower using several capsules we own,” the group reveals via core member, architect Akiko Ishimaru.

“We aim to keep the reconstructed ones for 50 years [100 years after their completion] by continuing to use them ourselves. We want to utilise the capsules that were born in Japan and rebuild them in Japan.”

Crowdfunding has helped pay for the work so far. “The cost of asbestos control and investigation during the survey and dismantling, transportation and storage of seven capsules, storage of original parts, printing of a book with records, restoration of the interior of capsule A606, crowdfunding gifts [such as the aforementioned book], and fees consumed most of the total 8,673,000 yen [HK$443,000],” the group says.

Ishimaru’s efforts to keep Nakagin from history’s scrapheap underscores the passion in the project. But that’s not to say she fully enjoyed her year living in the building (and seven more working in two other capsules).

“One of my fond memories is drawing the actual survey plan for Nakagin Capsule Tower on my laptop at the original desk of Capsule A606 in August 2021,” she recalls, adding, “The 1.3-metre-diameter round window meant that the small room didn’t feel cramped.”

Having been designed as “hotel-like second homes” – or crash pads for salarymen – none of the capsules came with a kitchen. It was “very quiet”, Ishimaru recalls. The prefabricated steel modules didn’t share walls and, by 2013, when she moved in, there were only a few residents left. “Since it was so old and there was no hot water, I couldn’t even take a shower inside the capsule, so I stopped living there and used it as a shared office.”

Other assessments were bleaker. “From outside, the tower looked like a dying animal, sweating and greasy in the heat, trapped inside its wire netting,” reads an account by The Paris Review of Nakagin’s condition in 2016. “Looking up, you could see that some capsules had been half-filled with rotting garbage, a rippling line of trash drawn across their single windows. Inside, panels peel from the ceilings and mold crawls underneath; grime and seepage scorches the concrete with strange, bubbling forms.”

The reviewer added: “Kurokawa’s masterpiece was an utter, unsalvageable failure.”

And there’s the rub.

On whether Metabolism remains relevant in the 21st century, few can deny the importance of urban development that hews closely to principles of sustainability and flexibility. “The idea that buildings can change, grow and regenerate over time within a city may be increasingly needed to adapt to urban population growth and environmental issues,” the A606 group argue.

But how to label Kurokawa’s achievement remains as polarising as the fractious debates about whether the building should have been protected for posterity.

“It was an amazing success because he made it happen,” says Yokoyama, referring to how Kurokawa turned theory into reality and created a prototype. “It wasn’t just imagination.”

Dytham also believes Kurokawa got it “pretty right”, explaining that Nakagin was an early model. “If you’re building 10 of them, you get it. That was the first one.”

He adds that, for him, the building’s success had much to do with a design so simple that even a child could read and recognise it. “That would be a totally protected building if it was in the UK,” he posits. “Traditionally, here [in Japan], things are built in a temporary way.”

However, the recent wide dispersal of Nakagin capsules underscores the fact that Kurokawa’s vision has come to fruition. As Dytham says, “How many buildings have been taken apart and put all around the world?

“Taking [Nakagin] to the rest of the world is as important as just being all in one place.”

Metabolism was simply operating at its own pace.
