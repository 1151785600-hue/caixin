US sanctions Cuba’s military representative in Beijing over China procurement

SCMP | FREE | 1035 words | 2026-08-07

The United States sanctioned Cuba’s senior military representative in Beijing on Thursday, accusing him of helping procure military-related equipment from China for the island’s armed forces.

Waldo Perez Cortes, Cuba’s military, naval and air attache in Beijing, was among six Cuban nationals and five entities added to the US Treasury Department’s Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons list.

The US State Department said Perez Cortes was an official of Cuba’s Ministry of the Revolutionary Armed Forces, known as MINFAR, who had been “involved in the procurement of military-related equipment from China to Cuba”.

It did not identify the equipment, Chinese suppliers or transactions allegedly involved.

Cuban state news agency Prensa Latina has identified Perez Cortes as a senior colonel serving as Cuba’s military, naval and air attache in Beijing.

Perez Cortes has appeared publicly alongside senior officers from China’s People’s Liberation Army and spoken about Beijing’s support for Cuba’s defence capabilities.

At a reception in Beijing in November 2025, he said Cuba received help from several friendly countries, including China, as it worked to strengthen its defensive capacity, according to Prensa Latina.

Lieutenant General Wu Aimin and military, naval and air attaches accredited to China attended the event, the Cuban news agency reported.

The US State Department declined to make further comments beyond the official statement of the announcement. The South China Morning Post has also contacted the Chinese embassy in Washington for comment on the allegations and sanctions.

The sanctions package also targeted several Cuban officials and state enterprises accused of obtaining military equipment from China and Russia.

Among them was Heriberto Sanchez Alleyne, director general of the state-owned importer Tecnoimport. The State Department accused Sanchez of procuring military-related equipment and services from China and Russia.

Tecnoimport, a subsidiary of Cuba’s military-controlled Grupo de Administracion Empresarial SA, or GAESA, imported military equipment on behalf of MINFAR, according to the department. Washington alleged that the company had acquired military-related equipment from China and Russia.

Another sanctioned company, Duna SA, was accused of importing military-related equipment from the two countries.

Also sanctioned was Monica Milian Gomez, whom the State Department identified as Cuba’s military attache to Russia. The department accused her of involvement in transferring military-related equipment from Russia to Cuba.

Jose Antonio Remon Rodriguez, head of MINFAR’s Foreign Relations Directorate, was accused of involvement in transferring military-related equipment from China and Russia to Cuba.

Oscar Enrique Biosca Gallego, head of MINFAR’s Economic Directorate, allegedly participated in efforts to procure military-related equipment from both countries.

The other newly sanctioned officials included Roberto Jesus Viciana Mousset, director general of Cuba’s Military Industries Union, and Sanchez.

Actions taken under Trump order that broadened US sanctions powers

Defence Minister Alvaro Lopez Miera and Roberto Legra Sotolongo, chief of the general staff and first deputy minister of MINFAR, were also designated under the new Cuba-related sanctions authority. Both were already sanctioned under a separate US human-rights programme.

The State Department accused Lopez Miera of advancing Cuba’s foreign military cooperation and Legra of attempting to procure weapons systems from Russia.

The entities targeted included Cuba’s Military Industries Union, known by its Spanish initials UIM, and four companies involved in military equipment, technical products and trade: Tecnoimport, Tecnotex, Duna SA and the Yuri Gagarin Military Industrial Enterprise.

The State Department described UIM as a military holding company responsible for producing and repairing weapons and equipment for Cuba’s armed forces. It accused the company of working with Russian defence entities to modernise Russian-origin weapons systems.

The Yuri Gagarin enterprise was accused of maintaining and repairing Cuba’s Russian-made military aircraft, while Tecnotex allegedly took part in efforts to refurbish the country’s Russian-origin helicopter fleet.

The action was taken under an executive order signed by US President Donald Trump in May that broadened Washington’s authority to sanction foreign individuals and organisations associated with key sectors of Cuba’s economy or the Cuban government.

Perez Cortes and five other MINFAR officials were designated under a provision covering leaders and officials of the Cuban government or newly blocked entities.

Four of the companies were designated for operating in Cuba’s defence sector, while Tecnotex was targeted for acting on behalf of the Cuban government.

US accuses Cuba of being staging ground for China, Russia, Iran plans

US Secretary of State Marco Rubio accused Cuba of serving as a staging ground from which China, Russia and Iran could conduct operations against the United States.

“Today, the Trump administration is taking further action to protect our national security by sanctioning entities and individuals who facilitate Havana’s military relationships and the shipments of arms to the Cuban regime,” Rubio said.

Any property belonging to those designated that is in the United States or under the control of US persons must be frozen and reported to Treasury. American individuals and companies are generally prohibited from conducting transactions involving them.

Foreign individuals and financial institutions may also risk US sanctions if they transact with the designated parties or operate in specified sectors of Cuba’s economy, according to the State Department.

The sanctions come amid heightened scrutiny in Washington of Cuba’s security relationships with China and Russia.

A State Department report released last month alleged that three of 18 foreign-linked intelligence facilities in Cuba were operated by China, while other sites had connections to Russia. Beijing and Havana have repeatedly rejected US allegations about Chinese intelligence activity on the island.

The Trump administration has intensified economic pressure on Cuba this year, targeting senior officials, military-controlled businesses, banks, mining companies, energy suppliers and other state enterprises.

Washington says the campaign is intended to deny resources to Cuba’s political and military leadership. Havana has denounced the measures as economic coercion that worsens shortages and harms ordinary Cubans.

Beijing has repeatedly opposed Washington’s pressure campaign against Havana. China’s foreign ministry said last week that the US should stop its threats of force, unilateral sanctions and blockade against Cuba, adding that China would continue supporting the country in defending its sovereignty and opposing external interference.
