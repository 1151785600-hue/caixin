Fandom vs flag: why young Chinese embrace Japanese pop culture but reject war-era symbols

SCMP | FREE | 1210 words | 2026-10-26

Young Chinese people enjoy Japanese pop culture such as anime and music, but many draw a firm line at symbols of Japanese militarism.

That line was tested recently at Moscow State University, where Chinese students confronted a classmate wearing Japan’s rising sun flag in class and called for his repatriation, state-run newspaper Global Times reported on Sunday.

Many countries, including China, view the flag as a symbol of Japanese militarism and aggression. Japan’s Self-Defence Forces still use the flag today.

The October 14 incident sparked an online backlash against the Chinese student and praise for those who reported him, following commentaries by People’s Daily, the Communist Party’s official newspaper, and the People’s Liberation Army.

“A disgrace to the Chinese people,” read one of the top comments under a post by Jun Zhengping, a social media account run by the PLA.

Among those condemning the student were people who enjoyed Japanese pop culture, including Lucy Huang, 24.

An anime fan since primary school, she has watched more than 200 anime series and read widely across manga – Japanese comic books and graphic novels.

“Japan in anime cannot really be called Japan but is an idealised utopia,” Huang said, adding that she drew a clear distinction between Japan in reality and the country as depicted in anime.

She said it was “unforgivable” for a Chinese person to wear the rising sun flag.

Japanese anime began appearing on Chinese television in the 1980s and gradually gained a larger audience over the following decades.

According to a 2018 report by Chinese consultancy iResearch, China had more than 200 million online anime users in 2018, with nearly 80 per cent of 803 surveyed anime consumers expressing a preference for Japanese animation.

Yet, for many young Chinese like Huang, an interest in Japanese pop culture does not mean an embrace of Japan as a whole.

Lillian Liu, a final-year university student in China’s Fujian province, echoed Huang’s anger over symbols associated with Japan’s wartime aggression against China.

Liu boycotted a September collaboration between WEGO – a popular Japanese fashion brand known for its anime merchandise and clothes – and Japanese anime series My Hero Academia.

Chinese platforms dropped the anime in 2020 after a character linked to Japan’s Unit 731, which conducted human experiments during World War II, appeared in the series.

“I really like Japanese anime and manga, but as Chinese people, we cannot allow our country’s political stance to be violated,” said Liu, who began watching Japanese anime in kindergarten.

In a September commentary, Beijing Daily, the official newspaper of the Communist Party’s Beijing Municipal Committee, described the collaboration as “an act of support for Japanese militarism”.

“This is shameful. I won’t buy anything from the store again,” said Liu, who estimated spending up to 20,000 yuan (US$2,995) a year on Japanese pop culture merchandise and had bought three transparent bags showing anime merchandise from WEGO before the incident.

WEGO Harajuku, the brand’s China subsidiary, later issued an apology, saying its Japanese parent company had cancelled the collaboration and removed related promotional posts.

But Yuki, 24, an anime fan who gave only her first name, said she had noticed a shift in how young people around her viewed Japan.

“The spread of anime has, to some extent, helped young people today avoid viewing all Japanese people as bad,” Yuki said.

Once a fan of My Hero Academia, Yuki stopped following the series after the 2020 controversy.

“Culture itself is also a form of politics. After the incident, I no longer engage with [My Hero Academia],” said Yuki, who regularly attends anime conventions where fans dress up as characters from animation and manga.

She has also seen how political tensions can affect the anime scene.

“The popularity of Japanese anime seems to rise and fall with China-Japan relations and political tensions,” she said, observing that a convention organiser had removed booths related to Japan after Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi’s contentious remarks on Taiwan.

In November last year, Takaichi told Japan’s parliament that a Taiwan contingency could trigger the deployment of the Self-Defence Forces, eliciting heavy criticism from mainland Chinese officials and state media.

Beijing sees Taiwan as part of China to be reunited by force if necessary. Most countries, including the United States and Japan, do not recognise Taiwan as an independent state, but Washington is opposed to any attempt to take the self-governed island by force and is committed to supplying it with weapons.

China and Japan have long had a complex and evolving relationship, shaped by more than 1,000 years of exchanges in writing, religion, art and technology.

During several dynasties including the Tang, Japan sent envoys to China to study its political system, Buddhism, written culture and urban planning. The island country drew heavily on Chinese civilisation in shaping its own institutions and culture.

But Japan’s industrialisation and military expansion in the late 19th century led to war with China. Japan defeated the Qing dynasty in the First Sino-Japanese War of 1894-95, forcing it to cede lands including Taiwan.

After Japan’s 1945 surrender at the end of World War II, China and Japan moved towards diplomatic relations, normalising them in 1972. Since then, ties have improved at times, only to be repeatedly strained by historical and territorial disputes.

Orna Naftali, an associate professor in the Department of Asian Studies at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, said both recent geopolitical tensions and historical factors had contributed to hostility towards Japan in China.

“Every now and then, there is some kind of geopolitical tension about different issues, which is because of reasons including history,” Naftali said.

As for popular culture, she thought it did not necessarily lead people to “develop more positive attitudes” towards a country’s government or its policies.

Naftali said young people’s views of Japan were shaped by domestic influences and interactions with the country’s people and family members.

“What to think of a certain country [is shaped] not just through textbooks but also different rituals and ceremonies and whatever the teacher says,” she added.

Naftali observed that relatives’ memories of the war, whether young people had in-depth conversations with Japanese people, and their socioeconomic background also played an important role in shaping their understanding of the country.

Although “there is a constant sort of animosity towards Japan in terms of the nationalist rhetoric”, young people’s views were more complex, she said, adding that some held more favourable attitudes towards Japan, while even those with negative views had nuanced perspectives.

Shin Kawashima, professor in the University of Tokyo’s Department of International Relations, said young people in both China and Japan had shown considerable interest in one another’s cultures.

“In that sense, in the distant future, as the current younger generations come into contact with one another, Japan-China relations may take on a form different from what they are today,” he said.

However, Kawashima voiced pessimism about the prospects for building stable, positive ties between Chinese and Japanese societies through youth exchanges, noting that the “Chinese government has currently suspended most official exchanges with Japan”.

“At present, the influence of the political sphere on the economy and society is increasing, and such possibilities are not expected to expand,” he said.
