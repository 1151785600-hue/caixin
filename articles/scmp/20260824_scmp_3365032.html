Brazil presidential debate zeroes in on Chinese investment and US tariffs

SCMP | FREE | 1280 words | 2026-08-24

Brazil’s presidential candidates opened the televised campaign season on Sunday with a debate that turned repeatedly to the country’s position between Washington and Beijing.

The exchange comes in an election cycle already shaped by American tariffs, a visa dispute over election monitoring and the Lula government’s warming ties with China.

Ronaldo Caiado, Augusto Cury and Renan Santos took to the debate stage in Sao Paulo but the lecterns reserved for President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva, Senator Flavio Bolsonaro and former Minas Gerais governor Romeu Zema remained empty.

Lula and Flavio Bolsonaro, who are leading in every published poll, refused to take part, with the Brazilian president telling a podcast in July he would skip debates if the exercise meant listening to nonsense.

Flavio Bolsonaro has tied his attendance to Lula’s, and Zema withdrew hours beforehand, saying the event had “lost its purpose”.

A senator for Rio de Janeiro and the eldest son of Jair Bolsonaro, Flavio became a candidate in December after being named by his father as his heir. Jair was sentenced to 27 years in jail for plotting a coup and masterminding a plan to assassinate Lula after losing his re-election bid in 2022.

The Band network has opened the televised campaign in every presidential race since 1989, the first direct vote after two decades of military rule. Broadcasters treat the event as the moment that sets the tone for the season.

US-China issue

Foreign policy rarely takes up much room in Brazilian presidential debates, which tend to be dominated by security, jobs and inflation. The absence of the two front-runners left the others time for subjects that usually stay at the margins.

Washington imposed a 25 per cent tariff on most Brazilian goods on July 22, capping a Section 301 case that ran for a year. It covered digital payment rules, ethanol market access and illegal deforestation. A separate forced labour investigation proposes a further 12.5 per cent duty.

The trade dispute has run alongside an argument over the vote itself, which Brasilia treats as a matter of interference.

Last month, Brazil denied visas to two US State Department officials who planned to question electoral authorities. And this month, Lula said he would withhold agreement for US President Donald Trump’s nominee as ambassador until the vote was over.

Meanwhile, Beijing has moved in the other direction, offering Brasilia support as Washington escalated.

President Xi Jinping telephoned Lula on July 26, four days after the tariffs took effect, and said China opposed external interference. The two governments agreed to widen cooperation on artificial intelligence, satellites, critical mineral processing and fertiliser trade.

In the debate studio, a radio journalist asked Augusto Cury how he would manage Washington during the second Trump term. The psychiatrist and author, who is running for Avante, argued that the tariffs answered American fiscal pressure rather than any dispute with Brazil.

“We cannot be subservient, put 25 per cent, plus 12.5, 37.5 per cent, and damage our export list,” he said. Cury said he would foster equally good relations with China while pushing Brazil out of its raw material role.

Caiado, the former Goias governor running for the PSD, or Social Democratic Party, attributed friction with the Trump administration to Lula’s electoral calculations.

“Lula is desperate to create this impasse because he saw the presidents of Canada and Australia winning, so he provokes Trump all the time,” he said.

Caiado said the foreign ministry had become a mouthpiece for the governing Workers’ Party. He promised to restore its ability to negotiate for a country he said retained leverage in energy, grain and fresh water.

However, it was Renan Santos who drew more attention than his two rivals on stage, despite never having held elected office.

The 42-year-old co-founded the Free Brazil Movement that organised the protests that preceded former president Dilma Rousseff’s impeachment in 2016. Polling has kept him third for most of the year – at between 5 and 9 per cent – but an AtlasIntel survey in July put him first among Brazilians aged 16 to 24, with 38 per cent.

Supporters and opponents call him “the Brazilian Bukele”, a label he encourages by citing controversial Salvadorean president Nayib Bukele – the self-proclaimed “coolest dictator in the world” – when talking about his war on crime.

Like Bukele, Santos’ platform pairs mass incarceration and militarised policing with wide deregulation of the economy.

On arriving at the studios, Santos was asked how he would handle warming between Lula and Trump as well as their divergence over Chinese investment.

“It seems that the sovereignty debate of Lula and Flavio involves choosing who your guardian will be,” he said. “One wants to choose China, the other wants to choose the United States. And that is not what makes a nation sovereign.”

Inside the studio he pressed the same charge against the governing party in language aimed at its trade record rather than its diplomacy.

“Right now we look at the Workers’ Party [Lula’s party] project wanting to put Brazil on its knees before China, without training a qualified workforce,” he said. “What matters to Lulism is that we keep buying the finished product from China while we sell ore or sell our grain.”

Kim Kataguiri, a federal deputy and one of Santos’s main campaign coordinators, said the position amounted to pragmatism rather than an alignment of any kind.

“He means that Lula, in exchange for nothing or for very little, automatically aligns with China,” Kataguiri told the South China Morning Post at the studios.

Kataguiri drew a sharper line on the other side, separating the American state from the administration that currently runs it.

“With Flavio, it is not even in relation to the United States, it is specifically in relation to the Trump government,” he said.

“It is not an alignment with the nation, with the American state, but with a figure, and a figure who, by all indications, will have difficulty electing a successor.”

He said Brazil should set terms for both the US and China instead of picking one, starting with what Beijing brings to the table.

“China wants space inside Brazil, so let it offer investment, let it offer technology, let it offer defence partnership,” he said, naming AI and rare earth processing, sectors he said Beijing led Washington, and added that Brazil could import that technology through strategic partnerships.

The same demand applied to the Americans, which he said had a shrinking commercial presence in Brazil.

“They do not want us to make more trade partnerships with China, so let them offer more partnership,” Kataguiri said. “American investment in Brazil only falls. The United States took Brazil as a given for the past 20 years and neglected our relations.”

Lula tells Trump real target is China

While the three candidates argued in the Sao Paulo debate, Record TV aired an exclusive interview with Lula recorded that morning at his official residence in Brasilia.

Presenter Mariana Godoy asked what had been settled with Trump on two subjects: the tariffs on Brazilian goods and cooperation against organised crime.

Lula described a call with the US president on Friday that ran about 80 minutes in which he reportedly told Trump the grounds for the new sanctions were false, citing record low deforestation and Lula’s own history of fighting forced labour, then placed the pressure elsewhere.

“Underneath it all, what he was really getting at was China,” he said.

Lula said he offered Trump a joint task force on critical minerals and rare earths, with one condition.

“Only this time we are not going to be an exporter of raw material. We want to transform it here, to make mobile phones, to make electric batteries, to make chips,” the president contended.
