First Look: Yurt, Hong Kong’s ‘first modern Central Asian restaurant’

SCMP | FREE | 1033 words | 2026-10-31

While Hongkongers scout remote destinations for their first holiday in 2026, a journey of discovery for the palate is now on offer closer to home. Yurt, newly opened on SoHo’s Elgin Street, is bringing the hitherto unexplored flavours of Central Asia to the heart of the city, proving you don’t need a boarding pass to be an adventurous gourmand.

In a dining scene that prides itself on diversity, Central Asia has remained conspicuously absent from the many menus on offer in Hong Kong. For the founders behind Yurt, filling that gap has been a project a long time in the making.

“This idea has been in the back of all our minds for years, even before we met,” they share. The catalyst was when Xeniya Tregubenko was introduced to fellow Kazakh native Ali Nuraly through a mutual friend early last year. What started as a casual chat quickly touched on a common dream – to introduce Central Asian flavours to Hong Kong.

For both, Yurt marks a distinctive new life chapter. For Tregubenko, a seven-year Hong Kong resident, the restaurant is about “putting down permanent roots” for her culture in her adopted city. For Nuraly, a more recent arrival, it signals “the exciting beginning of my entrepreneurial journey”.

Although the pair were confident in the appeal of their plan, Nuraly admits that taking the first concrete step towards opening Yurt was daunting. “Introducing a completely unknown cuisine … it was a leap of faith,” he says.

The greater challenge, however, was one of cultural translation. “It’s one thing to cook the dishes; it’s another to bring the spirit,” Nuraly explains.

The man tasked with helping achieve this goal is head chef Marat Zakaryayev, another native of Kazakhstan whose culinary experience runs from Almaty to London. “My goal is for the first-time taster to understand our homeland through its flavours,” he says.

Completing this culinary bridge is pastry chef Anna Lobanova. Formerly pastry chef at Whey, she now works from her base in Kazakhstan to create desserts that are authentically Central Asian yet designed to resonate locally.

Diners walking into Yurt get an immediate sense of Central Asia with a warm, cosy dining room featuring atmospheric decor and lighting along with objects from the region. As Tregubenko explains, it is filled with gifts and mementos brought by members of the city’s Central Asian community who have come to support the space. The result is an atmosphere that feels deeply personal – less like the opening of a commercial venture and more like the founding of a communal home.

The opening menu offers a concise, narrative-driven tour of the steppes through dishes from Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Uzbekistan. A meal begins with baursak – the essential welcome of home-made, fluffy fried dough. Its name translates to “striving for unity”, and it’s a staple of the dastarkhan or traditional banquet table in Kazakh and Kyrgyz culture.

Zakaryayev is keenly aware of the differences in topography between Hong Kong and Central Asia: “One shaped by the sea and rivers, the other by mountains and endless steppe.”

The head chef has embraced this opportunity to work with ingredients that would be less familiar back home – even if featuring seafood on the menu does not come naturally. One thoughtful appetiser of sea bass with pumpkin and mung bean purée, for example, incorporates local ingredients while remaining rooted in Central Asian spices and palate.

Mains present the pillars of Central Asian cuisine, starting with beshbarmak. Described as “the dish of celebration”, it features hand-pulled noodles and tender meat in a soulful broth. It is joined by pilaf, a fragrant rice dish with beef, raisins and quail eggs that serves as a flavourful ode to Uzbekistan.

The final act is a dessert that bridges the vastness of Asia, balancing the sweet-tart flavour of Kazakhstan’s famed Aport apple against a rich, umami-forward home-made cheesecake and cheese caramel, thoughtfully adapted to a “sweet but not too sweet” profile suitable for local palates.

Yurt’s founders have taken to publicising the concept as the “first modern Central Asian restaurant” in Hong Kong – and they are aware of the responsibility that comes with claiming such a mantle. “We’re the first chapter of this cuisine for many, and we take that very seriously,” says Tregubenko.

The team’s determination to present an authentic version of their native cuisine is what led them to include horse meat on the menu – a traditional and respected protein across the steppe. The founders initially grappled with the decision, aware of the potential for misunderstanding. Ultimately, they chose cultural integrity over compromise, offering it as an add-on sausage served with their signature beshbarmak.

“We felt like if we shied away, if we didn’t showcase this one key element of our cuisine, we’d be watering down our own culture,” Tregubenko explains. “It’s an act of cultural honesty and integrity for us.”

That same commitment has led Nuraly and Tregubenko to operate Yurt as a halal establishment, positioning it as one of Hong Kong’s few refined halal dining spots. That commitment extends beyond the preparation of the food to the drinks menu as well. They “focus on crafting exceptional non-alcoholic pairings”, ensuring the experience remains celebratory and inclusive, even if it can be a “challenge” to explain to certain guests why they don’t serve alcohol.

An initial positive response suggests a healthy appetite for this previously unfamiliar cuisine. Nuraly says they’ve seen “overwhelming support” from the local Central Asian community, who have been introducing friends and inquisitive local explorers. Their target audience, he says, is simply “the curious”.

Looking forward, the founders hope Yurt will evolve into a broader cultural hub – a “living bridge” for music and art from the region. For now, they’ve built a different kind of destination. As they put it: “If the yurt in the steppe is a movable home, then Yurt the restaurant is a home that found its place.”

It’s a home that invites Hong Kong’s ever-curious diners to pull up a chair and embark on a journey to Central Asia, no passport required.
