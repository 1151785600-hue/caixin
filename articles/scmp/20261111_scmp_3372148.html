Ellora and Ajanta: the history and mystery of India’s ancient cave-temples

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1540 words | 2026-11-11

The view of the Kailasa Temple from above takes my breath away – and I am not referring just to the huffing and puffing of the climb to the top of the hillock that overlooks this 8th century marvel.

Its sheer scale and significance seen from on high make me literally gasp in wonder. In an archaeological complex strewn with ancient Buddhist, Jain and Hindu temples, this one – dedicated to the Hindu destroyer god, Shiva – is the show-stopper.

It’s the middle of the monsoon season in central India and I have plodded along the rough stone pathways elsewhere at the Ellora Caves complex in incessant, if mild, morning showers. But any discomfort is forgotten when I look down upon this 1,200-year-old temple that was carved top-down out of solid basalt rock.

That’s worth repeating; this temple – with its central shrine, multi-storey gateway, assembly halls, free-standing pillars, life-size elephant statues and surrounding side galleries – was not built from the ground up, but hewn into shape from the top, slowly, painstakingly over the course of many years. It is estimated that more than 200,000 tonnes of rock were chiselled by hand to create the basic shapes, to which carvings and flourishes were later added. Measuring 30 metres in height, 90 metres in length and 53 metres in width, this is the world’s largest monolithic structure.

“How was this possible in the 8th century,” asks guide Vilas Mitkar, giving voice to the question uppermost in my mind. How did those Rashtrakuta artisans have access to such sophisticated theoretical and technical skills? And where did all the excavated rock and rubble go?

I hired this licensed guide at the main gate of the caves complex in the hope of learning more about Ellora’s history but, in this case, I am to be disappointed, because even archaeologists and historians have been unable to solve the puzzle of how this particular masterpiece was built.

Mitkar makes up for his lack of rational explanations with a plethora of far-fetched theories that have been repeated and embellished over the centuries. The one alleging the temple was constructed by aliens is particularly bizarre. There again, looking at the temple walls glistening in the weak post-rain sunshine, it is easy to believe that superhuman, if not actual otherworldly, hands were involved in their creation.

Within the temple, Mitkar stops to point out the splendour of the statues of assorted divine beings lining the corridors and edges of the pavilions, and the elaborate details in the carvings along the inner walls of the main sanctum. Depicted are scenes from the Hindu epics Ramayana and Mahabharata; flying angels and warring demons; and myriad incarnations of popular gods and goddesses. One could spend days deciphering these stories in stone.

Hundreds of such cave-temple complexes – most of them Buddhist – exist in the central Indian state of Maharashtra, having been built centuries ago to serve as both centres of worship for resident monks and places of rest for travellers. Many are in ruins or inaccessible to tourists, but the Karla and Bhaja caves, just off the highway linking the cities of Mumbai and Pune, have been fairly well preserved. And even within the urban chaos of Mumbai, there are the neglected Kanheri Caves, deep inside Sanjay Gandhi National Park, with similar architecture and iconography to those in the more significant sites.

Of these, the Ellora complex, roughly 320km from Mumbai and spread along 2km of a high escarpment, is one of the largest, with shrines dug directly into the basalt rock. And while the word “cave” may bring to mind dank and musty subterranean formations, these are massive, true to the etymology of the word “cavernous”.

Of the original 100 excavated between AD600 and 1000, only 34 of Ellora’s caves are open to the public, and it takes an entire morning to do just a cursory round of the most significant ones. Along the way, Mitkar generously colours his narrative with stories of hidden trapdoors and underground cities; he points to carved figurines of “extraterrestrial beings” on the walls. Many of Ellora’s pillars depict the yali: a mythical creature with the face of an elephant and the body of a lion – the epitome of strength and bravery.

Its Unesco World Heritage listing declares that Ellora “brings the civilization of ancient India to life”. While that is true, what is more significant, especially in today’s climate of frenzied communalism in India, is this statement: “Not only is the Ellora complex a unique artistic creation and a technological exploit but, with its sanctuaries devoted to Buddhism, Hinduism and Jainism, it illustrates the spirit of tolerance that was characteristic of ancient India.”

Tourists who have been quick to whip out selfie sticks and wisecracks fall into a respectful silence at Cave No 10 (also known as Vishwakarma Cave), a Buddhist chaitya, or prayer hall, with an imposing Buddha statue dominating its central altar. With a high domed roof and a wood-like polish to its rock pillars, this cave feels sparse and severe, but evokes the same sense of awe as the Kailasa Temple.

It’s a mystery how so much of Ellora has managed to withstand the ravages of time, and indeed, the plundering invaders who desecrated other monuments of the age, particularly those with a religious slant. It is known, however, how Ajanta – another cave complex, located a two-hour drive away and often mentioned in the same breath as Ellora – pulled it off.

At Ajanta, guide Naseeb Khan begins his tour not at the entrance to the caves but on a hilltop a few hundred metres away, from where the complex is visible in its entirety, sprawled along a horseshoe-shaped vertical cliff and concealed – as if by design – by a lush emerald forest.

It is believed the oldest of these 30 caves (although some sources say there are only 29) dates back to the 2nd century BC, with a second phase of building having taken place between AD480 and AD600. Not too long afterwards, Buddhism began to decline in popularity in the region, and the caves were eventually abandoned.

Ajanta simply slipped from view and remained hidden for more than 1,200 years – until 1819, when John Smith, a British Army officer hunting in the area, chanced upon the caves. One can only wonder at his amazement at finding these Buddhist monuments hewn into sheer rock.

We follow an easy downhill trail to the caves, passing seasonal waterfalls and panoramas.

Unlike at Ellora, which is under an hour’s drive from the nearest big city, Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar (named Aurangabad until 2023), we have Ajanta’s caves almost to ourselves, save for a few other domestic tourists.

Despite having been shielded from human interference for such a long time, the older Ajanta is in an overall more fragile state than Ellora, its cliff-edge, forested location having taken a toll. The damage is most evident in the erosion of the wall and ceiling frescoes, which were created with natural dyes and are considered the highlight of the complex. The few much-needed preservation measures in place include a ban on shoes and flash photography inside the caves, and a limit on the number of visitors at a time.

Walking barefoot on the cold stone floors seems appropriate and once my eyes get used to the darkness, I find it easy to trace the lines of the floral, geometric and even human shapes that adorn the walls. Themes include stories from the Jataka tales – ancient Buddhist literature conveying lessons and guidelines for ethical living; snippets from Buddha’s life; animals in conversation with humans; divine beings in silks and gold; and lithe creepers and intricate flowers. In one of the caves, Khan flashes a slim torchlight upon depictions of traders with Central Asian and African features, which attest to this region’s importance in a flourishing network of trade and commerce.

The most arresting of Ajanta’s frescoes is to be found in a crowded cluster in Cave No 1 and depicts Avalokiteshvara (called Guanyin in China) as the Bodhisattva Padmapani, or “Lotus Bearer”. The colours are faded, the paint is peeling, but my breath slows as I stand in the darkness, taking in the finer details of this 5th century painting of the bodhisattva of compassion.

Caves are denoted as either prayer hall or monastery (vihara), with the latter possessing the more advanced carvings and paintings. This is not surprising given that, at Ajanta’s peak, there were hundreds of Buddhist monks living and worshipping in these stone quarters. They enjoyed royal patronage, which in part allowed them to create art in such profusion.

“Painting was perhaps a form of meditation,” Khan muses. After all, Buddhist monks to this day engage in the act of creating delicate mandala paintings with coloured sand or rice flour. Once finished, the mandala is wiped out, to reinforce the idea that life is impermanent.

In the case of Ajanta, it is time that has been slowly destroying the art – another reminder that nothing lasts forever. There have been occasional rumblings about the need to close these caves to visitors to preserve them. But for now, I am grateful for this fleeting monsoon morning in the presence of such magnificence.
