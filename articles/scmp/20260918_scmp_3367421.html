Ode to the horse: 5,000 years of human-equine connection on display in new Kazakh museum

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1932 words | 2026-09-19

On the scenic drive east from Almaty, in Kazakhstan, along the old corridor of the A351 highway, towards the Charyn Canyon National Park, and the Xinjiang border beyond that, sits an unassuming museum run by an unlikely duo.

Nurbol Baimukhanov, 50, is an independent, published researcher of shezhire – traditional Kazakh genealogies, historically memorised and passed down through generations – and an award-winning documentary producer who speaks in equal parts archaeology and mysticism. His son Ilyas, 23, with a MacBook tucked under his arm and an eye for systems, is pursuing a master’s in management at Satbayev University, the nation’s oldest technical institute.

On a quiet stretch of the A351, father and son have built a showcase dedicated to the ancient Botai, the multi-hyphenate people of the Eurasian steppes – hunter-gatherer-herder-settlers – who some archaeologists believe were among the first to domesticate the horse more than 5,000 years ago.

Located around 70km from Almaty, the Ush Qiyan Open-Air Museum sits near the village of Akshiy, in the Enbekshikazakh district – the kind of place most travellers pass without noticing.

Opened this summer, it rises from the flat landscape as three circular log dwellings with sloping roofs – constructed from more than 1,000 logs, the exhibition brochure proudly proclaims – and is designed to evoke what some researchers believe are among the oldest known permanent settlements of the steppes.

Ush Qiyan receives no government funds, has launched no grand marketing campaign – it is just a father, his son and their semi-domesticated horse named Ayan grazing outside.

“Kazakh culture is, first and foremost, the culture of the horse,” says Nurbol. “In gratitude to these beautiful animals, we built this museum as a temple of knowledge to enlighten people.”

Before there were empires and criss-crossing trade routes, before valiant steeds carried armies across continents and monks towards enlightenment, there was a culture that first looked at a wild horse and saw something more than prey.

It is believed that the Botai people lived in what is now northern Kazakhstan, in the 4th and 3rd millennia BC. Mighty they were not.

As far as archaeologists could detect, the Botai built no cities, wrote no epics and erected no monuments that would survive the centuries and catch the eye. Instead, what they left behind were traces of dwellings, scattered pottery and the bones of horses – thousands upon thousands of them, now housed at the North Kazakhstan Regional Museum of History and Local Lore, in Petropavl.

Archaeologists around the world now widely believe that the Botai were the first to domesticate the horse, though the claim is debated each time new evidence comes to light. Experts argue that the proof is in the bones, which show signs of bit wear on the teeth, from the bridle gear used to control them, and in the residue of mare’s milk found in their pottery, leading them to theorise that the Botai were not just hunting horses, but riding them, milking them and living alongside them. If true, it would be one of the most consequential partnerships in human history. Migration, trade, warfare, the spread of ideas and empires – all of it would eventually travel on horseback.

And yet, there is a twist. Genetic research suggests that the animal the Botai domesticated is actually the ancestor of the Przewalski’s horse – the stocky, pot-bellied wild horse that still roams parts of Central Asia. In other words, the first horse believed to be part of human culture eventually returned to the wild, rather than develop into the modern steeds we see today.

Ush Qiyan attempts to capture this paradox – the last wild-horse species being descended from domesticated ancestors, raising questions around whether the breed is truly wild or simply feral – telling a complicated story about a partnership that was formed, dissolved and formed again, as the modern horse, tamed later in the Early Bronze Age, became the engine of human history.

It all started with Ayan, a stallion who seems to straddle both worlds. In the spring of 2021, Nurbol went looking for a light-hued horse in the Altai mountain range, in eastern Kazakhstan. He had no particular reason for doing so; he says he simply, suddenly, felt like having one.

A friend had told him about a remote village with no internet and a horse bearing a rare isabelline colour. Having negotiated a price, it took Nurbol three nights by truck to take Ayan back to Almaty.

“He was born wild, near Lake Zaysan,” says Nurbol.

Ayan is, according to Nurbol, a Zhabe – one of Kazakhstan’s oldest horse breeds, distinguished by its stout frame, thick neck, muscular croup and substantial body weight. But with a pale, almost metallic coat that glows golden in sunlight, Ayan is no ordinary Zhabe.

Whether the isabelline colouring is genetically possible for this breed is another matter, but Nurbol is not a man to let science get in the way of a good story.

“It was a sign from above,” he says, referencing the heavens. “Our horse bears all the hallmarks of an ancient celestial horse. It has a powerful mind and transmits information telepathically.”

Separating fact from fiction is a secondary concern for Nurbol.

He is a researcher whose published works focus on history, archaeology and ancestry. At the same time, he is a believer who sees little boundary between myth and reality. In his telling, Ayan the horse is both a living animal and the latest chapter in a legend that began with the Botai people and culture five millennia ago.

“In the Chinese epic Journey to the West, the White Dragon Horse came from the West,” says Nurbol, noting that, in the text, “the tale of the monk [Tang Sanzang] who met a White Dragon Horse happened somewhere in our area. [Kazakhstan] is a land of legends where myth and reality intertwine”.

Ayan eventually inspired the construction of Ush Qiyan, which began in the summer of 2024. It took about two years to finish, building only in the warmer months, with a small crew, and involved no architects or contractors in the conventional sense – just workers stripping bark from logs under a punishing sun.

“Construction was not easy,” Nurbol recalls. “It was very hot. People were breaking down like matchsticks.”

All the logs had to be processed and cut by hand, following what Nurbol dubs “experimental archaeology” – a scientific approach to understanding how ancient nomads built their dwellings, and the physical labour involved. It was, in his words, an attempt to recreate not just the look of the Botai world, but the toll it took.

Money for the project came entirely from the Baimukhanovs, and though that choice was deliberate, it had consequences. Nurbol remembers days when, due to a lack of funds, “we were idle and wondering how to raise money to continue construction. [But] this was our conscious choice, and we are pleased with the result”.

Ilyas was born in Almaty and grew up tagging along on his father’s scientific expeditions across Kazakhstan. Ush Qiyan, in some ways, is the culmination of a childhood spent among artefacts and open landscapes. Now in his early adulthood, Ilyas speaks with the composure of someone a decade older.

“My father and I are like-minded people,” he says. “I represent the younger generation, a new perspective.” While Nurbol supplies the vision, the younger Baimukhanov thinks about sustainability, management systems and visitor services.

His own “eureka!” moment came years earlier, in the Botai region of northern Kazakhstan, where he met the influential archaeologist Viktor Fedorovich Zaibert, who discovered, excavated and opened the Botai settlement site in 1980, in the Aiyrtau district. In doing so, Zaibert gave the world some of the earliest evidence for horse husbandry and domestication. “He [was] an amazing person,” Ilyas recalls. “He told fascinating stories about history.”

What struck him were the traces of ancient circles still visible in the earth – the outlines of dwellings long gone – and the bones and pottery scattered across the ground, some more than 5,000 years old.

“I am happy to have interacted with such a great archaeologist and seen the artefacts in person. It’s unforgettable,” says Ilyas. Zaibert died in 2022, but his influence, legacy and memory live on in the log walls of the museum.

A visitor to Ush Qiyan moves through the three log structures, each roof shaped like an inverted basket, echoing the ancient yurts of the early nomads. Inside, scientific replicas of nomadic dress from various periods, made from materials such as hide, bone and leather, created for the 2018 documentary Equus: Story of the Horse, which Nurbol co-produced, hang alongside other props. Installations showing reconstructions of historical dwellings and horse sculptures fill the spaces between, and films are screened for guests who want to linger.

In another room, Daniyar Sarbassov, a 36-year-old artist from the Bayanaul district, showcases his work: castings of horses, mostly.

“Daniyar is our friend,” says Nurbol. “He’s a talented sculptor with great potential.” But the connection to the museum runs deeper than friendship. Sarbassov’s horses are not decorative – they carry, as Nurbol puts it, “the code of the Great Steppe”.

One of his pieces, an aluminium-and-bronze statue titled Zhuyrik (2022), was sculpted earlier, in his studio, when Sarbassov had no knowledge of Ayan, or the world Nurbol was building. But when he spotted the ivory-coloured horse, something shifted. “After seeing Ayan, I decided that this work would be better placed at the entrance of the museum,” he says.

In Sarbassov’s description, Zhuyrik is not simply a horse running.

It is “a creature that merges with the wind and moves together with the air”. Its smooth surface catches the natural light from the structure’s oculus as it shifts throughout the day, seemingly dissolving the boundary between the horse’s body and the surrounding space.

“A truly swift horse is difficult to perceive in its entirety,” he says. “Its speed is often experienced through the impression, trace and sensation of movement left in our memory.”

As its metallic shine echoes Ayan’s coat, Sarbassov calls it “a spiritual symbol of the museum”. He hopes it will remain there permanently, and that visitors will find their own meaning in the work: “One viewer may see freedom in the image of a horse, another may be reminded of their ancestors, while a third may discover an entirely different meaning. My hope is that they will pause for a moment to reflect on their own history, their homeland and their place as human beings within this vast expanse of time.”

Outside, Ayan grazes, unaware of the symbolism attached to him. Nurbol considers him the living echo of the story Ush Qiyan exists to tell: the wild horse that came to humans, changed their world and still carries that independence in its blood. Whether that story belongs to history or to myth is, by now, beside the point.

Between them, a father and son have built a temple to the horse, raised by hand, off the beaten track. “We live in a complex era of digital technology; it’s difficult to be surprised by anything,” says Nurbol. “But at the same time, we look to the future without forgetting the past. Our museum is like a time machine.”

But a time machine moves forwards, too. More visitors will be drawn by the same road that connected cultures. Ush Qiyan sits beside it, ready to welcome those who pause on their way to somewhere else. “The Great Steppe has always been a place of meeting,” says Nurbol. “We are open to dialogue with the world.”
