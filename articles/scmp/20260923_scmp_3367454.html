Bukhara Biennial spearheads cultural blossoming of the city as well as the wider Uzbekistan

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1741 words | 2026-09-23

It might sound and look like an odd juxtaposition: chef-nun Jeong Kwan, of Netflix documentary series Chef’s Table fame, unearthing kimchi she has been fermenting for 10 weeks next to a new installation by British sculptor Antony Gormley.

Yet, set in Bukhara’s 16th century Khoja Kalon Mosque, the two made perfect sense as collaborative highlights of the closing ceremony of the inaugural Bukhara Biennial, which ran from September 5 to November 23 last year.

A contemporary art festival, the Bukhara Biennial was conceived by the Uzbekistan Art and Culture Development Foundation (ACDF) and forms part of a broader plan to revitalise Uzbekistan’s living heritage as well as establish a permanent cultural district in the historic city. Its first theme, “Recipes for Broken Hearts”, was inspired by Uzbekistan’s national dish, plov. A hearty meal of rice layered with meat, spices and vegetables, the ideas of healing and restoration imbued in plov were the conceptual crux of the gastronomy, art, craft, architecture, music and performance on display.

Gormley’s Close (2025) – created from mud bricks with Uzbek artist Temur Jumaev – channelled the theme by exploring vulnerability, communal recovery and a reconnection to ancestral roots. Jeong Kwan’s kimchi was woven into a collaborative culinary performance with Indian artist Subodh Gupta. Blending Korean temple food with Bihari fare, the hybrid meal was served inside Gupta’s pavilion-like installation, Salt Carried With the Wind (2024-25), situated outside the Ayozjon Caravanserai, one of four in an interconnected complex, on a site once occupied by Bukhara’s spice market.

All through the event, the locations were significant, as the Bukhara Biennial was designed to house site-specific installations, soundscapes and performances not in purpose-built, white-cube galleries, but in restored or adapted caravanserais (merchant inns), madrasas (educational institutions), trading domes and mosques. While fellow Silk Road stopover Samarkand and the national capital, Tashkent, are undergoing their own cultural transformations, this preservation-first approach is what sets Bukhara apart.

Since its establishment in the 6th century BC as one of the main centres of the Persian Empire, Bukhara’s status grew as a spiritual nexus for Sufism and as an intellectual hub and home to scholars, poets and artisans. That Bukhara has lived many lives is evident from both its monumental buildings, spanning the 5th to 20th centuries, and the most intricately embroidered gold zardosi motifs and ikat prints on silks and various textiles found in the shops of the Old Town.

In the city’s Registan square, the curved, mounded walls of the Ark of Bukhara, a sizeable citadel, stand tall. Built in the 5th century, the compound housed rulers and their courts until 1920, when it was attacked by the Soviet Red Army, which deposed the emir of Bukhara and established Bolshevik rule. Next to the fortress stands the most prominent reminder of that later era: the steely, industrial Shukhov Tower, a water supply, forming a visual paradox that differentiates Bukhara from the more uniform Tashkent, where the Soviet influence is starkly evident in the brutalist buildings that dominate that city.

Across the street from the Ark of Bukhara, the early-18th century Bolo Hauz Mosque dazzles with its intricate, three-dimensional muqarnas decoration and vibrant mosaic tilework. Its linear lines present a striking contrast to the more rounded, ancient structures elsewhere in the Old Town, both charting the evolution of Islamic architecture. Just a short walk away stands the 10th century Ismail Samani Mausoleum, the soft, circular geometry and woven pattern capturing an aesthetic from when the region’s prominent Zoroastrian community was in the early stages of converting to Islam.

Revitalising these venerated sites, and others, was a crucial aspect of the agenda, as “the plan to reanimate restored and previously closed landmarks and open them up as living sites of making and dialogue was […] appreciated not only by the inhabitants of Bukhara, but also the tourists”, claims Gayane Umerova, chairwoman of the ACDF and the commissioner behind the inaugural Bukhara Biennial.

She enlisted Dubai- and Tokyo-based Lebanese architect Wael Al Awar and his studio, Waiwai, to execute this vision, to revive and make accessible to its inhabitants as much of Bukhara’s history as possible. In addition to repairing mosques, caravanserais and other structures, the most dramatic intervention the architect made was to pedestrianise large parts of the Old Town. “We created outdoor spaces, seating and platforms where the local community could gather recreationally, and use beyond the biennial,” says Al Awar.

Aligned with Umerova’s desire to present Uzbek artists and artisans on a global stage, the curatorial theme of the first Bukhara Biennial was born from curator Diana Campbell’s interest in rice cultures. “Most cultures use rice in some way, and there’s some version of plov in every culture,” says Campbell, citing the likes of biryani, paella, Korean bap with kimchi and West African jollof rice, all of which were served to the public at the Rice Cultures Feast, another part of the closing programme.

A key aim of the event was to stimulate local communities, the ACDF having spent seven years researching and mapping out the forms of craft across the country. “Supporting artisans is part of a strategy to develop the creative industry in Uzbekistan,” says Umerova. “We are looking at ways to nurture what we have already, by protecting our heritage and developing a framework that is sustainable and inclusive for artisans.”

All 70 artworks – each a collaboration between a recognised artist and a local craftsperson or -people – were made on site, in Bukhara.

“By placing artisans and craft masters at the heart of the programme and pairing them with international artists, we invited visitors to experience craft not as a souvenir product, but as a living, sophisticated cultural practice,” Umerova says. “It frames craftsmanship as something to observe, learn from and respect.”

Some of the most transformational and noteworthy works were housed in the 16th century Gavkushon Madrasa, and highlighted the specific artisanal crafts Bukhara is celebrated for. Local ceramicist Abdulvahid Bukhoriy’s Blue Room (2024-25), made with coppersmith Jurabek Siddikov, was lined entirely with blue-glazed tiles produced by hand, the immersive installation featuring a central metal sculpture and delicate ceramic panels, the elements depicting aquatic motifs often found in Uzbek textiles – a nod to age-old beliefs that fish possess the power to absorb human illness.

Miniature master Davlat Toshev transformed two adjoining rooms with his signature hand-painted “wallpaper”. Featuring traditional visual themes such as pomegranate trees, Toshev employed ebru, a marbled-paper painting technique widely believed to have originated in Bukhara. Toshev has a large studio in Bukhara’s Old Town, where he conducts workshops for deaf and mute children, using art as a method of expression and communication, as well as a way to keep traditional mediums alive.

Less than 200 metres (656 feet) from the Gavkushon Madrasa, Brazilian artist Marina Perez Simão and Bukharan mosaic master Bakhtiyar Babamuradov mirrored the heavens. Across the courtyard floor of the Fathullajon Caravanserai – a 19th century Silk Road resting stop – they laid out Untitled (2024-25), a fantastical celestial map. Quickly becoming one of the event’s most photographed works, the vibrant installation paid homage to Uzbekistan’s legacy in astronomy and the creation of early space-mapping instruments.

Kwan’s involvement in the Bukhara Biennial was more significant than it might have seemed at first glance, with members of the Koryo-saram, the Korean diaspora in Bukhara, assisting in the preparation of the kimchi and the resulting meal. In 1937, Soviet leader Joseph Stalin, concerned about the military threat from Japan, exiled 172,000 Koreans from the Russian Far East to Central Asia. Now, Uzbekistan is home to the world’s fifth-largest overseas Korean population.

Increased global attention on Uzbekistan as a result of the Bukhara Biennial has established a gateway into a historically under-visited destination, and the massive funding poured into the exhibition marks an effort by the Shavkat Mirziyoyev administration to build cultural infrastructure, revitalise the nation’s creative landscape and attract foreign investment after nearly three decades of post-Soviet stagnation.

“We want to inspire younger generations to take the creative path and to equip those already in the creative industries, including artisans, with the same opportunities and infrastructure they’d experience internationally,” says Umerova, noting that 40 per cent of the country’s population is under 35 years of age.

Projects in the works for the city’s new cultural district include the Jadids’ Legacy Museum, scheduled to open in 2027. Set in the restored 19th century home of Usmon Khodjaev, a leader of the Jadid reform movement and the first president of the short-lived Bukhara People’s Soviet Republic (1920-24), the destination will be dedicated to the initiative that modernised education across Central Asia. Farther afield, in Tashkent, the Centre for Contemporary Arts (CCA Tashkent), housed in a former tram depot, opened earlier this month, and the National Museum of Uzbekistan is scheduled to launch in March 2028.

“Since we launched the 2025 Bukhara Biennial, Uzbekistan has become globally recognised for its role in fostering arts and culture across Central Asia,” says Umerova, who notes that the opening of the CCA Tashkent marks “an exciting next step – and a historic moment, not just for the people of Tashkent, but for the citizens of Uzbekistan more widely. As the country’s first contemporary art centre, I believe it will inspire the next generation of artists, curators, poets and thinkers”.

Bukhara’s inaugural biennial surpassed all expectations, clocking up a total of 1.8 million visitors. Umerova credits it with encouraging “a more thoughtful kind of tourism. People came not just to see monuments, but to understand the stories embedded in them”.

Dates for the next edition of the Bukhara Biennial have been set for September 3 to November 21, 2027. Thai architect Kulapat Yantrasast will direct it, leaning on themes of urban space, ecology and sustainable development. In line with the ACDF’s mission to foster community involvement, admission to venues and public spaces will remain free.

“When you bring art tourists, who actually look, care and value the work and sites, that’s a genius strategy,” says Campbell, who specialises in curating large-scale exhibitions centred on community engagement in regions with sparse cultural infrastructure, most notably as the artistic director of the Dhaka Art Summit, in Bangladesh.

In an era defined by content overkill, the ability to evoke genuine awe and wonder sometimes feels like a lost art. In Bukhara, it is simply an ancient art form being powerfully revived.
