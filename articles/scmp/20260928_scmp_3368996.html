Trump asked Xi if Beijing wanted US weapons: a joke, a gambit or something more?

SCMP | FREE | 1276 words | 2026-09-28

Donald Trump asked President Xi Jinping whether Beijing wanted to buy American weapons, the US ambassador to China said on Sunday, prompting the State Department to quickly rule out any such deal with Washington’s principal strategic competitor.

“He actually asked President Xi, would he like to buy some at one point,” the ambassador David Perdue said on Fox News after Xi’s state visit to the White House last week. He did not say when Trump raised the question, what prompted it, or how the Chinese leader responded.

A State Department official, speaking on background to the South China Morning Post, said: “US law prohibits arms sales to China and there is no offer or plan to sell arms to China.”

The swift clarification came as the disclosure ricocheted among Asia specialists, who noted the extraordinary contrast between Trump reportedly raising the prospect of arming Beijing and his administration continuing to hold up a roughly US$14 billion weapons package for Taiwan.

Washington has spent decades treating Beijing as a strategic rival, restricting military technology exports, strengthening security partnerships across the Indo-Pacific region and building its capacity to deter a potential conflict.

That is what makes Trump’s reported offer so extraordinary: the US has not sold conventional weapons to Beijing for more than three decades and has spent much of that time building its military posture around deterring the Chinese military, not supplying it.

In the 1980s, the Reagan administration briefly expanded military cooperation with Beijing as both countries sought to counter the Soviet Union. Washington approved several military related sales, including the roughly $550 million Peace Pearl programme involving US avionics for Chinese J-8 fighter aircraft.

That relationship ended after the Tiananmen Square crackdown in June 1989. Then-president George H.W. Bush suspended military sales and exchanges with Beijing. Congress later codified restrictions on the transfer of US defence articles to the Chinese military.

US law permits a presidential waiver in the national interest, but no administration has used that authority to restore a conventional US arms pipeline to Beijing.

The strategic landscape has since changed dramatically. Successive US administrations have spent billions preparing for a possible military confrontation with Beijing and supplying weapons to Taiwan to help deter a potential attack.

Meanwhile, China has built the world’s largest navy by number of ships and rapidly expanded its missile, air and nuclear capabilities. The Pentagon has identified Beijing as the United States’ most consequential long term strategic challenge.

That makes Perdue’s disclosure particularly striking.

Zack Cooper, an Asia security fellow at the American Enterprise Institute and former Pentagon and White House official, wrote on social media that he was “underwhelmed when it looked like the big deliverable both sides actually announced at this Trump-Xi summit was the lease of two pandas”.

“But it never occurred to me that an American president might offer to sell American weapons to China, he added.

Cooper later stressed that the remark came directly from Perdue, not an anonymous source. “This wasn’t a leak, it was a comment by President Trump’s ambassador to China on Fox,” he wrote. “I’ll take him at his word until I’m told otherwise.”

He also highlighted the contrast with the stalled weapons deal with Taipei, saying it was “shocking that Trump is resisting authorising arms sales to Taiwan but at the same time offering them to China”.

Beijing sees Taiwan as part of China to be reunited by force if necessary. Most countries, including the US, do not recognise Taiwan as an independent state, but Washington is opposed to any attempt to take the self-governed island by force and is committed to supplying it with weapons.

After meeting Xi in Beijing in May, Trump said he was holding the package “in abeyance”, calling it “a very good negotiating chip” and saying the decision “depends on China”.

Perdue offered no timetable on the sales in the interview on Sunday. “There are continuing conversations about other sales,” he said, adding that Trump “has a schedule of his own”.

Perdue also said that Trump and Xi discuss American arms sales to Taiwan “every time they meet” – a comment that drew attention to Washington’s pledge to Taipei in the 1982 Six Assurances not to consult Beijing on the issue.

Ryan Hass, who handled China policy at the National Security Council and now heads the China Centre at Brookings, said the disclosure would unsettle US partners in Asia.

“Trump’s offer to sell weapons to China will be unsettling to American partners at the pointy edge of China’s spear,” Hass wrote on social media.

Taiwan’s de facto envoy in Washington, Alexander Tah-ray Yui, said on the CBS programme Face the Nation on Sunday that last week’s summit statements listed no Taiwan deliverable, even though Beijing called the issue “foundational”.

“It was foundational, but there was no reaction from the US side,” Yui said. On the stalled package, he added: “The United States has been our main supplier of weapons to Taiwan. We hope that we can get that weapons package in as soon as possible.”

Peace through strength, he argued, was “the best way to avoid any conflict”, adding that deterrence rested on the idea that Beijing would not attack if the island itself were strong.

The episode has left a simpler question hanging over Washington’s China policy debate: was Trump seriously floating the idea, or was he joking with Xi? And if it was a joke, why did Perdue feel it was worth mentioning on national television?

Denis Simon of the Quincy Institute said the key distinction was between Trump raising the idea and making a concrete arms offer.

“There is no evidence that Trump made a concrete offer for China to purchase a specific US weapons package, nor that China accepted or seriously negotiated such a deal,” Simon said.

“It appears to have been a rhetorical/negotiating manoeuvre rather than an actual arms-sales proposal.”

But Simon said that this did not make the remark insignificant, particularly given its connection to Taiwan.

“Trump’s logic appears to have been something like: ‘You object to our selling weapons to Taiwan. But America sells weapons to many countries. Why shouldn’t China be able to buy American weapons if it wants them?’,” he said.

“That is an intentionally provocative way of reframing the Taiwan issue.”

Simon said the episode pointed to a more transactional approach to Taiwan arms sales, in which the weapons package itself becomes leverage in the broader relationship between Washington and Beijing.

In his formulation, Trump’s approach is closer to this: Taiwan needs weapons, Beijing objects, so let’s see what Beijing is willing to give in return.

He noted that Trump may also have been using the hypothetical sale to “tout the appeal of American military hardware”, suggesting that even Beijing might see US weapons as preferable to its own.

Scott Kennedy of the Centre for Strategic and International Studies had a more immediate reaction. “Wow! Not sure why Amb Perdue decided it’d be wise to share that comment with the world,” he said.

Bonnie Glaser, who heads the Indo-Pacific programme at the German Marshall Fund, focused on another part of Perdue’s interview.

The ambassador said Trump and Xi discuss US arms sales to Taiwan “every time they meet” and that Washington “doesn’t support Taiwan independence and doesn’t support coercion”.

“Shouldn’t the US oppose coercion?” Glaser wrote.

Bill Bishop, author of the Sinocism newsletter, offered a different interpretation, calling it “just more Trump joking” while adding that he was “not sure why Perdue mentioned it”.
