Analysis: Trade Surplus, Capital Inflows Drive Yuan’s Rise

Caixin Global | FULLTEXT | 720 words | 2026-11-24 13:18

1. China's yuan has appreciated approximately 9% against the U.S. dollar since early 2025, driven by strong exports and rising demand for yuan settlement, which has outweighed the traditional impact of interest rate differentials between China and the U.S. The currency's resilience reflects changing dynamics in China's foreign exchange market, where a record trade surplus and increased overseas investment in Chinese assets are generating significant demand for the yuan. Concerns about the U.S. fiscal outlook and geopolitical exposure have also contributed to the dollar's weakness and the yuan's strength. Expectations of further appreciation have encouraged companies to convert foreign currency earnings into yuan, creating a self-reinforcing feedback loop that could reverse if market sentiment shifts.

2. China's goods trade surplus exceeded $930 billion in the first nine months of this year, approaching the $1 trillion recorded for all of 2025, with September alone showing a record surplus of $133.5 billion. Banks recorded a foreign exchange settlement surplus of nearly $400 billion in the first nine months, meaning client purchases of yuan against foreign currencies significantly exceeded transactions in the opposite direction. Overseas investment provided another source of support, with foreign institutions and individuals holding about 7 trillion yuan ($1.2 trillion) in onshore yuan bonds and equities by end-June, up nearly 540 billion yuan from a year earlier. These flows have helped offset the traditional influence of interest rate differentials, as the yuan remained resilient even when the Federal Reserve raised rates by 25 basis points in September.

3. Sheng Songcheng, president of the China Chief Economist Forum Research Institute, stated that demand to convert foreign currency earnings into yuan had temporarily outweighed interest rate differentials in determining the exchange rate, but cautioned that the positive feedback between settlement demand and appreciation expectations could prove vulnerable. If companies begin anticipating depreciation instead, they could delay converting foreign currency earnings into yuan, potentially reversing the flows that have supported the currency.

4. The changing relationship between the yuan and China's export performance complicates international criticism that Beijing keeps its currency undervalued to support exporters. Following the G7 summit in June, German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said the yuan was undervalued, while People's Bank of China Governor Pan Gongsheng argued that rising protectionism and flaws in the international monetary system were exacerbating global imbalances and that structural reforms were needed. International Monetary Fund Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva has called on several countries to adjust their economic policies.

5. Historical data cited by the PBOC suggests that currency movements have become less closely correlated with China's export competitiveness. During periods of yuan appreciation—21% between 2005 and 2008, 10% between 2010 and 2014, and 9% between 2020 and 2021—China's share of global exports still increased. Conversely, the yuan's depreciation in 2016 and 2022 did not prevent China's export share from falling. One explanation is that Chinese exporters have become less exposed to exchange rate movements due to deeper integration into global supply chains, which provides greater scope to share currency costs with suppliers and customers, while more widespread hedging and yuan-denominated settlement provide additional protection.

6. The corporate foreign exchange hedging ratio reached 35.3% in the first half of this year, up 5.3 percentage points from 2025 and 13.6 percentage points from 2021, according to the State Administration of Foreign Exchange. However, hedging remains uneven, with some state-owned enterprises reluctant to use hedging because government auditors may treat losses on hedging transactions as a loss of state-owned assets. Internal performance incentives can also discourage hedging, as sales departments may negotiate contracts without accounting for currency costs, leaving finance departments to absorb resulting losses.

7. Using the yuan for cross-border settlement, which currently accounts for about 30% of cross-border transactions, can reduce companies' direct exposure to exchange rate fluctuations, but does not eliminate currency risk when contracts remain denominated in dollars, since the conversion rate still determines the final settlement value. Many market participants expect the yuan to continue appreciating moderately through the end of the year, supported by the trade surplus and demand for foreign exchange settlement. However, risks remain, including a more aggressive Fed response to inflation that could strengthen the dollar and reverse capital flows, and Barclays research warning that exporters' dollar settlement flows could ease as appreciation expectations become less one-sided.
