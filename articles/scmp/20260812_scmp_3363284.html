Bangkok branches out: secret to Thai capital’s green revival

SCMP | FREE | 1141 words | 2026-08-12

In recent years, Thailand’s capital has discovered a surprising new attraction to add to its temples, shopping malls and street food: urban greenery. Unlike the others, which took decades to develop, Bangkok’s green credentials are a more recent phenomenon, emerging only during the Covid-19 pandemic.

Despite their novelty, the city’s greening efforts are already bearing fruit. Visitors returning to Bangkok after the pandemic are often surprised by how green and walkable the city has become. It comes as a nice surprise given the city’s long-standing reputation as a concrete jungle with chronic traffic jams and accompanying air pollution.

It is even more unusual considering how Thai politics, in the last two decades, has been highly polarised. Bangkok’s early success in urban greening shows that it is possible, despite contentious politics and a divided and highly unequal society, to build a societal consensus around making the city greener.

There are three lessons from Bangkok’s urban greenery playbook that other cities, including Hong Kong, should pay heed to.

Clear goals

This first is to set clear, measurable goals. In December 2019, the Bangkok Metropolitan Administration (BMA) launched the Green Bangkok 2030 initiative that set three ambitious targets:

Increase green spaces in the city to 10 square metres (110 square feet) per person from 7.6–7.8 square metres per person;

Increase the green spaces that people can access within 400 metres – or five minutes’ walk – to at least 50 per cent of Bangkok’s total area; and

Increase the urban tree canopy to 30 per cent of Bangkok’s total area.

In mid-2022, the BMA initiated the Bangkok Million Tree Planting Project to increase urban green spaces, reduce air and dust pollution, and cool the city. Initially, the project sought to plant 1 million trees in the city by 2025. But after that goal was surpassed by mid-2024, it was increased by another 1 million. To date, nearly 1.2 million trees have been planted.

Pocket parks have also been built under transit lines and expressways as well as on under-utilised public or private lands such as abandoned piers, neglected canal paths and even garbage dumps.

The main benefit of setting such goals is to demonstrate the government’s seriousness of intent and its willingness to be held accountable. Governments are seldom short of visions but without committing to measurable goals and coming up with specific implementation plans such as the Million Tree Project, these will just be cheap talk.

A second benefit is to define exactly what is being measured and targeted. For example, much of Bangkok’s urban green spaces is not easily accessible. Actively accessible urban green spaces in Bangkok stand at a low 1.5-1.7 square metres per capita, well below the World Health Organization’s guideline of at least 9 square metres per capita for a healthy city. This is why the second target under the Green Bangkok 2030 blueprint is necessary: to highlight the importance of increasing green spaces that can be easily accessed by residents.

In a similar vein, BMA’s tree-planting initiative has not been sufficient to offset the city’s loss of urban tree canopy due to deforestation. This is why the third Green Bangkok 2030 target matters: to emphasise the protection of existing, mature trees even as new ones are planted.

Public support

The second lesson is to build public support and momentum with politically salient early successes. For Bangkok, this came in the form of the Benjakitti Forest Park, which opened in early 2022.

Situated between the two main commercial areas – Sukhumvit Road and Sathorn Road – the park has already become a popular destination that attracts tens of thousands of users daily. It also connects to the older Lumpini Park via a 1.6km-long treetop bridge that is reminiscent of New York’s High Line.

Sitting on 720,000 square metres of what used to be brownfield land that previously housed tobacco factories and warehouses, Benjakitti has become the city’s main green lung. Equally impressive is the fact that the forest park was planned to be a low-cost, low-maintenance operation through a self-sustaining, “messy nature” design and reliance on native plants to regenerate organically.

Since its opening, Benjakitti has served various environmental and social objectives. For example, its woodlands and constructed wetlands act as sponges to mitigate flooding in the city. It is home to diverse wildlife – including 91 species of birds – and its numerous boardwalks over the wetlands and a canopy skywalk provide visitors with an immersive urban nature experience. The sports centre and museum, both converted from old warehouses, provide residents with much-needed space for leisure activities, while an open-air amphitheatre can host events for 15,000 people.

Besides being aesthetically pleasing, the various functions of the forest park have helped endear it to the public. That it was built for under US$20 per square metre and was completed in about 18 months during the pandemic – a time when everyone could see the need for open public spaces – shows the BMA’s ability to deliver sustainability projects on time and on budget.

It is hardly surprising, therefore, that just four years after it opened, the park is already celebrated as the most vivid showcase of how Green Bangkok 2030 is transforming the city.

Good connections

Urban greening initiatives are also more likely to succeed if they are combined with efforts to make the city more walkable and public transit more accessible. To support the former, the BMA has upgraded more than 1,100km of pavements in recent years, including some along canals in the historic Hua Lamphong district.

While public transit in Bangkok has improved in recent years, the system is still inadequate for a sprawling city of more than 10 million. The sprawl makes public transit less viable financially, as more and longer lines are needed to serve a dispersed population. Reducing it, in turn, requires significant changes to urban and land-use planning.

Harnessing the energies of the private sector and the community is also key to the long-term success of greening efforts. Encouragingly, new commercial developments in Bangkok seem to have embraced the city’s greening push. For instance, Bangkok’s highest office building at the new One City Centre development in Ploenchit incorporates a large and verdant green space, including a rooftop garden.

Bangkok residents can also join tree-growing campaigns and support local groups to monitor and protect urban forests. The BMA engages local networks to come up with better rules for tree trimming and public space management.

Bangkok’s transformation into a green, walkable and sustainable city is far from complete. But already, its achievements in the last few years are a clear demonstration of what setting measurable goals, taking implementation seriously, having highly visible early success stories and synergising with the rest of society can do.

Donald Low is Senior Lecturer and Professor of Practice of Public Policy at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology.
