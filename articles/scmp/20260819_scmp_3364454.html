Wang Yi heads to Seoul as Trump cuts South Korea drills, opening door for China

SCMP | FREE | 1085 words | 2026-08-19

Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi is set to visit Seoul days after Donald Trump called for the US to scale back military exercises with South Korea, sending ripples of unease through American allies in Asia.

The US president posted his decision to social media on Sunday, raising questions about the durability of Washington’s security commitments in a region increasingly defined by competition with Beijing.

He offered several rationales, including cost-saving considerations, a goodwill gesture towards North Korea, and dissatisfaction with Seoul’s refusal to join the US in the war against Iran.

Observers said such moves threatened to damage alliance coordination, causing disquiet among regional partners and handing China a strategic opening to dilute America’s network of regional partnerships.

Trump posted on Sunday that he had instructed the Pentagon to scale back what he termed “inappropriate and hostile” military drills with South Korea – exercises traditionally aimed at deterring North Korea, a country he described as “unthreatening and respectful” during his time in office.

He added that he had ordered the US defence secretary to “substantially reduce” the drills, citing Seoul’s reluctance to support America’s efforts towards the “denuclearisation of the Islamic Republic of Iran”.

As Seoul and other US partners navigate the latest shocks, Wang will begin a two-day trip to Seoul on Wednesday, his first bilateral exchange trip in five years.

Trump’s call for reduced commitment came just hours before Washington and Seoul launched the 11-day Ulchi Freedom Shield drills, one of the two major annual exercises.

Lee Dong-gyu, a senior research fellow at The Asan Institute for Policy Studies, said Trump’s statements revealed his willingness to trade alliance security for political gains, such as re-engagement with North Korean leader Kim Jong-un, a calculation Lee said would increase Seoul’s trust deficit in its alliance with Washington.

William Yang, senior analyst for Northeast Asia at the International Crisis Group, said the statement matched Trump’s usual playbook of signalling frustration towards allies he viewed as failing to respond to US demands for support.

“While the order may be a tactical move from the US, it could undermine long-standing trust between the two countries and prompt Seoul to put more resources into hedging between Beijing and Washington,” he said.

Leif-Eric Easley, professor of international studies at Ewha Womans University in Seoul, said that curtailing joint manoeuvres risked undermining operational coordination and weakening deterrence against possible conflicts in Asia.

Experts noted that the latest remarks would unnerve regional partners such as the Philippines and Japan, saying their frictions with China left them particularly exposed.

Trump has regularly criticised US allies during his second term, intensifying concerns over the reliability of Washington’s commitments and pushing partners towards self-reliance and strategic hedging.

However, in a sign of reassurance, the US undersecretary of defence for policy, Elbridge Colby, used his first Southeast Asia tour last week to stress that Washington was not disengaging from Asia but “digging in” to maintain a favourable balance of power in the Indo-Pacific.

But he added that Washington was prioritising burden-sharing as it realised its allies “can and are prepared to do more”.

According to Yang, Washington’s allies will bear in mind the more transactional foreign policy outlook that Trump subscribes to and try to respond to demands from Washington.

Meanwhile, Lee noted that Beijing was likely to exploit the scaled-back exercises as a tool, seeking to constrain Seoul’s participation in the US alliance network and diminish America’s military footprint in the region.

“At the same time, leveraging its friendly ties with Pyongyang, China is likely to intensify both its appeasement and pressure on South Korea under the pretext of fostering a favourable environment for US-North Korea dialogue,” Lee said.

Trump’s directive has sparked anticipation of his renewed engagement with Kim, although it remains uncertain whether the North Korean leader will reciprocate – particularly as Pyongyang appears to hold more cards than at the last summits with Trump in 2018 and 2019.

Following his summit with Kim in Singapore in June 2018, Trump abruptly suspended major joint military exercises with South Korea, calling them provocative, inappropriate and too expensive.

But since the collapse of high-level diplomacy in 2019, Pyongyang has aggressively expanded its missile tests and gained more battlefield experience by sending its soldiers to take part in the Russia-Ukraine war.

Lee Choong-koo, a research fellow at the Korea Institute for Defence Analyses, said Trump’s directive was part of a broader US diplomatic charm offensive towards North Korea – one that might require China’s cooperation.

Seoul has long expected Beijing to play a role in bringing Pyongyang back to the negotiation table, as Pyongyang neglects Seoul’s repeated calls to improve inter-Korean relations.

“His move may encourage greater diplomatic engagement between the US and China on North Korea,” Lee Choong-koo said, adding that Beijing might welcome Trump’s announcement.

“With Foreign Minister Wang Yi expected to visit Seoul this week, China may emphasise that its support is essential to advancing South Korea’s diplomatic initiative to launch multilateral peace talks.”

On Monday, Trump told reporters that Kim had responded to his recent overtures and described the current state of communication as “very positive”. Kim has not yet commented publicly.

Easley, however, offered a more sceptical assessment. He argued that Pyongyang was not interested in talking because it was profiting from Russia’s war on Ukraine while modernising its own military.

“The Trump administration’s ongoing difficulties negotiating with Iran do not enhance its credibility with the Kim regime.”

Meanwhile, some observers pointed out that Trump’s announcement could further weaken US-led deterrence against China – a development that might work in Beijing’s favour.

The mismatch between US strategic documents on defending the first island chain and Trump’s decision on joint drills with South Korea would strengthen regional countries’ belief that they needed to adopt risk-reduction measures against the US, Yang said.

“This trend will benefit China, as it creates an opportunity for Beijing to drive a wedge between the US and its regional allies.”

The first island chain consists of several strategic arcs – stretching from Japan and Taiwan through the Philippines to Borneo – that underpin a US-aligned security architecture to check Beijing.

Although traditionally not viewed as part of the chain, South Korea, now hosting some 28,500 US troops under a decades-old alliance, is increasingly seen as the northern anchor of the chain’s defence network.
