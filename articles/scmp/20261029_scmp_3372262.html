From Hong Kong to the Olympics and beyond: swimmer Yvette Kong’s journey of resilience

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1530 words | 2026-10-29

I WAS BORN in Toronto, Canada, in 1993, and moved to Hong Kong at six months old. My mother’s side of the family fled to Hong Kong from Shanghai in the 1940s; my father’s side is Hakka. My father grew up in a family that had long been in the shoe business and ran a shoe shop in Mong Kok. My parents met while studying at the University of Minnesota, in the United States. When I was little, they both worked in the shoe trade; that business went into decline after the 1997 Asian financial crisis and gradually disintegrated.

IN 1996, AT the age of three, I had a serious case of Kawasaki disease (which can cause damage to the heart and blood vessels and mostly affects children). I remember relatives arriving with lots of Sailor Moon gifts, my grandmother crying and my mother curling up on a plastic stool beside my hospital bed to sleep. A needle was stuck into my back while I screamed. Only years later did I realise it was a bone-marrow test to rule out cancer.

ALSO IN 1996, my mother enrolled me in swimming lessons, to learn a survival skill. I loved it but wasn’t really any good at first. As a child, I was very slow on land. I daydreamed at school and struggled with simple tasks such as connecting the dots on Kumon worksheets. My mother asked a doctor whether the illness had damaged my brain. Nicknames followed: “a lump of rice”, “a cloud” – I couldn’t sit still.

DURING THE 2000 Summer Olympics, I sat in front of the television, hypnotised by the underwater footage and swimmers like (Ian) Thorpe, (Alexander) Popov – I announced that, one day, I would be one of them. Children don’t calculate winning odds.

WHEN I WAS seven, I joined the Good Hope School swim team as one of only two female Primary One pupils on the squad. For a girl who couldn’t organise herself on land, water became where I could be myself and feel my worth. I thought I should have been a fish.

BY THE TIME I was 10, my mother had started designing her own swimwear, based on what she had observed swimmers actually needed. She initially sold the swimsuits at my training sessions. Years later, that small poolside business grew into a shop for swimwear, in Tsim Sha Tsui, which she still operates today.

I FIRST REPRESENTED Hong Kong at age 11, at an interschool competition in Macau. At 13, I broke my first Hong Kong record – the 100-metre breaststroke, in 2006 – and everything changed. I was an up-and-coming prodigy. I set another Hong Kong record in the 200-metre breaststroke the next year. Swimming stopped being just something I loved, and turned into something everyone was invested in.

I MISSED QUALIFYING for the 2008 Olympics, in the 200-metre breaststroke, by about a second, even though a year later, I was ranked in the top 25 globally. Inexplicably, after hitting my peak, I plateaued. For the next six years, despite training harder and entering increasingly elite environments, I barely got faster.

THE NEXT OLYMPICS, in 2012, were worse: the qualifying rules changed and I landed on a wait-list. I flew to the Hong Kong team’s holding camp in Cardiff, Wales, anyway, to watch the athletes. My ego was too big at the time to let me walk away.

I WAS AT the University of California, Berkeley, in the US, from 2012 to 2015, on a sports scholarship to study cognitive science. Despite being in this elite environment, it felt like the harder I trained, the further backwards I went. By my second year at Berkeley, around 2013, I couldn’t even qualify for the NCAA (National Collegiate Athletic Association) Championships. I felt like a rock at the bottom of the pool. My body had stopped listening. I told my coaches I was quitting. I thought that would be the end of it, but memories and emotions from swimming that I had pushed down began resurfacing. I remember sitting in class and suddenly crying. I realised that emotions don’t simply disappear because you bury them.

I HAD ASSUMED that, if you did exactly what the Olympians did and had the best training, you’d go faster. What I eventually realised was that the body doesn’t respond to training in isolation. Performance is shaped by the whole person – psychology, stress, recovery, relationships, coaching and environment – and the human body isn’t a machine you can simply programme and expect an output from.

BECAUSE I WAS studying the brain anyway, I treated myself like a problem to solve. I found a book on EMDR (eye-movement desensitisation and reprocessing) therapy and thought, “Maybe I have trauma.” I found a therapist whose services I could afford and worked with her for months. Part of what I found was something simple: I’d started as a kid in la-la land who loved the water, and in the pursuit of excellence and competitions, I was lost.

AFTER I GRADUATED, I gave myself a year to swim on completely different terms. I read that the University of Edinburgh, in Scotland, turned swimmers who’d started nowhere near elite into internationally ranked breaststrokers. I cold-called and asked them to take me in. They were baffled: “You’re coming from Berkeley, where all the superhuman athletes are.” I arrived in Scotland in the summer of 2015, in flip-flops and shorts, and nearly froze. It became one of the best years of my life, mostly because my coach, Chris Jones, cared about me as a person. What I’ve carried into every workplace and classroom since is that humans are humans.

WHEN I QUALIFIED for the Rio 2016 Summer Olympics, I had already decided it would be my final try. I didn’t win a medal, but being there was more important, and the first place I went, in the Olympic Village, was the canteen. I sat watching athletes from every sport and country come and go. Every one of us had climbed a mountain to get there, I thought.

WEEKS BEFORE RIO started, I was hospitalised with a stomach bug. I thought performing at my best would take a miracle. I was anxious enough that I skipped the opening ceremony, which I now regret. When I walked out to the blocks at the start of my race, my senses seemed magnified. For a moment, it was only me and the race; the next, I was blended into the whole crowd and noise of it. I swam in that heat and went no further.

DEEP DOWN, I knew it would be my last proper race. Not to sound morbid, but it felt like the vision people describe at the end of their life – the child who wanted to be a fish, the 13-year-old breaking her first record, the girl who lost her way, the woman who found it again and even my future self, all converging at once, surrounded by everyone who had witnessed the journey.

POST-RIO, I was kind of at peace. I’d seen extraordinary athletes and how anxious and depressed they were, and I understood that achievement and well-being were not the same. It changed what success meant to me: results matter, but they are not the sole measure of life.

I JOINED MIND Hong Kong in 2017, as a founding board member. It started with just the three of us; now, there are more than 100 staff. I also gave myself a year of seriously practising martial arts and wushu, which I’d been interested in since watching Bruce Lee as a child – I’ve always been drawn to warrior-girl types such as Sailor Moon and Mulan.

THEN THE PART I never planned: I went corporate. I’d never even done an internship before, so it was a shock. I worked for half a year at Goldman Sachs, two years at McKinsey & Company and another two at Estée Lauder. I then went back to school remotely to study performance psychology at the University of Edinburgh during Covid-19. Later, I did an MBA at Tsinghua University, in Beijing, and an MSc at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, in the US, where professors asked me to stay on to teach. I went from student to lecturer and built my own course on transversal design for social impact.

ALL OF THIS led to my Hong Kong-based start-up, Arelyx, founded last year. We build what I call “human-capacity intelligence” for an artificial-intelligence-shaped world, starting with education. We help schools understand and develop capacities such as resilience, ethical judgment and agency. Our first deployment began in Hong Kong this September, at Catholic Mission School.

I SPOKE ABOUT my new book, The Unmeasured Human, in August at the International Olympic Committee, in Lausanne, Switzerland, and noticed it was exactly 10 years since I had represented Hong Kong at Rio 2016. When I retired from swimming, people asked if I’d write my story. I said I was too young and there was more I needed to find out. It took a decade.
