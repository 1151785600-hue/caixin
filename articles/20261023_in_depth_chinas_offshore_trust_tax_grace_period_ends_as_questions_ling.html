A 90-day grace period for wealthy Chinese individuals to declare personal income taxes on offshore trusts expired Oct. 22, prompting substantial back-tax payments while some taxpayers held out over valuation and scope disputes. The deadline marks a milestone in China's tighter scrutiny of wealthy citizens' overseas assets, with some trust holders selling equity to meet tax bills, raising concerns about further selling pressure in financial markets.

The Ministry of Finance and State Taxation Administration announced rules on July 24 imposing a 20% tax rate on gains from property transferred into offshore trusts and returns during trust operations. Existing trusts received a 90-day transition period waiving late-payment charges, with a final reminder issued Oct. 19. Some high-net-worth individuals accelerated payments, while others remained hesitant over the scope of taxation and retroactive collection of taxes on historical returns.

Ye Yongqing, a tax partner at Anli Partners, said all his clients had completed declarations, with most in the first group notified having complied. Yang Xiang of Beijing Hongfan Law Firm noted the wealthiest are generally more willing to cooperate since their structures are disclosed in filings, making them easy to identify. Other taxpayers worry that disclosing trust structures and beneficiaries could invite scrutiny of additional offshore income; many mainland clients asked about the rules but few had begun filing, an industry source told the South China Morning Post.

Even for the ultra-rich, tax bills can strain cash. A tax lawyer said several clients owed billions of yuan because authorities sought taxes covering five or more years, forcing asset sales. Bloomberg News identified eight Chinese entrepreneurs among the world's 500 richest holding shares worth about $140.2 billion through offshore trusts as of Oct. 16, including about $37 billion for NetEase's Ding Lei and $29 billion for PDD's Huang Zheng — market values, not taxable income.

Recent share sales fueled speculation about tax-driven selling, though connections remain unconfirmed. SP NP Ltd., the Haidilao founding family's holding company, sold 259 million shares for about HK$2.8 billion ($360 million) held through Rose Trust. Qudian disclosed paying 790 million yuan ($118 million) in taxes owed by founder Luo Min after equity restructurings. Concerns coincided with a sharp Hong Kong stock decline: the Hang Seng Index fell 3% to below 23,000 and the Hang Seng Tech Index dropped 5%.

Disputes remain over enforcement. The July notice required back-tax payments for trusts established between 2023 and 2025, with longer look-back for "large amounts," defined Oct. 19 as unpaid taxes exceeding 100,000 yuan. The standard look-back is three years, extending to five, but authorities often review five to eight years in practice. Valuation is another point of contention: taxable income uses market value at transfer less original cost, but some executives transferred stock options before their companies delisted; shares are now worth less than 5% of transfer value.

Enforcement has revealed classification errors: Canadian life insurance policies were mistakenly identified as offshore trusts, and Hong Kong residents' Mandatory Provident Fund accounts were also misclassified — though the MPF "Master Trust" structure differs from family trusts. Changing citizenship or residency does not necessarily remove Chinese tax obligations if one's principal economic activities remain on the mainland.
