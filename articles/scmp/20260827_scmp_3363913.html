The 11 key achievements of China’s ‘iron premier’ Zhu Rongji – in his own words

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1717 words | 2026-08-27

Known for his sharp intellect, no-nonsense demeanour and relentless drive for reform, China’s former premier Zhu Rongji was the primary architect of the country’s transition from an isolated, rigidly planned economy to a market-driven titan connected to the rest of the world. During his tenure from 1998 to 2003, he engineered an economic “soft landing”, steered the nation through the Asian Financial Crisis and secured entry into the WTO, laying the groundwork for the country’s historic transformation.

Here is a look back at his key contributions, remembered through his most memorable and hard-hitting quotes.

1. Spearheading Shanghai’s revival

During his time as Shanghai’s mayor from 1988 to 1991, Zhu restructured the city’s industrial base, focused on exports and kick-started the construction of the Pudong New Area, an economic development zone. Early on, he recognised that foreign direct investment was key to the city’s future growth.

“If there’s one thing about me, my guiding principle, it’s independent thought. I feel that whatever’s on my mind is what I should say out loud. … What Shanghai needs most right now is grounded, practical follow-through.” – Giving a speech as a candidate for Shanghai mayor in 1987.

2. Defusing “triangular debt”

When Zhu was appointed governor of the People’s Bank of China in 1993, the country’s financial and payments system faced immense challenges with banks and state-owned enterprises (SOEs) hopelessly enmeshed in “triangular debt”.

Put simply, the phrase describes a chain of debts – Company A owing money to Company B, Company B owing money to Company C and Company C owing money to Company A – which can paralyse the economy.

Zhu tackled this issue by injecting targeted funds, establishing the responsibility of borrowers and allowing those unable to pay their debts to go bankrupt.

“The main source of triangular debt is that government departments, localities and enterprises blindly carry out construction projects in pursuit of rapid growth, resulting in a large fixed asset investment gap.” – Speaking at a national conference on clearing up triangular debt on September 3, 1991.

3. Engineering a “soft landing” for the Chinese economy

In the early 1990s, China’s breakneck economic expansion led to surging inflation and speculation, prompting fears that the boom would spin completely out of control. As head of the central bank, Zhu imposed strict macroeconomic tightening, controlled the money supply and clamped down on loose credit and speculative fixed-asset investments. Such tough measures successfully steered China towards a “soft landing” without triggering a devastating crash.

“Having worked in economics for over 50 years, I’ve felt the brunt of our country’s recurrent syndrome: the moment life gets even slightly better, then comes the grandstanding, the blind complacency, the pointless thrashing about and the totally ignorant decision-making.” – Giving a speech at the Ninth Plenary Meeting of the State Council, January 27, 2003.

4. Weathering the Asian Financial Crisis

As premier, Zhu had to face down the 1997-1998 Asian Financial Crisis, standing firm against pressure to devalue the Chinese currency, which helped stabilise regional markets and prevent a cascading competitive devaluation across Asia.

He also provided steadfast economic backing to Hong Kong as speculative attacks hit the local currency and stock market, leaving a legacy as a resolute crisis manager.

He also launched massive domestic infrastructure spending, aggressive restructuring of state-owned enterprises and financial sector reforms.

“No matter whether there is a minefield ahead of me or a deep abyss, I will charge forward, never looking back and dedicate myself wholeheartedly to the country until my last breath.” – Speaking at a press conference soon after his appointment as premier on March 19, 1998.

“The past year was extraordinarily tough for me – far tougher than I had anticipated. First, I did not expect the Asian financial crisis to hit with such severity. Second, the historic, devastating floods that struck our country also caught me off guard.” – Speaking at a 1999 press conference.

5. Backing Hong Kong’s future

Throughout his tenure as premier, Zhu was the face of Beijing’s support for Hong Kong in the wake of the 1997 handover, frequently reminding mainland and local officials alike of their historic duty to ensure the city’s success under the framework of “one country, two systems”.

“I just do not believe Hong Kong cannot be run well. If Hong Kong goes down the drain, it’s not just your responsibility – it’s ours, too. If Hong Kong, after returning to the motherland, gets ruined on our watch, wouldn’t that make us ‘sinners of the nation’? That will not happen.” – Remarks in Hong Kong on November 19, 2002.

6. Overhauling the tax system

Zhu presided over a landmark overhaul of China’s fiscal structure, introducing a tax-sharing system that divided powers and revenues between the central and local governments, cementing Beijing’s macroeconomic control.

By the early 1990s, China faced a severe fiscal crisis that threatened Beijing’s ability to govern effectively and could have undermined the country’s economic transition. Under the then fiscal contracting system, local governments collected revenues and negotiated fixed remittances to the central government, which often led to provinces hiding funds in off-budget channels to avoid sharing funds with Beijing. Deprived of money, the central government struggled to maintain macroeconomic control, manage regional disparities and fund national infrastructure projects.

Zhu Rongji spearheaded the landmark 1994 tax-sharing reform by intensively negotiating with reluctant provincial leaders to secure their compliance. The reform abolished discretionary negotiations in favour of a clear, rule-based tripartite tax scheme, with the newly created State Administration of Taxation collecting both central and shared taxes.

The results of the 1994 overhaul were immediate and transformative. In the first year, the central government’s share of total national tax revenues surged from 22 per cent to over 55 per cent, instantly revitalising Beijing’s fiscal capacity and cementing its authority over monetary and economic policy.

However, the reform left behind a persistent structural imbalance: while Beijing centralised revenue collection, local governments still had to pay for most public services. This mismatch eventually forced local authorities to rely heavily on off-budget financing, land sales and local government financing vehicles – creating fiscal challenges that China continues to navigate today.

“At present, the central government’s finances are in dire shape, nearly unsustainable. If the central government’s revenue is not properly concentrated and its financial resources are not strengthened, we will not be able to make our ends meet, and ultimately the whole country will suffer and everything will be stalled.” – Speaking to Guangdong province cadres during his tax reform research trip in September 1993.

7. Modernising the financial sector

Under Zhu’s watch, China launched sweeping reforms of its financial sector. He clarified the functions of the central bank, deepened reforms of commercial banks, foreign exchange systems and the securities and futures markets to create a unified and strictly managed financial system.

“If you are not diligent in your duties, nor honest in your work, indulging in wining and dining, issuing arbitrary approvals, practising nepotism, cultivating relationships everywhere, and treating state property as if it were yours, and you still sit on the podium giving a report, how can the people sitting down there not criticise you?” – Criticising financial officials’ misconduct at the 1993 National Financial Work Conference.

8. Persisting with painful SOE restructuring

Zhu placed SOE reform at the heart of China’s economic transition. Championing a modern enterprise system defined by “clear property rights and separated government and enterprise functions,” he drove a massive restructuring that controversially resulted in millions of lay-offs, while simultaneously pushing for a new social security net.

“State-owned enterprises only need a third of their current workforce … I refuse to believe that SOEs cannot perform just as well as foreign and private companies.” – Remarks made in 1999 during an interview with German newspaper Die Zeit.

9. Reshaping urban housing

In 1998, Zhu unveiled China’s housing blueprint, which aimed at replacing a system in which housing was mostly provided by the state with one in which private firms did the heavy lifting, laying the foundation for the urban housing market which later became a major driver of the Chinese economy.

“Right now, some cities are recklessly selling off land and carrying out mass demolitions without thinking through the consequences, completely ignoring the displaced residents. If this continues, it’s going to threaten social stability. The proceeds from land sales must first go towards rehousing evicted residents – the central government simply cannot afford to shoulder this burden.” – His handwritten comments on June 4, 1994, regarding the Ministry of Construction.

10. Championing WTO entry and Sino-US diplomacy

Zhu was perhaps the most important official behind China’s gruelling 13-year marathon to join the World Trade Organization (WTO), securing the country’s integration into the global economy. During his historic US visits, he deployed charm, wit and firmness to manage the nations’ complex ties.

“There will be differences of opinion between us. Yet only friends who hold differing views can be the best of friends; only honest critics make true friends.” – Speaking in the United States in April 1999

“When President Lincoln fought to preserve America and opposed secession, he did not hesitate to use force. We ought to learn from President Lincoln.” – Responding to a question from the press on Taiwan in Washington on April 8, 1999.

11. Crusading against corruption

Perhaps what endeared him most to the public was his fiery disdain for corrupt officials. He demanded integrity and famously staked his own life on the fight against corruption, during both his time in Shanghai and Beijing. He was also a strong advocate for simplified protocols, clean government and high efficiency.

“I have prepared 100 coffins here; 99 are for corrupt officials, and one is for myself. It’s a matter of mutual destruction, but it will bring long-term stability and development to the country and restore the people’s confidence in our cause.” – Giving a speech at a central anti-corruption conference in 1998.

“All I hope is that after I step down, the people will say, ‘He was an honest official, not a corrupt one,’ and I will be fully satisfied. If they can be a bit more generous and add, ‘Zhu Rongji actually got a few real things done,’ I’ll be very thankful.” – Conducting a press conference at the annual sessions of China’s top legislature and political advisory body, known as the two sessions, in March 2000.
