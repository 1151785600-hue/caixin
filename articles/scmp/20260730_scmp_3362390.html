Malaysia’s ‘shocking’ deal to return 5,000 Rohingya to Myanmar triggers refoulement fears

SCMP | FREE | 1055 words | 2026-07-30

Malaysia’s claim that Myanmar has agreed to take back 5,000 Rohingya has raised hopes of easing pressure on its strained refugee system, but has also alarmed activists over the risks of returning members of the persecuted minority group to a country still engulfed by conflict.

Rights groups insist any movement of refugees into Myanmar – where the military is fighting multiple conflicts and most Rohingya are denied citizenship – must be voluntary and backed by guarantees of safety, legal status and freedom of movement.

Wednesday’s announcement covers only a fraction of the 126,144 Rohingya registered with the United Nations refugee agency in Malaysia, but experts say it would mark a rare attempt to return refugees from a persecuted minority to their country of origin.

As of the end of February, Malaysia had 215,600 registered refugees and asylum seekers, nearly 194,000 of whom were from Myanmar – including Rohingya.

Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim announced the agreement at a campaign event in Negeri Sembilan on Wednesday, presenting it as a product of bilateral ties.

“People ask why we do not simply send them back. Send them where? Myanmar had refused to accept them before,” Anwar was quoted as saying by local media.

“We have good relations with Myanmar. They have now agreed to take 5,000 Rohingya from Malaysia,” he said, without identifying who gave the undertaking, where the refugees would be sent or whether they had agreed to return.

Debbie Stothard, coordinator of the pro-democracy group Altsean-Burma, said any coerced removal would breach the principle of non-refoulement, which prohibits sending people to a country where they face persecution or serious harm.

“It is absolutely shocking and incomprehensible that Malaysia is talking about, let alone thinking about, forcibly deporting asylum seekers from Burma,” she said. “This is essentially refoulement under international law.”

Stothard said returnees could face forced conscription, arbitrary detention, torture and forced labour, arguing that the violence that drove people from Myanmar had not abated.

There were 5,483 armed clashes and attacks on civilians, 1,625 civilian deaths and 1,416 air attacks in Myanmar during the first six months of 2026, according to figures compiled by Altsean-Burma from the Armed Conflict Location and Event Data project.

The UN said this month that more than 16 million people needed humanitarian help in Myanmar and nearly 3.8 million were displaced, as fighting, movement restrictions and blocked aid access deepened the crisis.

In Rakhine, the western state that most Rohingya fled, Myanmar’s military and the Arakan Army continue to fight for control of the territory.

Moe Thuzar, a senior fellow at Singapore’s ISEAS – Yusof Ishak Institute and coordinator of its Myanmar studies programme, said the military government was seeking to expand its diplomatic room and establish itself as the country’s principal international representative.

“The regime in Naypyidaw is actively seeking to widen its diplomatic space to assert itself as the primary interlocutor for Myanmar,” she said.

Thuzar said the Association of Southeast Asian Nations had been monitoring the Rohingya repatriation issue and associated humanitarian conditions since before the 2021 military coup, but had made little, if any, progress towards securing their safe and voluntary return.

“As the ground and administrative control of Rakhine state remains contested, and the question of the status and treatment of the Rohingya is still pending, existing concerns will continue about what ‘safe and voluntary return’ means in actual implementation,” she said.

Charles Santiago, co-chairman of Asean Parliamentarians for Human Rights, said Malaysia should not present the proposed return as a diplomatic achievement without explaining what awaited the refugees.

“Malaysia cannot, in good conscience, celebrate the return of 5,000 Rohingya to Myanmar without first answering one fundamental question: return them to what?” he said. “If there are no credible guarantees of safety, dignity, citizenship and voluntary return, this is not a diplomatic achievement to shout about but a moral failure.”

Refuge under strain

Anwar’s announcement came two days after Malaysian authorities detained more than 100 Rohingya who had gathered outside the UNHCR office in Kuala Lumpur seeking help. The group said they had been evicted from homes in the northern state of Penang.

Police released 109 of them after confirming they held valid UNHCR documents, relocating the group to addresses linked to relatives or contacts in Kuala Lumpur, Selangor and Penang.

The episode followed weeks of worsening hostility towards Rohingya in Malaysia, where online misinformation and hate speech have been blamed for harassment and the closure of community-run schools serving refugee children barred from entering the public education system.

Malaysia is not a signatory to the 1951 Refugee Convention and has no law formally recognising refugees, leaving UNHCR cardholders without the right to work or attend government schools, and vulnerable to arrest under immigration law.

The government last week asked UNHCR to suspend new registrations while it rolls out its own refugee registration document system.

Foreign Minister Mohamad Hasan later said Malaysia should reconsider the UN agency’s presence unless it accepted stricter joint screening.

“We want cooperation and strict screening,” he was quoted as saying. “If they do not want to cooperate, in my opinion, it is time for us to reconsider the presence of UNHCR in the country so that we do not continue to be burdened.”

The agency said it remained committed to supporting Malaysia’s national asylum framework and had consistently maintained that Rohingya returns must result from each refugee’s free and informed choice, and take place safely and with dignity.

This Week in Asia has reached out to Mohamad Hasan for comments.

About 1.2 million Rohingya are based in Bangladesh, mainly in refugee camps in Cox’s Bazar. Conflict and dwindling aid continue to drive their dangerous journeys across the Andaman Sea.

The UN said this month that more than 500 people, mostly Rohingya, were feared dead after two boats that left Rakhine were reported missing or capsized.

Mariko Hall, UNHCR’s spokeswoman for Asia and the Pacific, told This Week in Asia that the agency was “concerned” about reports of Rohingya refugees returning from Malaysia to Myanmar, but had not been involved any such discussions.

“Given the ongoing conflict across Myanmar, current conditions do not support the safe and sustainable return of refugees from Myanmar,” Hall said.

“Any refugee return must be voluntary, based on an informed decision, and take place in conditions of safety and dignity. UNHCR stands ready to engage with relevant authorities on protection safeguards.”
