How Asian shipyards in Japan, Korea and Philippines keep US warships battle ready

SCMP | FREE | 1455 words | 2026-09-05

After being stranded in the South China Sea following a power failure in July, the Arleigh Burke-class destroyer USS Benfold was towed to Subic Bay in the Philippines for emergency repairs.

The facility is among the repair and maintenance hubs the US Navy has been developing in the region, strategically pushing for allies to perform major maintenance, repair and overhaul (MRO) work on US naval vessels given the decline of its shipbuilding base at home.

The repair work on the Benfold in the Philippine port took about 10 days before it resumed operations.

While US law mandates that major warships be built domestically, outsourcing MRO to foreign yards offers more flexibility, especially for forward-deployed vessels.

Naval vessels homeported outside the United States are not strictly prohibited from receiving foreign MRO services and deployed US-homeported ships may also be serviced by foreign workers under specific conditions.

The capacity of regional allies in the western Pacific, including Japan and South Korea, is particularly vital, given their distance from the continental US as well as Washington’s intensifying strategic competition with Beijing.

“By establishing the ability to repair and sustain larger numbers of ships closer to potential conflict areas in the Indo-Pacific, the United States could potentially support extended deployments – including during wartime – by reducing the need to recall ships to the US mainland for repairs,” the Washington-based think tank Centre for Strategic and International Studies said in a recent report.

South Korea and Japan’s advanced shipbuilding industries rank second and third in the world, respectively, in terms of capacity. The Philippines and Singapore, meanwhile, occupy strategically important positions in the region and have long served as key partners for the US military.

Meanwhile, China at No 1 accounts for more than half the world’s shipbuilding capacity, providing a solid industrial base for its rapidly expanding navy.

Shipyards in the region

The US Navy runs the US Naval Ship Repair Facility and Japan Regional Maintenance Centre (SRF-JRMC) at its bases in the region. It is headquartered at the Yokosuka Naval Base in Kanagawa prefecture, with detachment sites in Sasebo, Nagasaki prefecture, and Singapore’s Changi Naval Base.

The organisation employs local workers and occasionally outsources certain tasks to local contractors.

In addition to the directly operated SRF-JRMC, the US Navy has been expanding a network of private companies in the region through its Master Ship Repair Agreement (MSRA). The certification qualifies a shipyard to bid on and perform MRO work on military and support vessels.

For instance, the US Navy issued solicitation notices last month, inviting bids from MSRA and Agreements for Boat Repair (ABR) holders to carry out minor repair and maintenance of the America-class amphibious assault ship USS Tripoli and San Antonio-class amphibious transport dock USS New Orleans, both homeported in Sasebo.

Japan

Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (MHI)’s Yokohama Dockyard & Machinery Works is the largest ship repair yard in Japan, with abundant experience in repairing civilian and naval vessels. It has two dry docks, seven piers and seven jib cranes. The dock can accommodate huge ships of up to 270,000 tonnes, according to the company.

The yard serviced the Arleigh Burke-class guided-missile destroyer USS Milius in 2019 as part of a pilot trial – the first time the US Navy had outsourced such work to local Japanese firms.

Last year, MHI became the first Japanese yard to secure a regular overhaul contract for the USS Miguel Keith, a Puller-class expeditionary mobile base.

Sumitomo Heavy Industries Marine & Engineering is another prominent MSRA holder. The company website says it has focused on repair work mainly for the US Navy in Yokosuka in recent years. They provide repair services to ships belonging to the Seventh Fleet, including nuclear-powered aircraft carriers, and “are engaged in repairing” US Army vessels including landing craft.

Sasebo Heavy Industries sits next to the Sasebo Naval Base and shares a dry dock with the US Navy and the Japan Maritime Self-Defence Force. Having suspended new shipbuilding in 2022, the company now focuses exclusively on ship maintenance and repair.

Japan Marine United Corporation operates five ship-repair yards across the country, at Isogo and Tsurumi in Yokohama, as well as Kure, Maizuru and Innoshima. The Maizuru facility took part in the repair of the Arleigh Burke-class destroyer USS Fitzgerald last December.

South Korea

Unlike in Japan, the US Navy does not have a direct ship-repair facility in South Korea. Instead, it collaborates with major local shipbuilders, primarily for logistical vessels. Notably, MSRA certifications for South Korean contractors have been mainly awarded in the past few years, reflecting the US Navy’s strategic shift towards MRO outsourcing.

In July 2024, Hanwha Ocean and HD Hyundai Heavy Industries (HHI) made history as the first South Korean shipyards to obtain MSRA from the US Navy, shortly after the former acquired the Philly Shipyard in Pennsylvania.

Hanwha Ocean conducts its repair works at the Geoje shipyard on an island off the southeastern coast near Busan.

It completed South Korea’s first MRO project – maintenance of the USNS Wally Schirra dry cargo and ammunition ship – in 2025 and also worked on the USNS Yukon, a replenishment oiler assigned to the US Navy’s 7th Fleet.

HHI secured the overhaul project for the Lewis and Clark-class dry cargo and ammunition vessel USNS Alan Shepard in August 2025 after efforts to tap into the US by forming an alliance with the US-based military shipbuilder Huntington Ingalls. The work was carried out at a berth near HD Hyundai Mipo Dockyard in the southeastern city of Ulsan from last September.

HJ Heavy Industries, a mid-sized shipbuilder, became the third South Korean firm to pass US Navy evaluations to get an MSRA in January this year, after signing a contract in December to carry out a midlife maintenance contract for the 40,000-tonne Lewis and Clark-class dry cargo ship USNS Amelia Earhart at its Yeongdo shipyard in Busan.

SK Oceanplant, another major defence contractor that has built more than 30 South Korean naval and coastguard vessels including the latest Ulsan-class Batch-III frigates, officially signed the MSRA in February. The company operates a 430-metre (1,410 foot) ultra-large floating dock in its shipyard in Goseong on the southern coast and conducts maintenance for more than 30 commercial vessels annually.

Samsung Heavy Industries, one of South Korea’s “Big Three” shipbuilders alongside Hanwha Ocean and HHI, has reportedly begun the MSRA application process and aims to complete certification this year.

Singapore

Strategically located at the entrance of the Strait of Malacca, the crossroads connecting the West Pacific and Middle East, Singapore has long been a hub for US naval operations. Along with the SRF-JRMC’s Singapore detachment, the US Navy primarily works with two local MSRA-holding partners.

State-owned defence contractor ST Engineering Marine operates facilities such as its Benoi and Gul yards. Its recent MRO contracts came not only from the Pacific Fleet, but also the Military Sealift Command and Naval Supply Systems Command. It was awarded a contract in February for the regular overhaul of Safeguard-class rescue and salvage ship USNS Salvor.

Government-linked Sembcorp Marine held an MSRA before its merger with Keppel Offshore & Marine in 2023 to form Seatrium Limited. Its Tuas Yard has a repair dock that is 400 metres (1,310 feet) long and 14 metres deep, one of the deepest in Asia for mega vessels, while the Admiralty Yard is equipped with multiple dry docks and quays, including one that is 13 metres deep.

India

The US Navy has also expanded cooperation with India against the backdrop of deepening defence ties under the framework of its Indo-Pacific strategy. It has certified Indian shipbuilders Mazagon Dock and Larsen & Toubro with MSRAs in 2023. Mazagon’s facilities are in Mumbai and Larsen & Toubro runs the Kattupalli Shipyard in Chennai.

In 2024, the third Indian MSRA was awarded to state-run Cochin Shipyard. Under these agreements, the Indian contractors will primarily support ships under the US Navy’s Military Sealift Command.

The Philippines

Although the Philippines lacks an advanced domestic shipbuilding industry, its strategic location as a key hub in the South China Sea has led the US Navy to contract US-owned firms to operate maintenance and repair facilities in the country.

The Agila Subic Shipyard in Subic Bay, where the USS Benfold arrived four days after losing power and was repaired in July and August, was formerly operated by Hanjin Heavy Industries Philippines (HHIC-Phil) and is now owned by New York-based Cerberus Capital Management.

Colorado-based defence contractor Vectrus (now V2X after a merger) provides services at the site, which features a 500-metre long dock and forms part of the growing network of overseas facilities supporting US naval operations in the region.
