Hong Kong guqin devotees embrace the ancient zither’s history, philosophy and craft

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1839 words | 2026-09-16

When civil servant Jackel Wan Chak-kui, 28, first heard a guqin being played, he was a young boy wandering through Chi Lin Nunnery, in Diamond Hill. He remembers being entranced by the instrument’s music, at once subtle and comforting. It seemed to envelop him like a warm embrace, complementing the natural beauty of the gardens, melding with stone, water, wood and stillness.

Years later, as a student at Baptist University, Wan chanced upon the sound again, while scrolling through YouTube. He delved deeper this time, yearning to learn more. He ordered his first guqin from Taobao (“Not a good one, but at least I could have a touch [of the instrument],” he says) and taught himself from scores found in the university library, learning where to place his fingers on the seven silk strings.

A seven-stringed zither, the guqin (“ancient stringed instrument”) was once the preserve of noblemen and royal courts, poets and scholars, and has been part of a musical tradition that has existed for more than 3,000 years in China, played in small, elite gatherings.

Since Unesco first recognised guqin music as part of humanity’s intangible cultural heritage in 2003, the seven-stringed zither has become more widely appreciated, with the instrument itself having been inscribed on China’s National List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2006. Virtuoso Chen Leiji played a specially composed piece on the guqin during the opening ceremony of the 2008 Beijing Olympics, reaching millions of viewers worldwide, and today, it’s commonly seen being played in concerts and online videos, with younger musicians being drawn to the instrument.

In Hong Kong, the guqin’s renaissance goes beyond simply renewed popularity, say scholars and players, many of whom construct their own. What they want to preserve are not just the rich sounds of an ancient instrument, but also a traditional way of life.

When it comes to the instrument and its music, “the guqin may be more accessible than before, but true revival is not just about more people playing it. It is about whether people understand its inner life: its craft, philosophy, subtle sound, cultural memory and intensely personal mode of expression”, Wan explains. “Because the more you understand Chinese culture, the more you realise that the guqin goes beyond music to explore other cultural aspects, such as Taoism, philosophy and history.”

A player’s understanding of those references can shape their interpretation of the music.

Wan gives as an example the piece Li Sao (“Encountering Sorrow”) – a lament to Qu Yuan (circa 339BC-278BC), the poet-politician whose exile, grief and moral idealism are central to one of the great works of Chinese literature, also titled Li Sao. To perform such a piece properly, Wan says, it is necessary to enter the emotional and philosophical world behind the notes, rather than just play them.

“It is like the first-, second-, third-person thing – the player might perform as if they are Qu Yuan, or they could be a storyteller, telling the story of Qu Yuan. Or [they could] consider themselves as humming the Li Sao prose,” says Wan, explaining that “to acquire these perspectives, one must cultivate not just the musicality, but also the culture, in a broad sense, of the qin”. He continues, “It is a good mix of understanding traditional culture and the current situation of ourselves.”

Players should learn to develop an appreciation for the guqin’s silences, too, says John Yiu Shek-on, an art historian and the deputy director of the Jao Tsung-I Petite Ecole academic research and cultural centre, at the University of Hong Kong.

“It has so much void – the space between the musical notes,” he says.

As a child in Hong Kong, Yiu played Western instruments, including the piano, but was later drawn to the guqin through its cultural and literary presence.

“I didn’t start with the music,” Yiu admits. “I actually started from writing about it, because it is a very revered instrument in Chinese poetry.”

He notes that the music’s silences remind him of “negative space”, referring to the intentionally untouched areas of silk or paper in Chinese paintings, which are not so much empty but alive with suggestion.

In 2005, he began learning to play the guqin and was soon appreciating the lingering pauses before notes, as part of the music’s emotional landscape. He found himself drawn not only to the instrument’s sound, but also to its refusal to overwhelm the listener – a quiet invitation to slow down and listen inwardly.

Unlike many old-school qin players, Yiu says, both private enjoyment and public performance matter to him. “I enjoy playing just for myself […] and actually, just last year, I had a concert with my teacher in Shanghai.”

Yiu is a member of the Deyin Qin Society, founded in 1998 by disciples of Tsar Teh-yun (1905-2007), a guqin master, poet and calligrapher who relocated to Hong Kong from Shanghai in the 1950s. “She is widely regarded as one of China’s last great literati,” says Yiu, whose own teacher learned from her directly.

Tsar was born in Huzhou, Zhejiang province, into what Yiu describes as a scholar-gentry family. She received an education in traditional arts, poetry and Kunqu – a polished, flowing style of singing that originated in Suzhou more than six centuries ago – and spent around 30 years teaching in Hong Kong. “Importantly, she did not teach for a living, and treated her students like friends. Many of her students later became established scholars or successful professionals. What united them was not only musical ability, but a temperament suited to the guqin; they’re all quite low-key people,” says Yiu.

Nowadays, the Deyin Qin Society has fewer than 80 members but “we hold regular, semi-private yaji [‘elegant gathering’] salons every two months. We also do public concerts sometimes”, at venues such as the Xiqu Centre and Hong Kong City Hall.

Appreciation of the seven-stringed zither is passed on through other intimate circles as well. Wee Lian-hee is a professor at the Academy of Language and Culture at Baptist University, and his initial encounter with the guqin came in the 80s.

Living in Singapore, he read about the seven-stringed zither while researching Chinese instruments, but struggled to find much information. It wasn’t until the early 2000s, while pursuing a PhD in linguistics in the United States, that he learned of Choi Chang-sau (1933-2025), the third-generation owner of Hong Kong’s Choi Fook Kee Musical Instrument Factory, a producer of Chiuchow and Hakka instruments, as well as violins and guitars. Choi was a foremost expert in crafting, restoring and preserving the guqin.

Established players choose their disciples with care. When Wee visited the master’s workshop, expressing an interest in learning how to make the instrument, he was advised to return after he had settled in Hong Kong. In 2001, Wee moved to the city for work but, preoccupied with his career, postponed his guqin studies.

In 2012, he sought out Lau Chor-wah, an esteemed guqin musician and philosopher, and a founding member of the Deyin Qin Society. Lau refused to teach him for two years, a test to see if he was worthy and genuinely interested. Eventually, he proved his dedication, and she agreed. Her teaching was strict and rigorous, says Wee, requiring weekly attendance at a fixed time, and the first three months were dedicated solely to learning how to string the instrument, a process that requires enormous focus and patience, and can take a novice hours. Precision and gentle finesse are required to maintain the correct tension on each silk string if the right pitch is to be attained.

With a recommendation from Lau, Wee was accepted by Choi as a student, although the master insisted that, to become a maker, one must first be an accomplished player. “Both playing the guqin and crafting it are equally fulfilling,” says Wee. He views both as integral to developing a connection to the instrument.

Of the 50 or so students who studied under Choi, as many as 10 are still making guqin in Hong Kong, among them Sou Si-tai, chairman of the Deyin Qin Society.

A guqin’s body is traditionally fashioned from thoroughly seasoned wood, typically that which has been dried in the shade for about a century. Wee explains that a range of wood is used for different parts of the qin: “The top board is typically paulownia or cedar, the bottom board is more varied and the inlaid parts may be various kinds of hardwood.” After careful selection, the wood is hollowed and shaped before layers of ash and lacquer are applied, polished and adjusted.

Constructing a guqin usually takes more than 200 person-hours, spread over nine painstaking steps that must be planned according to the season. Every detail affects the sound: the thickness of the boards, the curve of the body, the placement of the bridges, the tension of the strings and the way the surface responds to a player’s touch.

“Historically, the guqin is a niche instrument,” says Wee. “Sudden popularity has led to commercialisation and changes that should raise discussion.”

One area of contention is the use of metallic strings, which are louder, more stable and less expensive, but alter the instrument’s sound dynamics and are harsh on the fingers. Unsurprisingly, they are not favoured by purists.

Wee is unsure how many guqin he owns, with several still under construction. Although he appraises and repairs antique guqin for friends and collectors, he says he would never consider selling one of his own creations, as they are “my personal explorations into creating the sounds for my music”.

Says Wee, “It takes me an average of three years to make a guqin, so even if I were to imagine selling it, I wouldn’t know how to price it. I usually lend my instruments to like-minded friends and musicians for their playing pleasure.”

For his wife, he carved a guqin from the beam of a 200-year-old school in Yuen Long that was demolished, and decorated the instrument with a symbol representing his name, inlaid in sheep bone. Using material found in the vicinity of his village, in the Lau Fau Shan area, Wee fashions many of his guqin in memory of deceased pets. In one instance, the ashes of their dog Lily were incorporated into the guqin he made for his wife, an instrument that bears a carving of one of Lily’s paws.

Wan recently made his first qin, an undertaking he estimates took more than 200 hours. He shares that the process demanded a different kind of attention than that needed for playing. It meant learning to understand the instrument not only through sound, but through wood, lacquer, strings and touch, and to consider how small changes in shape, tension and surface alter the way it resonates.

“Both crafting and playing [the guqin] are a means of connecting with nature, to the world around you,” says Wan. “I’d say it’s a way of life.”
