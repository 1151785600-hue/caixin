South Korea hands police more power even as dead bodies expose the lies

SCMP | FREE | 1074 words | 2026-08-28

On a farm up in the hills of South Korea’s Jeju Island, investigators this week uncovered what a mother had spent three months fearing they would not find: the body of her daughter.

Jang Mi-ran, 37, vanished without a trace in May. Police records would later show her case had been quietly closed without anyone ever verifying if she was alive.

It was the second such discovery in a matter of days. The first was the skeletal remains of a man in his sixties, found on Saturday after his family warned officers he had spoken of suicide and left his children a farewell message.

Both cases had passed through the hands of the same officer, identified only by his surname Bu, who allegedly told worried relatives he had personally traced and spoken to the missing individuals – conversations investigators say never took place.

What began as a local scandal on the southern resort island has since turned into a far larger debate, on whether South Korea’s national police force is fit to inherit the sweeping investigative powers it is about to receive.

A president’s rebuke

“The recent breakdown in police discipline and irresponsibility in public safety matters have become serious problems,” President Lee Jae Myung told a cabinet meeting on Tuesday in response to the scandal, demanding the creation of a dedicated government body to police the police.

He spoke days after signing into law revisions to South Korea’s Criminal Procedure Act that will, from October, strip prosecutors of their remaining investigative powers and hand much greater authority to the police – the culmination of a decades-long campaign to cut the prosecution service down to size.

“Going forward, the police may exercise the greatest power among government agencies,” Lee said, noting that people were deeply concerned about the change.

“Power, when concentrated, always creates problems. The level of responsibility should be strengthened to match the extent of the authority.”

He acknowledged the public’s “scepticism” about whether the country’s police were equipped to wield such extensive authority responsibly – scepticism that has only grown as more details of the Jeju cases have come to light.

“Problems involving police discipline are not something that started yesterday or today,” Lee said. “Given the sheer size of the police force, problems can arise in many places, but we need both preventive measures and solutions.”

For the president, the failures were structural. “The police are a hierarchical organisation in which following orders is extremely important and command responsibility is therefore crucial,” he said.

“If a frontline officer is at fault, can the matter really end with disciplining that individual? If so, what is the command structure for? … Those who have the authority to command must also naturally bear responsibility commensurate with that authority.”

It’s high time for this country to overhaul the police

Choi Jin, Institute of Presidential Leadership

Compounding matters is a second scandal that unfolded in Gwangju, where a police inspector was arrested in July on suspicion of destroying evidence during the investigation of a high-profile murder whose suspect happens to be the son of a senior police official.

“These incidents reveal the shocking laxity in police discipline and accountability,” Choi Jin, head of the Institute of Presidential Leadership, told This Week in Asia.

“It’s high time for this country to overhaul the police, focusing on enhancing accountability and oversight, improving command and control, and strengthening investigative capabilities.”

Fabricated calls

Public anger has only grown since details of the Jeju disappearances began to emerge in mid-August.

Jang’s boyfriend reported her missing in May. Yet he says when he asked for updates, Bu told him she had been found and spoken to, but did not want her whereabouts shared

That was a fabrication, investigators allege: phone records show no such call was ever made. Bu is accused of instead deleting Jang’s file from the missing-persons system altogether, apparently to keep his unresolved-case count low.

An autopsy on the body recovered from the Jeju palm farm on Monday confirmed it was Jang. Police believe the circumstances suggest she may have taken her own life following a dispute with her boyfriend – the same dispute that had preceded her initial disappearance.

The man in his sixties suffered an almost identical fate. His family had explicitly warned police he was suicidal, but Bu allegedly closed that case too – again after supposedly claiming contact had been made.

The man’s remains were only located after the case was reopened.

Bu has since been placed under arrest and all four officer in his chain of command at Jeju Seobu Police Station have been suspended pending further investigations.

It has since emerged that Bu was previously investigated over an alleged bathroom intrusion and questioned in connected with suspected domestic violence, fuelling questions about how he remained on frontline duty.

‘Painful reform’

Analysts say the case reflects a structural blind spot rather than a single officer’s failures.

Lee Keon-su, a professor in Baekseok University’s Department of Police Science, pointed to a loophole: under current police rules, missing adults were typically treated as “runaways” – a category with non of the urgency reserved for cases involving people with intellectual disabilities or dementia.

“Sloppy initial responses, such as the false closure of Jang’s disappearance case, are entirely possible occurrences within the current system,” he said.

Bae Sang-hoon, a psychological analyst with more than 20 years’ experience in criminal profiling , argues that the fix must come from outside the chain of command itself.

“A system must be established to allow a third-party agency to look into and monitor police investigations into missing-person cases,” he said.

Political scientist Jhee Byong-kuen of Chosun University traced the deeper malaise to internal culture. He said promotion decision that hinged on the goodwill of superiors created fertile ground for exactly the kind of corner-cutting alleged in Jeju.

“Transparency and oversight are the two key words for police reform,” Jhee said.

The issue has since migrated to the National Assembly, South Korea’s parliament.

Han Byung-do, floor leader of the ruling Democratic Party, told a parliamentary meeting on Tuesday that “painful reform” was needed.

“The police are now at the final test of whether they can regain the public’s trust,” Han said.

For a force about to inherit more power than it has held in decades, the pressure is on to rebuild that relationship.
