Will Philippines’ push for seabed rights derail South China Sea code talks?

SCMP | FREE | 1018 words | 2026-08-03

The Philippines’ latest push for seabed rights in the South China Sea could complicate its efforts as this year’s Asean chair to build regional consensus, analysts say, adding another layer of complexity to already slow-moving talks on a code of conduct for the disputed waterway.

On July 28, Manila delivered an official presentation of its partial submission on its extended continental shelf in waters the Philippines considered its territory to the UN Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf, the Department of Foreign Affairs (DFA) said on Wednesday.

In a statement, Rogelio Villanueva Jnr, DFA assistant secretary and spokesman for maritime affairs, said the submission “was a demonstration of the Philippines’ steadfast commitment to the United Nations Charter” and the 1982 UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (Unclos).

Article 76 of the convention provides the basis for coastal states such as the Philippines to establish the outer limits of their continental shelf, including the seabed and subsoil of submarine areas extending beyond 200 nautical miles (370km) but not more than 350 nautical miles from their baselines.

Such submissions concern seabed and subsoil rights rather than sovereignty over disputed islands or waters, and the UN commission’s role is to assess the scientific and technical basis of a claim before making recommendations.

“The presentation further underscored that the submission was part of the country’s responsibility to secure a peaceful South China Sea through the rule of law in order to sustain the livelihoods of Filipinos, foster trade, protect marine biodiversity, and unite coastal and littoral States,” the DFA said in its statement.

Overlapping claims

Questions remain over how the UN commission will handle the submission in contested South China Sea waters, where the Philippines’ assertion and overlapping ones by Southeast Asian neighbours sit alongside China’s sweeping claim over nearly the entire waterway under its 10-dash line.

In 2016, an arbitral tribunal in a case brought by the Philippines found no legal basis for China’s historic-rights claims in the disputed sea. Beijing has dismissed the ruling as “illegal, null and void”, arguing the tribunal exceeded its jurisdiction.

In response to Manila’s UN presentation, Beijing accused the Philippines of “flagrantly infringing” on Chinese sovereignty and undermining regional peace.

It also said Manila was using the submission “as a pretext to hype up” the 10-year-old arbitral award.

Analysts caution that the Philippines’ UN presentation remains an early step in a long process.

“The recent presentation before the Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf represents an important procedural milestone, but it is not yet a final recognition of the Philippine claim,” said Gary Ador Dionisio, dean of the De La Salle – College of Saint Benilde’s School of Diplomacy and Governance.

“The scientific and technical evaluation process remains ongoing and may take several years, particularly in areas where overlapping claims have been formally raised.”

The Philippines’ claim will undergo several steps under the commission’s rules of procedure, including a scientific and technical examination to assess whether it meets Article 76, a formality check and publication for three months to allow other states to provide comments.

Darwin Angeles, a lecturer at the University of the Philippines’ College of Law, said fulfilling these steps was the crucial part, noting that a subcommittee would be formed to prepare recommendations on the claim based on scientific and technical evaluation and the comments of other states.

In 2024, Malaysia and Vietnam lodged their own protests against the Philippines’ submission.

Kuala Lumpur sent its own diplomatic note rejecting Manila’s claim, saying the Philippines’ identification of its extended continental margin was projected from the baselines of Sabah and “clearly disregards Malaysia’s indisputable sovereignty over the state of Sabah”.

Manila has a long-standing dormant claim over Sabah, a dispute rooted in historical ties to the Sultanate of Sulu in the southern Philippines.

Vietnam filed its own extended continental shelf claim a month after the Philippines’ move but said it was willing to discuss mutually beneficial solutions with Manila.

Given Malaysia and Vietnam’s claims, the UN commission “may consider this a dispute under Unclos and therefore, it may just make a decision on the non-contentious portions in accordance with Rule 46 of the Rules of the UN Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf”, Angeles said.

“The disputing states may raise their respective claims for adjudication by the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea.”

Code complication

Analysts say the Philippine submission would not derail talks on a South China Sea code of conduct – a long-delayed Asean-China framework meant to set rules for behaviour in the disputed waterway and reduce the risk of confrontations – but could make it harder for the bloc to agree on a common position.

“Malaysia and Vietnam are expected to continue protecting their respective legal positions, particularly where overlapping claims exist,” Dionisio said.

“Such diplomatic protests are normal and should not be interpreted as a breakdown of Asean solidarity. Instead, they underscore the importance of sustained dialogue and legal diplomacy.”

Joanne Lin, a senior fellow at the ISEAS – Yusof Ishak Institute, told This Week in Asia that while the Philippines’ submission “should not directly derail” code of conduct negotiations, “it highlights the difficulty of forging a common Asean position when member states hold overlapping maritime claims and differing legal interests”.

Lin said this could complicate consensus on other sensitive code-related issues, particularly its geographical scope, and place a greater burden on the Philippines as Asean chair to separate its national position from its regional role “and act as an honest broker”.

“Ultimately, the main obstacles to concluding the COC [code of conduct] remain the long-standing disagreements between Asean and China over its substance, legal character and implementation rather than the Philippine submission itself,” she said.

Dionisio stressed the Philippines’ “dual responsibility” as Asean chair was to “firmly uphold its sovereign rights while maintaining its credibility as an honest broker capable of advancing regional consensus”.

He said: “The appropriate path forward is continued bilateral and trilateral consultations, technical engagement among claimant states, and adherence to Unclos mechanisms without prejudicing future maritime boundary negotiations.”
