Hong Kong’s 1,000-year-old tea heritage is being rediscovered and rehabilitated

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 3275 words | 2026-08-08

Ask a Hongkonger where the city’s tea story begins, and they will probably point to the British – the colonial traders who funnelled Chinese leaves through Victoria Harbour to the world.

Only, they would be off by about eight centuries.

Most Hongkongers today don’t know the city ever had its own tea plantations, let alone that tea had been growing here long before the British arrived in the mid-19th century. Li In, a co-founder of the Hong Kong Tea Production Institute (HKTPI) who has practised the art of tea-making for more than a decade, found out in 2017 – in a London classroom, at the UK Tea Academy.

“I was born and raised in Hong Kong and it took a British instructor, Jane Pettigrew, mentioning tea gardens in Lantau for me to first hear about [Hong Kong’s tea heritage],” she says.

Ngong Ping Tea Garden, the plantation in question, on Lantau Island, was founded in 1948 by Brook Bernacchi, a British lawyer and politician who arrived in Hong Kong as a marine with the World War II liberation forces in 1945. He built a life on the plateau near the Po Lin Monastery. His Lotus Brand was marketed as “the only Hong Kong tea”.

By the early 1980s, unable to compete with mass production, the tea plantation area was downsized. After cycling through different hands and functions – a recreational space, a restaurant – the site was eventually abandoned in 2014, its remnants still sitting beneath a pine tree, on a trail south of the Big Buddha – a colonial relic.

Li went looking for it anyway. In March 2023, she and a small group of enthusiasts harvested what they could from the site’s overgrown tea bushes and processed them into a small batch of red and green teas. It didn’t amount to much, but it proved one thing: tea was still growing in Hong Kong, decades after Lotus Brand had folded and the city had moved on.

Li was determined to take local tea cultivation seriously, but she needed more than a ruin. Four months later, her search took her to the upper slopes of Tai Mo Shan, to Kadoorie Farm and Botanic Garden.

Established in 1956 by the Kadoorie brothers as an agricultural aid station for struggling farmers, the 148-hectare sanctuary is widely recognised for its “nature as a classroom” model, welcoming visitors to see its resident animals, wander the terraced gardens and breathe air that doesn’t taste like exhaust fumes. Less known is the fact that Kadoorie has been quietly growing tea on its property since the early 2000s.

Kadoorie’s plantation wasn’t always hidden in plain sight: the farm first brought tea plants over from Ngong Ping in the 70s and 80s, strictly for exhibition. In 2004, the decision to actually grow tea was made, born of a pragmatic desire to fully use the available land. At 400 metres above sea level, the mountainside terrace is a difficult site, plagued by strong winds, thin soil and no reliable water source. Vegetables would struggle in these conditions, the team at Kadoorie assessed. But the same terrain was ideal for tea.

“It was about picking the right plant for the right place,” says Yip Tsz-lam, Kadoorie’s regenerative agriculture manager, who has worked at the farm since 2003. “Of course, we also wanted to continue Hong Kong’s centuries-old tea culture.”

Single-lane and steep, the road up to the terraces is a series of hairpin turns. It takes about 10 minutes by car, before the incline levels out. On our visit, Tai Mo Shan lives up to its colloquial name – “Big Mist Mountain” – its slopes wrapped in clouds so dense the tea bushes in the distance vanish into white.

It’s the kind of climate tea plants dream about. China’s great tea-growing regions, such as Fujian and Yunnan provinces, thrive in similar environments. Mist diffuses the sunlight, slowing photosynthesis to give the leaves time to accumulate amino acids, the compounds that become sweetness and complexity in the cup.

On these terraces are three cultivars, all sourced from Fujian: Ming-fa, Wong-chi-heung and Purple Bud Buddha’s Hand. Many people assume green and red teas come from different plants, when, in fact, they all belong to the same species; the difference is in the processing. Likewise, herbal teas are not tea at all. Only leaves from Camellia sinensis qualify; the rest are tisanes (herbal infusions). What Kadoorie is growing is the real thing.

Of the 2,000 bushes planted in 2004, about half died in the first few years. “We lacked experience with the terrain,” says Yip. But the survivors spread into the gaps and the yield held steady.

Growing the tea, however, was just the start. Kadoorie’s first harvest, in 2008, caught the team off guard. “Our initial amateur attempts resulted in terrible-tasting tea,” Yip recalls.

Kadoorie needed help, and found it closer than they expected. A colleague at the farm’s apiary introduced his mentor, Wong Tung-pui. Si Gung (“Grandmaster”), as he was known, was a Hakka man from Huizhou, in Guangdong province, who came to Hong Kong in the 70s. In his youth, he managed a tea plantation on the mainland and had learned the Hakka tradition of pan-frying leaves by hand.

When Kadoorie approached him, Si Gung was no longer working in agriculture – he kept bees and grew plants for pleasure in the countryside – but he still had the knowledge. “We were lucky to find him,” Yip says. “At that time, finding anyone in Hong Kong who still knew how to manually fry and process tea was incredibly difficult; the broader tea industry, including in mainland China, had already shifted almost entirely to machine-processing.”

Si Gung passed on the pan-frying tradition of his people, a rare skill that has faded even within Hakka communities. Kadoorie adopted it by default. “It wasn’t a planned cultural preservation effort,” Yip says. “It was born out of sheer necessity.”

For the next 15 years, Kadoorie’s tea operation ticked along quietly, following the rhythm of the 24 terms in the solar calendar. Between the winter solstice and Ching Ming in early April, the plants were pruned and fertilised, then the leaves were harvested until the end of the rainy season, usually around the autumn equinox. A limited amount was sold at the farm shop. Kadoorie never thought to promote the tea; they saw no reason to draw attention to something that still felt unfinished.

Connecting with Li and her HKTPI co-founders, Chan Tsz-ting and Season Li Tin-on, gave Kadoorie the downstream partner it needed. Kadoorie would manage the tea-growing and HKTPI would take the leaves further than ever before, experimenting with oxidation levels, producing multiple styles from a single cultivar and bringing the results to the public through workshops. Without them, “a lot of the tea’s potential would be wasted”, Yip says.

Li developed a method she calls “guerrilla tea-making” to process the leaves. On harvest days, the initial withering took place on the farm, in the sunlight. By dusk, the leaves were packed and taken home. In her living room, with no climate-control equipment, she relied entirely on her senses: watching the leaves, feeling the humidity, adjusting each step. “My job is not to decide what the tea tastes like,” she says. “It’s to show what the land, the weather and the leaves are giving us.”

With just the Ming-fa cultivar, Li produced several teas with different characters, evoking Hong Kong’s history as a crossroads of tea cultures, where the toasted-rice notes of Hakka pan-fried green, the floral sweetness of British-style black tea, the intense fragrance of Chaozhou Phoenix Dancong and the roasted oolongs of Fujian had come together and been absorbed.

When Li gave a finished red-tea variant to Ip Wing-chi, founder of Lok Cha Tea House and a doyen of Hong Kong’s tea world, he likened it to Taiwan’s Oriental Beauty. Also known as Dongfang Meiren, Oriental Beauty is one of the most expensive oolong teas in the world – harvests can sell for thousands of dollars per kilogram – so this was no small compliment. But why would a red tea from Tai Mo Shan carry that same oolong note at all?

Plot 107, where the leaves were plucked, held the answer: the buds there were stunted, fibrous and difficult to process. “We investigated and found tea green leafhoppers,” says Li.

Barely visible, the leafhopper’s bite causes the plant to release a chemical defence with a honeyed fragrance: the signature of Oriental Beauty. In Taiwan’s Miaoli and Hsinchu regions, farmers deliberately cultivate these insects. A pest, under the right conditions, became a gift for the tea-makers.

Li had not expected to find leafhoppers thriving on Tai Mo Shan. Warmer temperatures, driven by climate change, scrambled harvest seasons, but it also made the insects more prolific.

“It’s a double-edged sword,” Li says. Leafhoppers trigger the sweet aroma, but they reduce yields to one-fifth of a standard harvest. For a farm already operating at micro scale, the numbers were punishing.

But the trade-off posed an important question: “Do we want volume or quality? Hong Kong has the means and should pursue quality. If we could only produce a plain, characterless tea, producing it here would be meaningless.”

Li does not claim perfection for the “Hong Kong Oriental Beauty”, but the experts have given their verdict. Dr Chen You-ren, former director of the Taiwan Tea Research and Extension Station, and Dr Kim Young-suk, a Korean tea studies professor, were satisfied with the quality. It matters to Li that the validation comes from Asia’s tea-making heartlands: “It gave us confidence that Hong Kong tea can reach the international artisan stage.”

But Li had more in mind than just making good tea – she wanted to know its origins. HKTPI co-founder Chan, a tea historian, had dug through academic papers and Qing dynasty gazetteers in an attempt to map out where Hong Kong’s earliest tea gardens used to be.

His findings rewound the clock by centuries. Hong Kong Society Before the Colonial Era, a 1990 textbook by historian Siu Kwok-kin, described tea cultivation in the New Territories during the Ming dynasty (1368-1644). By 2019, Siu had revised his position, arguing for Song dynasty origins, and Chan found further evidence: Southern Song (1127-1279) records of tea-growing clans migrating to the New Territories and Northern Song (960-1127) tea-ware excavated at Sung Wong Toi, in Kowloon. Compare that to the start of British colonial rule, in 1841 – tea was never just a footnote in Hong Kong’s history.

During the Qing dynasty (1644-1911), the written record started to catch up. An account in the 17th century Guangdong Xinyu encyclopaedia described tea-growing on the cliffs of Bui Dou Saan – present-day Castle Peak – with “the scent of orchid and jasmine, sweet and fragrant”.

By the Jiaqing period (1796-1820), the Xin’an County Gazetteer, a pre-colonial record of the region, had listed four famous teas grown locally: Phoenix Tea, from Lantau’s Phoenix Mountain, harvested by indigenous villagers and prized for its cooling properties; Mung Saan Tea, from Castle Peak, steeped in cloud mist and said to rival its famous Sichuan province namesake; Daam Gon Tea, from Tsing Yi, known for aiding digestion; and Ching Ming Tea, from Tai Lam, a fermented, medicinal tea preserved with salt.

Chan’s research included a 1986 paper by Dr Patrick Hase, a historian specialising in the New Territories. It described Hakka families in Mau Tso Ngam, a village in the foothills of Fei Ngo Shan, who still grew tea, sustaining two centuries of continuous cultivation.

In December 2024, the co-founders went on a “treasure hunt” to Mau Tso Ngam, along the MacLehose Trail, timed to the flowering season of Camellia sinensis so its white blossoms would make surviving tea trees visible. What they found was not a ruin.

A handful of elders remained in Mau Tso Ngam, along with mature tea trees planted decades and centuries ago, by mothers, grandmothers and ancestors. Cheng Kwun-ming, the village head, walked the visitors through the trees, pointing: this one, his mother planted 50 years ago; that one, a woman in her 80s told them, had been there since her father-in-law was alive. He used to carry the leaves across Fei Ngo Shan to Hong Kong Island to sell. Stories unspooled, rooted in the soil, still growing.

One of the villagers still harvesting and processing the leaves is Cheng Hung-ying. Now in her 70s, “Sister Hung” has picked and pan-fried tea since her teens. In her 30s, she cleared a patch of bamboo forest on the slope behind Mau Tso Ngam and planted tea trees. “Her mother could only drink the village’s own tea,” Li says. “So, that’s why she started. It was an act of care.”

“Cheng [the village head] always asks me the same question,” Li recalls. “‘What do you taste?’ And I tell him about the aroma, the sweetness, the body. And he says, ‘No. It’s yan ching mei [the taste of connection and community].’”

Somewhere along the way, Li’s search for Hong Kong’s forgotten tea had become a search for herself. Last year, while consulting with Lam Man-ying, director of the Hong Kong Hakka Association, to gather context on Mau Tso Ngam, the conversation turned to her family background. As they mapped out her grandfather’s village, the pieces clicked: Li is of Hakka descent. She has, unknowingly, spent years chasing her own heritage.

Li keeps returning to Mau Tso Ngam. On another visit, Sister Hung shows her the Hakka pan-frying process from start to finish.

Moving through her tea trees with ease and familiarity, Sister Hung demonstrates how to pick the bud and the two youngest leaves, pinched clean with a flick of the wrist.

Back in the village, at an outdoor wok set-up weathered by years of use, she gets the fire going in just a few minutes, using wood she chopped herself, from behind the small row of houses, most of them now uninhabited. In the three hours it takes to pan-fry the leaves, one person tends the fire, the other keeps the leaves moving to stop them from burning. She uses no thermometer, no timer – only her hands and decades of instinct.

With the wok at around 300 degrees Celsius, Sister Hung works fast to saat ching (“kill the green”), using a Y-shaped wooden tool passed down from her father. Next, she rolls the leaves back and forth on a bamboo withering tray with her gloved palms – the same motion as kneading dough – until the cell walls break down and the juices coat the surface, so the flavour can build.

Finally, the leaves return to the wok at a lower heat. With her bare hands, she turns them, lifting and dropping, pressing and spreading, for the better part of two hours. If she loses the rhythm, the batch is ruined.

Sister Hung no longer lives in Mau Tso Ngam year-round, but she comes back to pan-fry tea and make alkaline rice dumplings, another Hakka tradition recognised as part of Hong Kong’s intangible cultural heritage. She is the last person in her family who knows how to do it.

“My children didn’t want to learn; they don’t even want to try the tea I pan-fry any more,” she says. “I come back every year anyway. It has become a ritual for me.”

Li, who trained in Taiwan, Japan and Britain, understands the science of oxidation, amino acids and catechins. But what Sister Hung knows is held in the body, passed down through years of watching and copying until the skill becomes instinct.

“My job is to understand her, to turn what she knows into something we can replicate, to carry forward the culture,” says Li.

When the village head learned that Li and her team worked with Kadoorie, he welcomed them without hesitation. In the 50s and 60s, the Kadoorie Agricultural Aid Association supported Hakka farmers across the New Territories; the trust already existed.

HKTPI is reviving that decades-old partnership for a new purpose. Mau Tso Ngam’s tea trees represent a living gene bank of cultivars that have adapted to Hong Kong’s terroir. If the ageing tea-makers can’t tend to them, the genetic knowledge will be lost.

Last November, HKTPI invited the villagers to Kadoorie Farm and Botanic Garden to plant saplings from Mau Tso Ngam’s heritage trees, bringing their cultivars into Kadoorie’s conservation infrastructure.

“We’re not reviving a lost craft,” Li says. “We’re a bridge. One end connects to the tea traditions Hakka ancestors cultivated; the other end connects to the present, the land, what’s still here.”

HKTPI and Kadoorie now co-host workshops where the public can harvest leaves from the terraces and try their hand at traditional pan-frying. At the Hong Kong International Tea Fair 2024, they poured cups of Tai Mo Shan tea for visitors who did not believe the city could produce anything worth drinking.

Some visitors asked what the point was. Even if the tea were good, what did a few kilograms from a mountainside matter? It’s true that Kadoorie’s harvests are too limited to sustain widespread commercial production. But Chan has greater ambitions than just retail growth: “Our dream is that one day, when foreign dignitaries visit the city, the tea they are served is ours.”

Inspired by her findings, Li is building another stage: the Hakka Tea Theatre, a clear expression of her role as a “translator”. Here, the tea served is Hakka pan-fried green, the food is Hakka yellow-wine chicken, the stories are from Mau Tso Ngam and the crafts are local. But the format is British afternoon tea.

The project mirrors her own trajectory, from running a British-style tearoom in Mong Kok, training overseas in various tea cultures, to serving tea at a consulate in London. She understands both lexicons – the formal precision of a Western tea service and the embodied knowledge of a Hakka village. At the Tea Theatre, the two meet. “I’m not asking people to choose between Chinese and Western,” she says. “I’m showing them that the tea doesn’t belong to either. It belongs to the land it came from.”

Chan thinks about the future in two timelines. In five years, HKTPI will have the resources to sustain the partnership with Kadoorie, safeguard tea-making traditions in Mau Tso Ngam, explore other forgotten tea plantations and stage more Hakka Tea Theatre experiments. In 10 years, a younger generation will join them, “to inherit the culture, the technique and the vision of this land”.

Li had learned, through a serendipitous journey, that this land belongs to her, too. She had started in a London classroom, followed the trail through an abandoned colonial plantation, up a misty mountain and into a tiny village that had never stopped making tea, and found herself inside the story she was chasing.

“Letting the next generation know that Hong Kong has this history is not just about tea,” Li says. “It’s the confidence of knowing that we are not rootless; we carry something we can be proud of.”
