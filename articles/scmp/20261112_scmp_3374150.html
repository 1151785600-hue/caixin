Gulf allies’ Trump ties hand a hostile US Congress subpoena targets

SCMP | FREE | 1061 words | 2026-11-12

Washington’s key Gulf allies are expected to come under scrutiny as Democrats use their new congressional majorities to investigate business dealings involving US President Donald Trump and his inner circle.

Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates and Qatar could all be dragged into congressional investigations over allegations that Trump, his family and influential members of his administration abused their positions to enrich themselves in return for political favours, according to observers.

Democrats, who won control of the House and the Senate in last week’s midterm elections, have “made it clear” that their inquiries would “focus on business arrangements as a way of following up on voter concerns about corruption”, said Kristian Coates Ulrichsen, a Middle East fellow at Rice University’s Baker Institute for Public Policy.

The investigations would seek information on the workings of Trump administration officials and others close to the White House since January 2025, he said. Gulf officials “will be watching closely” to see if they are linked to any inquiries once the new Congress convenes on January 3, according to Ulrichsen.

Kenneth Katzman, a former senior analyst with the Congressional Research Service, said the Gulf monarchies’ ties to Trump’s special envoys – his son-in-law Jared Kushner and long-time business associate Steve Witkoff – were “certainly going to be front and centre in certain investigations” into whether they profited from their roles as political brokers and diplomatic mediators.

With Trump scheduled to leave office in January 2029, Democrats are expected to use the hearings to “position themselves” ahead of the November 2028 US presidential election, observers say.

But Katzman, now a senior Middle East fellow at the Soufan Centre think tank in New York, doubted that investigations into alleged corruption among Trump’s associates would “move the needle” on US policy. Nor did he expect Trump to remove Kushner and Witkoff as mediators in the Gaza, Iran and Ukraine conflicts.

Subpoena power

Andreas Krieg, an associate professor of defence studies at King’s College London, noted that Democrats “now have something they previously lacked”: subpoena power in both the Senate and the House of Representatives.

He expected an “aggressive investigation into the intersection of Gulf money, Trump family business interests and American foreign policy”.

The central question, according to Krieg, will be whether private commercial relationships created privileged access to decisions involving national security.

“Democrats will want to establish who paid whom, when decisions were made and whether commercial interests influenced policy,” he said.

The UAE

Krieg described the UAE as “particularly exposed” because shortly before Trump returned to office, investors linked to Emirati national security adviser Sheikh Tahnoon bin Zayed al-Nahyan reportedly acquired a 49 per cent stake in Trump’s World Liberty Financial (WLF) for US$500 million.

The deal “creates an obvious line of inquiry, especially alongside subsequent American decisions” on advanced technology and defence cooperation with Abu Dhabi, he said.

A landmark agreement in May 2025 established a framework for expanded bilateral collaboration on artificial intelligence, semiconductors and digital infrastructure. Washington and Abu Dhabi also formalised a major defence partnership that same month.

In a CBS News interview aired on Sunday, Trump claimed to “know nothing” about his Emirati partners in WLF. “My sons run the company. It’s hard to say to them, ‘Don’t do anything any more for four years because I’m president,’” he said.

Financial disclosures filed by Trump earlier this year show his businesses received about US$300 million in payments from Gulf entities in 2025.

Gulf projects accounted for nearly two-thirds of the US$59.5 million in foreign real estate licensing income – a figure 10 times higher than in 2023, CNBC reported in August.

Saudi Arabia

Analysts said Trump’s July agreement to grant Saudi Arabia access to American nuclear power technology, without safeguards against uranium enrichment, was also likely to come under the congressional microscope.

Krieg predicted a “sustained campaign” of hearings, restrictions, demands for additional safeguards and attempts to delay sensitive transfers.

Trump’s September decision to sell 48 F-35 stealth fighters to Riyadh presented “another obvious target”, he said, raising concerns over military technology, Israel’s qualitative military edge in the region, Saudi Arabia’s human rights record and Trump’s broader relationship with Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman.

Krieg expected both agreements to face “serious political resistance” on Capitol Hill, though he said delaying or attaching strict conditions would “be easier than cancelling them outright”.

As in the UAE’s case, both agreements with Saudi Arabia followed a major business deal involving the Trump family. In January, Saudi real estate developer Dar Global unveiled plans to build two Trump-branded luxury projects in Riyadh and Jeddah valued at US$10 billion.

Alongside the massive Saudi investment commitments and the sensitive defence and nuclear pacts, the Trump-branded developments “create an uncomfortable appearance of overlapping commercial and strategic interests”, Krieg said.

The nuclear deal was “particularly vulnerable”, he said. Democratic senators Ed Markey and Jeff Merkley introduced a resolution opposing it in October. Significantly, Republican Senator Rand Paul has separately introduced a resolution opposing the F-35 sale, showing that congressional resistance was “not exclusively Democratic”, Krieg said.

Ulrichsen suggested the civilian nuclear agreement might draw greater Democratic scrutiny than the F-35 sale. Democrats “may not want to be seen as wavering on commitments to strengthen regional security partnerships” to offset uncertainty triggered by the Trump administration’s launch of the Iran war, he said.

Qatar

Krieg said Qatar was also expected to face congressional scrutiny over its investments in Trump’s real estate business and its donation of a US$400 million Boeing 747 retrofitted as an Air Force One, “although its diplomatic usefulness provides a degree of political protection”.

None of the arrangements with the three wealthy Gulf monarchies “establishes a corrupt exchange by itself”, Krieg said, but congressional investigations “can be politically devastating without producing criminal convictions”.

Ulrichsen said the prospect of a deeply divided Washington, coupled with the uncertainty surrounding the post-Trump transition, would “likely only reinforce and accelerate geopolitical hedging across the Middle East”.

Regional states were expected to diversify their political and defence ties “not to replace or supplant, but to complement the role of the US”, he said.

Krieg agreed that Washington remained “militarily indispensable” to Gulf states.

“What is changing is the ability of regional governments to assume that a personal relationship with the president will protect them from the rest of Washington,” he added.
