Scott Kennedy on China’s ‘slow tech dragon’ and 2 more Xi-Trump meetings

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 4194 words | 2026-09-29

Scott Kennedy is a senior adviser and trustee chair in Chinese business and economics at the Centre for Strategic and International Studies. His areas of focus include China’s hi-tech drive, US-China relations and global economic governance.

Open Questions first appeared in SCMP Plus. For other interviews in the Open Questions series, click here.

How would you characterise the outcomes of the China-US summit compared to expectations going in? Is it largely a continuation of that track or do you see it moving the relationship a few steps forward?

The summit continued the pathway set in May and, in terms of specific deliverables, met the low expectations that observers had going in. Most notably, they agreed to extend the economic ceasefire on tariffs and export controls, but only for two months, to January 10, 2027.

That will provide a little reassurance to financial markets and to businesses that trade and invest between the US and China and elsewhere. But that’s still just four months away, and as we get closer to January, if nothing is resolved by November or December, people will get more worried.

On the trade front, they agreed to cut tariffs on US$30 billion of products on each side and encourage trade of specific non-sensitive products, such as agricultural goods, fish and seafood, medical devices, and consumer goods.

They also agreed, according to the US side, to refer to artificial intelligence as “super intelligence” and create a notification system of serious incidents. [Chinese President] Xi [Jinping] emphasised the importance of keeping AI under human control in his opening remarks, views that were reaffirmed in subsequent Chinese statements.

The other significant outcome mentioned by both sides is their common commitment that Iran should not be permitted to have a nuclear weapon and that no one should be able to charge tolls for international waterways. Another point of emphasis during the discussions, though not mentioned in the fact sheets, was Taiwan, with Beijing trying to further pocket what they saw as adjustments in US language and actions coming out of the summit in Beijing.

But the summit’s deliverables were still quite modest. As with Beijing, there was much more pomp than substance.

US officials have said Beijing proposed a “bigger deal”. What do you think China means by that? And what would Beijing want in return?

We would need an explanation from either side, but one could hypothesise that the Chinese were looking to extend the commercial ceasefire to the end of the Trump administration, and in return would potentially offer larger and longer commitments to purchase American products, whether that would be commercial aircraft, agriculture, energy, or other goods. And they might have an offer to expand the amount of goods on each side that were tariffed at normal or below-normal levels.

China could also have proposed an investment arrangement with specific deals identified, pilots, or a mechanism to evaluate new Chinese investment. All of that could have been part of a larger deal. US President [Donald] Trump said that coming out of one of the meetings [with Xi], the two sides had made “tremendous strides”, but based on the fact sheets, no such big deal was reached.

You mentioned what China might want out of the summit. Are the two leaders just building momentum for when they meet again, say at the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (Apec) or Group of 20 (G20) summits later this year to announce a bigger deal?

China may be holding back on some things for Apec and the G20, and President Trump may also be. But I think China would have been totally satisfied if they had made a big deal in Washington. China’s goal is to keep the current relationship in this form, for as long as they can. The kinds of things they are likely offering to keep the trade peace are not giant sacrifices to China. None would require China to make any adjustments whatsoever in economic strategy or industrial policy. No attention is being paid to the thorny issue of imbalances. So, China would be happy to make this kind of deal any time, whether it had been in Washington or on the sidelines of the Apec leaders’ meeting or the G20 summit.

So what limited the two sides from announcing a bigger deal in Washington?

At a minimum, it was the US side and their concerns about obstacles obtaining rare earths. Over the past year, China’s rare earth exports globally have risen but they have fallen towards the US by about 25 per cent and dropped dramatically with regard to Japan. The US wants to ensure that the flow of rare earths returns to normal levels in line with China’s commitment.

The US was also looking for support on Iran. Although we typically think of Iran as a security issue, a different bucket than on economics, we know that both sides in certain circumstances will connect different issues. It’s possible – although we don’t know – that the US made extending the ceasefire for a longer period conditional on greater Chinese support with regard to Iran, not just in word but in deed.

Both leaders have played up their personal rapport while deliverables look thin. Can a relationship this consequential rest on the chemistry between two leaders? Is that enough and is it sustainable?

People talk about a G2. The US and China do not have what is conventionally considered a G2, a group of two very powerful countries who collaborate in managing global affairs. That is not what they are doing.

President Trump’s version of a G2, based on his language and behaviour, is probably more like “two guys”. In his mind, he and Xi have a very strong personal relationship that is the key source of stable ties. It’s reflected in his praise of Xi, why he went out to personally greet Xi at Joint Base Andrews, had an extravagant welcoming ceremony and state dinner, and showed him the White House’s helipad and the East Wing construction site.

We have not seen Xi explicitly frame their relationship in personal terms. In his various remarks, he is respectful of President Trump and expresses appreciation for his graciousness, but it is clear he is representing the Communist Party and China, not framing this as a personal friendship. Xi tried to be pleasant, and he looked comfortable with Trump, but it’s clear he is not returning the same kinds of personal compliments that he receives. And so for him the G2 is “two governments”, not a bromance.

I don’t know whether Trump’s approach has something to do with a desire to be included among some of the world’s most powerful leaders or to develop friendships. He may be doing this strictly for utilitarian purposes or may be driven by something else. But he’s done this with several other political leaders. He may want to give Xi face, believing that will help keep the relationship stable and make Xi more amenable to making concessions aligned with US preferences.

It’s clear that Xi and other senior officials avoid directly offending Trump, and they showed him a lot of deference when they hosted him in Beijing in May. So both sides are careful in how they handle the interpersonal relationship and questions of respect and face.

You have been watching China for decades. Where does this moment in the US-China relationship rank against some past inflection points, including China’s accession to the World Trade Organization (WTO) and the 2018 trade war?

For much of the past half-century, the US has been focused on trying to find ways to integrate China into the international system and have a stable relationship, and China has been interested in pursuing that path as well. That really describes much of the relationship through 2015, the last time Xi made a state visit to Washington.

But the promise of that state visit and other meetings during the [Barack] Obama administration were not realised, and what we’ve seen is the US becoming more frustrated with China’s trajectory, and going back and forth between different kinds of approaches to deal with the China challenge.

First, it tried under Trump’s first term to use unilateral pressure on China to push it to meet its bilateral and multilateral commitments. Then the [Joe] Biden administration concluded that China couldn’t be effectively integrated into the international order, and therefore its focus shifted to insulating the rest of the world from the problems that China’s economic system and foreign policy created for others.

Under the second Trump administration, the US has decided to abandon that original international order and build a relationship with China on a very different foundation, rooted solely in power politics and a very different conception of the global order – in many ways, an order which China’s current leadership is much more comfortable with and more familiar with than what was critical to the original project the US was pursuing. And so we’re dealing with each other on very different terms than how the US and China interacted with each other for much of the last half-century.

The US government is still grappling with finding the right framework. The label of “constructive strategic stability” – expanded with the modifier “on the basis of respect, fairness and reciprocity” in Washington – is more a slogan than a detailed concept and blueprint, but that’s where we are right now.

You talked about this new approach towards China in Trump’s second administration. For now, the relationship has some semblance of stability, with Trump and Xi meeting up to four times this year. How do you view this new stability and engagement? Is it a genuine recalibration of ties or is it a kind of managed truce that still has sticking points?

Those who are more optimistic about the potential endurance of stable ties believe that the US has essentially abandoned its mission to manage the world in line with the rules-based international order and instead be more pragmatic about how the world works and embrace realpolitik. As a result, that means being less concerned about the rules of the game and more about outcomes.

So, it’s still a competition, but not a competition of competing orders. In fact, there may also be some areas of cooperation, since some of the issues that generate tensions in a competition of those two systems and their underlying values and ideologies are being put to the side.

Those who think that the post-World War II, international rules-based order may have flaws but should be reformed and strengthened look at the current ceasefire as quite temporary and believe that there will eventually be a “snapback” and resumption of greater tensions.

Once the US reduces its vulnerabilities, it will go back on the offence again. I honestly don’t know what the right expectations should be because the US is still trying to figure out what kind of world it wants to have and the same with many other countries. There appears to be a shifting balance of power between China and the West, but we don’t know how durable that is because we’ve got a lot of different variables that we’re trying to assess.

Artificial intelligence has been repeatedly floated as an area where the US and China can cooperate. But the suggestion seems to be at odds with an escalating US campaign designed to slow China’s AI progress – and the two countries also view AI in fundamentally different terms. Is cooperation possible in a sector marked by deep differences and one in which the US is trying to contain China’s progress?

Certainly, if AI is viewed strictly as a race to get to AGI [artificial general intelligence] because of the economic and potential military benefits that accrue to the country that gets there first, then that makes cooperation extremely difficult. But I don’t think that’s really what has been occurring. There’s growing evidence that we’re not strictly in a race.

The US and China have different kinds of approaches to AI. There’s no certainty that there is some magical threshold that one crosses that differentiates yesterday from tomorrow. And there are significant risks from AI that are not a product of interstate competition, challenges that everyone faces from the technology and its potential consequences on the world – deepfakes, hallucinations, loss of control and military applications, and other kinds of risks. There’s also some amazing potential benefits that we could share that could help each other and the rest of the world.

Given the complexities of the technology, the various different countries and stakeholders involved, we’re still at a very early stage. This is like the late 1940s at the very beginning of the nuclear era. We’re at the very beginning of the AI era. It’s really up to us – and “us” meaning everyone on the planet, not just powerful governments – to define what AI means and how we should address this challenge. This is a moment when the clay is still quite wet.

In May, Xi and Trump struck a trade deal built on familiar terms: Chinese purchases of American soybeans and Boeing planes, similar to the phase one trade agreement reached in 2020. Given that history, can such trade deals really function as a stabiliser for the relationship? Or must real stability come from elsewhere?

In an era in which we are involved in great power competition, trade and investment are not going to be an obvious ballast in the relationship. Since the two countries’ comparative advantages are increasingly overlapping, they are competing for the same section of the pie in the global economy. The US also is focused heavily on “new quality productive forces”, just as the Chinese are.

And so, in an era when the two sides are inherently less competitive, interdependence is less problematic and serves as ballast. But in an era defined by rivalry, interdependence is viewed by both governments as a risk, and both have taken enormous steps to de-risk and hold the upper hand.

The agreements on capping tariffs and continuing to provide rare earths, deals on individual products like aeroplanes and agricultural goods, those are very superficial arrangements which don’t suggest a high level of trust.

If such transactions are the cornerstone of these summits, the two sides are just dealing with the most superficial elements of the relationship because the US and China are at fundamental loggerheads over the more serious challenges with no clear path to resolution.

If you don’t think stability in the relationship can come from economics, then where can it come from? Can anything anchor the relationship?

In this kind of world, with a shifting balance of power, competing visions of the global system and an unwillingness to anchor one’s actions on basic principles that should constrain the behaviour of everyone including themselves, instability is more likely to be the hallmark of the relationship.

Without other stabilisers, avoiding conflict requires greater dependence on leadership diplomacy, and the leaders’ depth of involvement will depend on where the other country fits in the views of those two leaders and the dynamics of domestic politics. But the chances of a more fundamental stability seem out of reach.

China’s hi-tech industries have become world-class, driving a narrative that it is overtaking the US. But China’s strengths in manufacturing, in exports and technology, coexist with weak consumption, high youth unemployment and a property slump. How should we understand this divergence, and does it complicate the story of Chinese strength?

I think of China as a “slow tech dragon”, where it is simultaneously achieving amazing technology breakthroughs, manufacturing at scale, diffusing those technologies widely – not just within China but far beyond – while at the same time having a very slow macroeconomic environment. Those two things are the product of a common source, which is the emphasis by China’s policymakers on investment in technology as the primary source of China’s growth trajectory, replacing investment in infrastructure.

As a result, what we’re seeing is an allocation of resources tilted heavily towards advanced technologies and away from other potential investments in human capital, the social safety net and other areas. In some ways, this is a misallocation of resources that is leading to a very imbalanced economy domestically, and therefore globally. China’s economy is at least one-fifth of the world’s economy, and any imbalances in China become global imbalances.

China’s economy has both impressive strengths and significant weaknesses, and it likely will be a slow tech dragon for some time to come. It is that challenge that the world faces. We face a country that is technologically quite advanced but is choosing to focus on the supply side of technology as opposed to the consumption side.

For China to continue with this growth model, by definition it needs the world to adjust and absorb the gap between what China produces and what it consumes. The rest of the world benefits in some ways, but on balance, it is going to be pushing back, aiming for China to rebalance domestically in order to address global imbalances. Of course, those global imbalances aren’t only created by China. The US overconsumes and has a massive amount of debt. In some ways these are mirrors of each other.

But China’s approach is, on its own terms, creating challenges for others, and managing a relationship with a slow tech dragon is quite difficult. The US is still struggling to figure out a way to do so, and China is increasingly encountering pushback – in Europe and Latin America, not just the US – from the consequences of its economic growth strategy.

How sustainable is this Chinese approach, especially when global pressure on China is building?

There are three different kinds of ways in which the Chinese economic model will run into obstacles that it can’t overcome and which will force it to change.

The first is debt, because China’s financial system is so inefficient and there has been a rapid build-up of debt to fuel investment. Even though China is a hi-tech power, many of its companies are not very profitable, and so China’s credit picture continues to worsen.

The second potential obstacle is the effect of an economic transition on labour in China. In some ways, these great advancements are requiring a lot less labour, and in some sectors different kinds of labour, generating a mismatch between the type of human capital China has and what its economy needs. The Communist Party was created on the foundation of labour protests, and if this mismatch continues to worsen, labour instability is a potential challenge, not just for China but for others.

The third obstacle is pushback from the rest of the world. Countries individually and collectively say they are not going to adjust any longer, and that instead they are going to make China adjust. Currently, because of the Trump administration’s approach, many countries in the world similarly view challenges emanating from China, but there’s no effective coordination among others.

We saw a statement from the G20 gathering in Asheville [in North Carolina] recently about concerns over non-market practices, but there’s no significant coordinated action. If international unhappiness coalesces into joint or aligned steps, limiting China’s ability to have large trade surpluses, this could become a more effective constraint on China’s approach.

Trade wars are no longer just a US-China story. The US is in an escalating one with Canada, and Beijing’s doing the same with the EU. If tariffs are becoming the default tool major economies use to settle disputes, what does this mean for the future of the global economy?

The US still has tariffs on China of roughly 30 per cent in total. And if the investigation into manufacturing overcapacity yields a verdict soon, the US could impose an additional 7.5 per cent on China. Total US tariffs are much higher than the 3 per cent level average tariffs that the US had before. Although tariffs on China are still extremely high, they’re also high on just about everyone else. The US is still using tariffs to try to lower its bilateral trade deficits, encourage manufacturing at home, and raise additional revenue. It’s not been hugely successful in those goals, but the [Trump] administration is not giving up.

US efforts are having a corrosive effect on the multilateral trading system because it’s clear that US actions violate its WTO commitments. But the US is not the lone bad actor. US protectionism is a reaction to changing terms of trade and a deep sense of unfairness by the US about its trading relationship with many countries, including China, and its view that industrial policy and non-tariff barriers have led to unfair changes in the commercial relationship, in market opportunities, bilaterally and globally.

And so, US policy is born out of frustration. The Trump administration’s approach to that frustration further erodes the multilateral order, but it’s part of the back-and-forth tit-for-tat that’s been occurring for over two decades.

Despite all of that, according to the WTO, 72 per cent of global trade still occurs on a most-favoured-nation basis. We see efforts by countries to push back on super high tariffs, and countries have cut bilateral and regional deals to expand their opportunities and sustain an open multilateral trading order. Having recently been in Geneva – in a Track 2 dialogue with Chinese counterparts – there is a lot of recognition at the WTO and by its members of these risks to the multilateral order in the context of growing great power competition.

Brics has been pushing harder to settle trade outside the US dollar, and the Netherlands recently pulled roughly US$10 billion in gold out of American vaults. With US allies hedging against the US dollar, what does it say about how secure the dollar’s central role is? And will this be the start of something structural?

Investors and governments are always making adjustments in their holdings of currency based on a variety of circumstances, including macroeconomic conditions across different economies and trends in exchange rates. We’ve seen significant adjustments in holdings of currencies, and we’ve also seen a shift towards shorter maturities recently.

This could be normal, short-term adjustments. The American stock market is performing at record levels, with investment pouring in from around the world. This trend is built largely on optimism about AI and America’s own version of “new quality, productive forces”, the cutting of taxes in the US, and what looks in many respects like still a relatively strong economy.

But there may be another story. Observers are concerned about growing geostrategic tensions, rising tariffs, and dangerous regional hotspots around the world. They’re worried about some of the foundations of the rule of law in the US, its democratic institutions, and the independence of the Federal Reserve.

These worries may signal a transition that is more structural and not just a short-term blip. At the end of the day, a lot will depend on people’s confidence in the US government’s ability to be a wise steward of its economy and the international order.

That said, even though there are worries about the US from a variety of quarters, there are not good alternatives to a dollar-based international monetary system. China is the world’s largest trader, but most of its trade still occurs in dollars, and most of its international financial transactions still occur in dollars. China still holds a lot of US Treasuries, and China’s capital account is still largely closed. China doesn’t have deep financial markets that are internationally open to serve as a significant alternative.

Even with the variety of experiments and initiatives it has pursued, central authorities prioritise domestic financial stability above all. Limited investment opportunities in renminbi, insufficient policy transparency and concerns about the rule of law together are constraints on the renminbi playing a larger international role.

Europe’s economy is growing very slowly. [There are] significant worries about Europe’s economy and European Union gridlock, concerns maybe even larger than those about the US. Hence, the euro is unlikely to be a clear, obvious alternative to the dollar.

And so, in a world where money needs to take some form, and investments are relative, even with concerns about the US and the dollar, it looks like we will continue to live in a dollar-based global economy for quite some time. There are obvious risks, but I don’t see us shifting significantly in a different direction in the near term.
