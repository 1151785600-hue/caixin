Why Southeast Asia feels relief and caution after the Xi-Trump summit

SCMP | FREE | 1109 words | 2026-09-27

Southeast Asia is welcoming the thaw in US-China ties continuing after the Xi-Trump summit, with analysts saying the region could feel less pressure alongside fewer open frictions between the two over the South China Sea.

Yet, caution persists as the summit left deeper disputes unresolved.

“Stable great-power relations – competitive but with sturdy guardrails – will create more legroom for third countries to navigate an increasingly complex world,” according to Lucio Blanco Pitlo III, a research fellow at the Manila-based Asia Pacific Pathways to Progress Foundation.

Such stability, he said, could ease pressure on countries of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (Asean) to pick a side, slow supply-chain disintegration and reduce the intensity of great-power competition in long-standing flashpoints like the Taiwan Strait and South China Sea.

Beijing described Xi’s visit, his second summit with Trump in five months, as a step towards a “constructive China-US relationship of strategic stability”, emphasising continued economic engagement, artificial-intelligence dialogue and the need for Washington to handle the issue of Taiwan cautiously.

Trump’s lavish treatment of Xi suggested that both sought to avoid escalation, as “the language and tone coming out of the summit suggest a continuation of interest in stability on both sides”, said Chong Ja Ian, an associate professor of political science at the National University of Singapore and a non-resident scholar at Carnegie China.

On the other hand, better US-China relations could revive concerns in the region about a “G2” dynamic in which major powers make decisions affecting the region without meaningful Southeast Asian input.

“A sustained thaw would give Southeast Asia more breathing space, particularly economies such as Vietnam that connect Chinese supply chains with American consumers,” said Nguyen Khac Giang, a visiting fellow at the Vietnam Studies Programme of Singapore’s ISEAS-Yusof Ishak Institute.

“But no one in the region wants a ‘G2’ deciding everyone else’s interests over their heads. One Cold War was enough.”

Chong expressed similar concerns, saying any US-China arrangement that made decisions about Southeast Asia without regional participation would curtail the agency of Asean states.

Such new momentum across the Pacific may also draw their attention to how the two powers are handling issues over Taiwan and the South China Sea in the absence of a reference to the highly contested waters in the Chinese read-out.

Beijing sees Taiwan as part of China to be reunited by force if necessary. Most countries, including the US, do not recognise Taiwan as an independent state, but Washington is opposed to any attempt to take the self-ruled island by force and is committed to supplying it with weapons.

Instead of mentioning the South China Sea in its read-out, the White House simply stated that the US military’s presence has “preserved a free and open Indo-Pacific” since World War II.

According to Zhu Junwei, director of the Horizon Insights Centre, a consulting firm, while the disputed sea falls directly within the US definition of the “Indo-Pacific”, it was not a primary agenda item and required no explicit mention.

Its absence signalled an intent to avoid public friction given the two sides’ shared commitment to build towards the “constructive, strategically stable relationship”, Zhu noted.

“Their differences on issues like the South China Sea should be managed and handled through consultation,” she added. “Unilaterally criticising the other party in the read-out does not help manage these differences.”

“Nevertheless, this will not stop the US State Department or the [American] military from continuing to speak out on the South China Sea in the future.”

Such an approach might even be welcomed in some regional capitals, Giang said.

“From that perspective, the absence of Southeast Asia and the South China Sea from the public read-outs may be welcome,” Giang added. “Southeast Asian capitals are not naive enough to mistake silence for indifference. But Beijing’s emphasis on Taiwan signals its priorities, at least in the short term.”

Even as leaders spoke of regional stability in Washington, tensions flared again in the South China Sea.

The Philippine Coast Guard accused China on Friday of obstructing a resupply mission to Second Thomas Shoal, known as Renai Jiao in China and Ayungin Shoal in the Philippines.

The China Coast Guard said it had lawfully monitored and warned a Philippine vessel delivering supplies to what Beijing described as an “illegally grounded” ship at the shoal.

As for Taiwan, it remains a significant concern for Southeast Asia because of the large number of regional citizens living and working there and the broader security and economic implications of any conflict.

“Over a million Asean nationals live and work in Taiwan, so a contingency there portends a serious human and economic tragedy for regional countries,” Pitlo said.

“Constructive dialogue between China and the US may help avert a crisis over these hotspots. That said, US allies and partners hope that a modus vivendi will not come at their expense.”

Meanwhile, the two-month trade truce extension between the US and China and their proposed economic arrangements could provide some immediate relief for businesses. But the longer-term impact on Southeast Asia will depend on how Washington and Beijing implement their commitments.

Asean exporters have been concerned about transshipment risks and the spillover effects of a prolonged US-China trade war, particularly for those relying on Chinese inputs to manufacture goods for the US market.

“Progress on the trade front is a welcome respite,” Pitlo said.

Still, analysts cautioned that a more cooperative tone was unlikely to reverse continuing efforts by Washington and Beijing to secure supply chains and restrict sensitive technology transfers, and Trump’s unpredictability remains a risk factor.

As Giang saw it, “Under the Trump administration, predictability still has a short shelf life.”

Southeast Asian countries therefore need to compete on infrastructure, skills and local suppliers regardless of the direction of US-China relations, he said.

That could make it increasingly difficult for Southeast Asian economies to continue benefiting from their traditional role as intermediaries between the US and Chinese markets, Giang added.

On the proposed dialogue on artificial intelligence, Pitlo said a China-US understanding could help “assuage fears that great-power strife may lead to technology fragmentation” at a time when Washington and Beijing are advancing competing visions for future cutting-edge technologies.

But Giang argued that an AI dialogue alone was unlikely to overcome deeper structural divides or resolve the region’s technology dilemma.

“On AI and advanced technology, dialogue could help manage risks, but it does not automatically open access to advanced chips or bridge competing technological ecosystems,” he said.
