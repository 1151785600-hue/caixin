As toxic haze spreads, can Indonesia make land burners pay?

SCMP | FREE | 1046 words | 2026-08-25

As massive forest and land fires burn across parts of Indonesia, sending haze towards neighbouring Malaysia, President Prabowo Subianto has vowed firm action against big corporations found responsible – reviving a long-running test of whether Jakarta can punish those behind the blazes and finally break the cycle.

“We will go after them if they are proven guilty. We will penalise them according to the law,” Prabowo told reporters in Palangka Raya, Central Kalimantan, on Saturday.

He flew first to West Kalimantan, then Central Kalimantan, over the weekend to assess the damage in person and meet local officials coordinating the emergency response.

The latest fires are part of a wider trend. Forestry ministry data shows more than 202,000 hectares (499,153 acres) of land were burned between January and July, including nearly 95,000 hectares in July alone.

Six provinces have been designated priorities: Riau, Jambi and South Sumatra on Sumatra island, alongside West, Central and South Kalimantan on Borneo. Fires have also broken out elsewhere, including Java, Papua and Nusa Tenggara.

The blazes have produced choking smoke, forced school closures and blanketed parts of the region in haze that has drifted into Malaysia, with Sarawak among the worst-affected areas.

Indonesia has deployed thousands of ground personnel and aircraft including water-bombing helicopters, as well as cloud-seeding technology to contain the fires and slow the haze’s spread.

But environmental activists and analysts say containment is no longer enough, pointing to what they describe as chronic weaknesses in prosecution, deterrence and enforcement of Indonesia’s forest-fire laws.

Janang Firman Palanungkai, Central Kalimantan executive director of environmental watchdog Walhi, said fire prevention must take precedence.

“While we appreciate the efforts to contain the fires, prevention should be the priority here,” he said.

Police have named 72 arson suspects nationwide as part of a government crackdown, 14 of them from Kalimantan. Prabowo’s chief of staff, Minister of State Secretariat Prasetyo Hadi, said four companies operating in West Kalimantan were under investigation for land burning.

“We won’t hesitate to act sternly, possibly even revoking their licences,” he warned.

Under laws passed in 1999 and 2009, deliberately setting fire to forest areas carries a sentence of up to 10 years in prison and heavy fines.

Uli Arta Siagian, national campaign coordinator at Walhi, said uncompromising government action was crucial to ushering in a new era of fire mitigation.

“We sincerely hope the president will make good on his promise, without fear or favour.”

During a televised coordination meeting in Pekanbaru on Monday, Prabowo asked Riau police chief Herry Heryawan if suspect interrogations had uncovered evidence of corporate involvement. Herry said that they had not.

But Uli said her organisation had little doubt that most fires in Kalimantan were driven by commercial interests rather than natural causes.

“Our data shows that 74 per cent of the hotspots in Kalimantan, numbering 25,524, are within the concession areas of different companies,” she said.

In Indonesia, concessions are large tracts of state land licensed to companies for commercial logging, mining or industrial agriculture.

Hotspots within concession areas do not in themselves prove corporate responsibility, but activists argue the overlap should trigger deeper investigations and stronger enforcement. Walhi’s own analysis found thousands of hotspots inside company concessions in Kalimantan spanning plantation, mining and forestry activity.

“The recurring nature of our forest fire saga is a sure sign that our government has failed to deter corporations from causing harm to the environment,” Uli said.

Cheap land clearing

Two commercial practices associated with land development heighten fire risk: draining peatlands, which leaves carbon-rich soil dry and highly combustible, and clearing land with fire rather than using machinery or labour.

Uli said both practices persisted because they were cheap, despite severe public-health and environmental costs.

“Companies torch the land because it’s cheaper than hiring people and machinery, but at a great cost to the environment, not to mention the health hazards incurred,” she said, criticising initial official statements that attributed the crisis primarily to natural factors such as El Nino and a prolonged dry season.

“These may be contributing factors, but they don’t negate the fact that there are those who intentionally set fire to the land for commercial gain.”

Rony Teguh, a forest-fire detection technology researcher at the University of Palangka Raya, agreed. He said this year’s fires shared patterns with the disastrous 2015 season, when about 2.6 million hectares burned, costing Indonesia an estimated US$16.1 billion in damages and shrouding the region in haze.

“People tend to focus on what’s going on while the fire is raging. But I’d say we must look at what happens after the dust has settled,” Rony said, noting that his past research frequently found palm oil seedlings growing across burned sites once the fires died down.

“This happened every time, so it was difficult not to conclude the land had been torched to make way for new plantations.”

Uli said Walhi’s field observations over the years corroborated Rony’s findings. An official at the Ministry of Forestry declined to comment when approached by This Week in Asia, citing a lack of adequate information from senior officials.

Uli said the problem was not a lack of environmental regulations but weak enforcement of the rules already in place.

“The government has actually succeeded in prosecuting a dozen cases against errant companies, but how many were really made to pay through the nose?” she asked.

Since 2015, Indonesia’s courts have convicted at least 12 corporations for starting or negligently allowing forest and land fires. Yet authorities have repeatedly struggled to enforce the penalties handed down.

According to a report by local news outlet Tempo, by 2024 the government had collected only 400 billion rupiah (US$25 million) of the 20 trillion rupiah (US$1.2 billion) in accumulated fines imposed on companies found negligent over the 2015 fires.

Janang said Walhi had begun urging local authorities to act as hotspots began to flare three months ago.

“We pushed hard for an environmental audit of the companies and a re-evaluation of their permits, but our pleas fell on deaf ears,” he said. “The question is whether our law enforcement agencies dare to dig further and hold the masterminds behind forest fires to account.”
