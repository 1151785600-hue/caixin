Singapore ministers earn millions. What do other Asian leaders make?

SCMP | FREE | 1186 words | 2026-09-16

Singapore’s ministers are among the world’s highest paid, a system its leaders have defended as necessary to attract capable people into public service and reduce the temptation of corruption.

Its latest salary review has once again put the spotlight on that approach, while highlighting a wider regional divide over how Asian governments value political leadership and public service.

Under the city state’s formula, the prime minister’s salary is pegged at twice the benchmark for an entry-level minister, which is based on the median annual income of the country’s top 1,000 Singaporean earners after applying a 40 per cent public-service discount.

When Prime Minister Lawrence Wong’s annual pay benchmark rises to S$3.6 million (US$2.83 million) on October 15, it will be almost four times the basic annual salary of Hong Kong leader John Lee Ka-chiu.

Wong has pledged to donate his S$1.4 million increase over five years, while serving ministers will only gradually move towards the S$1.8 million entry-level benchmark.

For analysts, the issue extends far beyond the size of any one leader’s salary. Political pay structures reveal what governments value, what they expect citizens to accept and how much of a leader’s total compensation is visible to the public.

While Singapore defends high salaries as a way to attract talent and guard against corruption, the balance looks very different across the rest of Asia – shaped instead by bureaucratic hierarchy, austerity, symbolic restraint or off-the-books perks that do not always appear in headline figures.

Premium on talent

Singapore’s high ministerial salaries stem from a broader choice about who should govern, according to Donald Low, a former civil servant in the city state and professor of practice in public policy at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology.

Rather than relying only on politicians who rise through party ranks, the long-ruling People’s Action Party (PAP) routinely recruits mid-career professionals and senior public servants.

However, these new political entrants must still stand for election, engage voters and face public scrutiny, Low said. “It’s a very unusual model of how politics is done, and high ministerial salaries are almost inevitably a consequence of this model.”

Low said Wong’s donation pledge and the gradual phase-in for serving ministers showed that public expectations remained a factor, even within a market-linked system.

Hong Kong’s administrative benchmark

Hong Kong’s chief executive also sits near the top of regional salary rankings, but that figure is set through a different system.

Following a 1.3 per cent adjustment in July, Lee’s cash pay stands at about HK$475,600 (US$61,000) a month, or HK$5.71 million annually. Since 2007, the chief executive’s salary has been pegged at 112.5 per cent of the wages received by the chief secretary for administration.

“Rather than asking what a political leader might earn in the private sector, Hong Kong has positioned the chief executive’s salary within an established public-service hierarchy,” Low said.

The contrast also reflects different career arcs. Hong Kong’s principal officials often serve one or two terms before returning to another profession, making any private-sector pay cut temporary.

“In Singapore, a ministerial appointment is more likely to become the final major phase of a person’s career,” Low said.

Hong Kong’s former chief executives remain subject to a three-year post-office employment control period, which includes a total ban on outside jobs during their first year out of office.

Institutionalised austerity in Japan

Until December 2025, Japan’s prime minister and cabinet members who were also Diet members were entitled to additional executive pay and bonuses, in addition to their standard legislators’ salaries.

However, Japanese leaders have long maintained a tradition of returning their executive pay and bonuses – a practice started by former prime minister Naoto Kan in the wake of the 2011 Fukushima disaster.

What began as an emergency response survived as a long-standing norm of financial restraint under subsequent administrations.

The practice was formalised when legislation was enacted last year stating that Diet members appointed as prime minister or ministers would receive no additional pay.

Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi described the change as “mi wo kiru kaikaku” – or self-sacrificing reforms.

The revised law means Takaichi now receives standard Diet pay and year-end allowances, totalling about 21.9 million yen (US$141,000) annually, down from the 40 million yen figure often cited in older comparisons of Japanese prime ministers.

Hidden figures and off-the-books perks

In Malaysia, Anwar Ibrahim has refused a salary for both his roles as prime minister and finance chief since taking office in 2022. His cabinet also implemented a 20 per cent monthly pay cut that remains in place – gestures meant to show solidarity with citizens facing economic pressures.

Bryan Cheah, a senior researcher with the anti-corruption non-government organisation Centre to Combat Corruption and Cronyism, said modest salaries and voluntary pay cuts could not answer wider questions about politicians’ wealth, investments and business interests.

Malaysia has no statutory requirement for ministers and elected representatives to make comprehensive public asset declarations.

“Such disclosures are entirely voluntary and lack a common protocol,” Cheah said.

He pointed to instances where candidates published their bank statements during the last general election, calling them “wholly inadequate” as such documents “[do] not exclude the possibility that their wealth may be deposited with a family member”.

Cheah cited enforcement cases involving the seizure of assets worth millions of ringgit as a reason official salary figures alone did not necessarily reassure the public.

“Without the requisite institutional reforms that demonstrate the real earnings of ministers and elected officials, this gesture remains a gesture alone,” Cheah said.

Indonesia presents a sharper divide between headline salary and the overall value of political office.

A cabinet minister’s basic monthly salary is 5.04 million rupiah, with a 13.61 million rupiah position allowance bringing the published total to about 18.6 million rupiah (US$1,056).

But ministers can also receive an official residence, vehicle and driver, health coverage, travel expenses and ministry-specific performance allowances.

“No single public document tells a citizen what a minister actually takes home in total,” said Yanuar Nugroho, a visiting senior fellow at ISEAS – Yusof Ishak Institute and senior lecturer at the Driyarkara School of Philosophy in Jakarta.

“The 18.6 million rupiah figure is real, but it is a small and slightly misleading fraction of the real value of holding office.”

President Prabowo Subianto has also floated salary cuts for ministers to improve budget efficiency.

Nugroho said the gesture could matter politically, but not financially.

He cited independent estimates that the move would save about 6.5 billion rupiah a year in basic pay, or 47.9 billion rupiah including allowances – roughly 0.0005 per cent of Indonesia’s 2026 state budget.

Public anger over political privilege has already proved combustible. Protests erupted last August over a 50 million rupiah monthly housing allowance for legislators, before spreading nationwide after ride-hailing driver Affan Kurniawan was killed by a police vehicle.

Indonesia could borrow Singapore’s clearer consolidation and disclosure of political pay, Nugroho said, without adopting its link to elite private-sector incomes.

“Transparency travels,” he said. “The market-rate aspiration does not.”
