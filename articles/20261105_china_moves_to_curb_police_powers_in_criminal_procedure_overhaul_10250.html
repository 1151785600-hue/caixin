China has proposed a major overhaul of its criminal procedure law, including an explicit ban on presuming guilt, restrictions on residential surveillance, and safeguards against improper detention and asset seizures. The draft amendments were submitted Nov. 2 to the NPC Standing Committee for initial review, marking the fourth revision since 1979. Often called a “little constitution,” the law balances state investigative powers and individual rights and responds to years of reform demands.

The draft has 55 amendment provisions, including 13 new articles. Shen Chunyao, director of the Legislative Affairs Commission, said it targets abuses that “seriously undermine judicial fairness,” citing misuse of residential surveillance at designated sites, profit-driven enforcement, criminal probes in economic disputes, and appointed lawyers blocking defendants’ choice of counsel. The ban on presuming guilt would go into the law’s general provisions; scholars say that recognizing the presumption of innocence is essential to preventing wrongful convictions. Existing law lacks the express principle but includes safeguards: prosecutors’ burden of proof, no compelled self-incrimination, and mandatory acquittal when evidence is insufficient.

Residential surveillance at designated places would be limited to national-security or terrorism cases; other suspects without fixed residences would face other legally authorized measures. The draft explanation says this restores residential surveillance’s intended role as an alternative to detention. Liberty-restricting measures must be necessary, proportionate, and adjustable. Grounds for extending police detention are narrowed. The current 37-day pre-arrest maximum has been abused; a 30-day extension would remain only for major suspects in serious cases involving criminal groups, cross-border offenses, economic crimes, national security, or terrorism.

Prosecutors reviewing arrest requests would have to question suspects, hear defense lawyers, regularly review detention necessity, and approve restarting detention-period calculations. Detainees must be released or given alternative measures when detention approaches the possible prison sentence.

The draft overhauls property rules. Seized criminal proceeds and related income go to the state treasury unless legally returned to victims; authorities cannot divert or spend them, link them to budgets, or return funds to case-handling agencies. Agencies must distinguish lawful from illicit assets, company from personal property, and suspects’ property from relatives’ or third parties’ property. Seizures must leave families basic living expenses; productive assets can remain in reasonable use if proceedings aren’t compromised. Courts examine evidence and decide disposition at or after sentencing; interested parties may object. Police notify prosecutors of substantial seizures; prosecutors can require correction. Deadlines are left to other national rules.

The proposal ends “placeholder defenses”: appointed legal-aid lawyers’ representation ends when a defendant retains own counsel. Defense lawyers get more rights to case information and notifications at key stages. Detention centers must ensure lawyers actually meet clients within 48 hours. Legal aid expands to suspects facing at least three years’ imprisonment and ordinary-procedure cases; victims may apply. Duty lawyers get explicit rights to case info, client meetings, and file inspection.

In the leniency system for guilty pleas, written admissions require simultaneous audio/video recording; courts verify voluntariness and authenticity. Defense counsel can dispute guilt or sentencing recommendations; objections must be preserved and heard. Witnesses must appear if testimony changes materially; unauthenticated pre-trial testimony cannot be used. Mandatory interrogation recordings expand to cases with possible sentences of at least three years, covering entire interrogations and preserved without editing.

Minor victims qualify for legal aid, and questioning must protect privacy and avoid repeated interviews. Foreign suspects and defendants get fair treatment; qualifying evidence from international cooperation is allowed; countermeasures are authorized against improper foreign criminal jurisdiction or discriminatory restrictions on Chinese citizens or organizations.
