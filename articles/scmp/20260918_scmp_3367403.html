Paul French lifts veil on final months of China’s last emperor in the Forbidden City

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1717 words | 2026-09-18

On a recent afternoon, Paul French – British author, broadcaster, blogger but definitely not, as he always stresses, an academic – is explaining his technique.

“My trick, which I suppose is reasonably successful, is to try and write Chinese history linked to a genre that lots and lots of people read, [because] there’s that many people” – his finger and thumb indicate a space so narrow you’d hardly be able to pass a sliver of paper between them – “who read books on China. And there’s this many people” – his hands convey near-ceiling height – “who’ll read about true crime or gangsters or royalty.”

Such an approach has, indeed, made him reasonably, and internationally, successful. In 2011, he published Midnight in Peking: How the Murder of a Young Englishwoman Haunted the Last Days of Old China, which made it onto the New York Times’ bestseller list, won both the 2013 Edgar Award for “Best Fact Crime” in the United States and Britain’s Crime Writers’ Association’s “ALCS Gold Dagger for Non-Fiction”, and was named “International Success of the Year” at the 2012 Australian Book Industry Awards.

In 2018, his City of Devils: A Shanghai Noir was a Kirkus Reviews’ “Best Book of the Year”. In 2024, Her Lotus Year: China, The Roaring Twenties and the Making of Wallis Simpson received much coverage and quotably upbeat reviews (“Her Lotus Year stays in the mind as a bright, shining, lively snapshot,” says The Spectator. “Delightful to read.”).

Now, it’s the turn of Puyi in The Last Emperor of China: Twilight of the Forbidden City, which falls into a royalty subgenre that French calls “doomed monarchy”.

Although he’s writing – primarily – for a Western audience, as a category, it has promising global reach. Earlier this year, French was told by his Chinese publisher that only one other foreigner has shifted (and signed) more books in China: British author and historian Simon Sebag Montefiore, whose 2016 work on those quintessential doomed monarchs, The Romanovs: 1613-1918, was a massive hit.

Puyi, of course, didn’t meet his doom with an execution in a cellar. He was six years old in 1912, when the Manchu empire ended and the new Republican government decided to leave him behind the walls of the Forbidden City – an oddity, roaming around the “Great Within”, attended by eunuchs (“teapots without spouts” was the local term). With his peculiar upbringing and background, that child would grow into the adult who, during the second Sino-Japanese war (1937-1945), would become his country’s “Great Traitor”.

French, who was a language student in 1980s Beijing and lived in Shanghai for many years before returning to Britain in 2014, had been marinating this story for a while. He sensed that – “a bit like Wallis Simpson”, the American socialite whose relationship with Britain’s King Edward VIII led to his abdication – people sort of knew about Puyi but not really. He’d been struck by the blank stares he met when he mentioned him to Chinese-language students at a couple of British universities.

French realised that it’s been almost four decades since he, along with many others, had been “blown away” by the Bernardo Bertolucci epic, The Last Emperor (1987), which was – an amazing achievement – filmed inside the Forbidden City.

While he was wondering how to tackle the topic, he wrote an article about Puyi’s wife, Wanrong, in 2024, for this magazine. “Wanrong is so beautiful and there are so many pictures of her and I thought it would be fun,” he says. “I did mention, in a couple of lines, that she’d had a tutor and this was one of the things the film had missed.”

In fact, Joan Chen – who played Wanrong in the film – makes a single reference to her tutor as a “Miss Windsor”. Bertolucci, who co-wrote the screenplay with his brother-in-law, Mark Peploe, knew Wanrong was educated but not by whom, so they made up a name with British royal resonance. Wanrong’s tutor, however, was not British. Isabel Ingram was an American “mishkid” – a child of missionaries – born in Peking (Beijing) in 1902 and educated at Wellesley College, in Massachusetts, in the US.

After the PostMag piece came out, French was contacted by a man called Kenneth Mayer, who told him he was Ingram’s grandson. “He said, ‘Thank you so much for noticing Isabel; no one else does,’” French recalls. “And then he said, ‘By the way, you really should read her diary and she has some letters from this Johnston guy …’”

French was staggered. He hadn’t known about the diary, and the “Johnston guy” was Reginald Johnston, Puyi’s Scottish tutor, played in the film by Peter O’Toole.

Johnston had written a memoir which, in its 486 pages, makes a single reference to “the Empress’s American friend who had been her teacher of English both before and after the Empress entered the Forbidden City as a bride”; and his letters and journals had been destroyed after his death in Edinburgh, in 1938.

Diary! Letters! Such finds are history’s gold dust. Even better, Ingram, who died in Virginia in 1988, had typed up all of it. (French, who revels in his North London unacademic blokeishness, makes some cheerfully unrepeatable comments about the trials of handwritten research materials.) Once he’d read the Ingram documents, he knew he had his book. Ingram was 20 when she became Wanrong’s tutor, in October 1922. A few months later, on December 1, Wanrong, aged 16, married Puyi, also 16.

French decided to focus The Last Emperor of China on the 23 months between that five-day wedding – “the last burst of imperial pageantry Peking would ever see”, he writes – and the couple’s expulsion into the “Great Without” on November 5, 1924, which brought an end to more than 500 years of (occasionally interrupted) dynastic occupation of the Forbidden City.

Apart from sporadic chunks of necessary context, the book is like a wonderfully evocative play with four principal performers – a middle-aged Scottish man, a young American woman and two teenage Manchu royals – on a spectacular stage.

French says he’s only too aware that he joins a long line of Puyi biographers, but what Ingram brings to his version is the voice of modernity. Formal lessons with Wanrong, who had a golden fountain-pen and silver inkwell, rarely took up more than an hour or two. Afterwards, there would be hair-braiding, hat-selecting, wardrobe-rummaging, dancing to gramophone records, chocolates and cigarettes.

Ingram’s language – “I love her for life”; “cute little thing”; “the Empress is still darling”; “he nearly died on the spot” – could be that of a Californian blogger, and the film these scenes evoke isn’t the Bertolucci drama; it’s Clueless, in every sense. Her diary also testifies to Puyi and Wanrong’s genuine friendship as they go cycling and kite-flying and dog-walking around their vast, mouldering compound. Many happy photographs are taken.

For French, Ingram is an important witness to the growing intimacy between the newlyweds. As he says, a great debate among biographers concerns Puyi’s sexuality and whether he was asexual or homosexual. Famously, on his wedding night, the last emperor had sloped off to the Hall of Mental Cultivation for a think.

“He’s 16!” says French, and goes on to note that “the single most surprising thing [in the diary] is when Isabel writes about ‘the curtain being drawn’ and there are no lessons today … Obviously, like all young people, they work out what goes where”.

But Puyi and Wanrong’s isn’t the only unexpected romance in the book. It becomes clear through his letters that Johnston, 28 years older than Ingram, is attracted to her. French believes he was “heartbroken” when she refused him, which is why her name was never mentioned in his memoir. Perhaps. (On the other hand, this reader thinks it was more about Johnston’s considerable, publicity-hogging ego.)

At any rate, Johnston’s letters to Ingram veer in tone from housekeeper (“I am sorry to hear chewing gum has been introduced into the palace”) to stalker (“Who is the young man you kept looking at so earnestly all the time?”). Eventually, he becomes self-deludingly creepy. “Did you explain to the Lockharts how and why you refused to let me kiss you when we were on the Emperor’s swing together?” he writes. “And yet it would only have been a remote, innocuous fatherly salutation!”

By then, the quartet had broken up. On November 5, 1924, several hours after the early-morning palace coup, Ingram was taken by rickshaw to the Forbidden City as usual, but was barred from entering. At the Gate of Divine Prowess, the commandant told her that Wanrong had gone into the “Great Without” “hatless, coatless, baggage-less”. Ingram, who would leave China in 1927, never saw her dressing-up companion again.

In the Bertolucci film, when soldiers arrive to evict Puyi and Wanrong, they are playing tennis. In real life, he was peeling her an apple, that ancient symbol of expulsion, in the Palace of Gathered Elegance. Soldiers took a – distinctly unhappy – photo, which is in the book, of his abandoned bedroom: an adolescent muddle of kung fu novels and toys.

“Once they go outside the gilded cage, they’re lost,” says French. “You could write another book about Puyi in Manchuria and try and get into the psychology of what happened to that relationship when he’s manipulated, with no advisers, and she knows that they’ve made a horrendous mistake and slips into opium abuse.”

In this immensely readable book, however, French steps only briefly beyond the palace gates and into that future when Puyi was crowned the emperor of Japan’s puppet state, Manchukuo, in 1934 – another doomed monarchy.

Another sombre note is the contrast between Ingram’s spirited diary and the fate suffered by Wanrong in the final summary, which is heart-wrenching.

Puyi survived and, in the 60s, made several return visits to the Forbidden City. Premier Zhou Enlai liked to keep an eye on him.

Asked what he thinks of Puyi now, French replies, “I do take the Zhou Enlai line. He’s supposed to have invited Puyi to a lunch and told him, ‘You weren’t to blame for becoming emperor and what happened then. But you can be blamed for what happened after.’”
