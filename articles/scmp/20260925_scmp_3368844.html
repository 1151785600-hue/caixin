Trump hails WWII ‘ally’ China as Xi tells of bonds ‘forged in blood and fire’

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1539 words | 2026-09-25

In a surprise nod to shared history, US President Donald Trump called China a World War II ally as he welcomed Xi Jinping to the White House on Thursday ahead of summit talks.

But the historical reference was far from one-sided, with both leaders repeatedly referring to the joint fight against Japanese aggression as they sought to emphasise the long history of US-China friendship and the need to seek common ground.

“If we focus on our common interests, I’m confident there is much we can achieve,” Trump said in the White House foyer during the state welcome ceremony ahead of formal talks with the Chinese president.

“The pursuit of those common interests has connected our people across the vast oceans, across big tremendous distances, and many differences, and even made us allies in World War II,” he noted.

Trump went on to praise the “brave American pilots” of the Flying Tigers who had volunteered to “fight for China’s survival”, and said that he was happy to work with Xi in the same way.

“Just as our people worked together to win that war, President Xi and I will continue working together to forge a better future for both of our nations,” he said.

The Flying Tigers were American volunteer pilots who came to China in 1941 to fight Japanese forces alongside Chinese troops, before the US formally entered the second world war. They flew fighter missions against Japanese aircraft and helped to defend Chinese cities and supply routes during the conflict.

“They were just unbelievable,” Trump said.

His remarks came days after Japan – now a US treaty ally – renewed its call to remove the “former enemy state” clause from the UN Charter, and after Trump’s talks in New York with Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi.

The clause “must be deleted”, Takaichi told the UN General Assembly in New York on September 22, pointing to a 2005 consensus resolution where world leaders had “resolved” to remove it.

The clause allowed allied states to act against defeated World War II enemy states without UN Security Council approval, but has been declared obsolete by the world body twice - in 1995 and 2005.

While Trump made no direct reference to Japan, Xi was more forthcoming – explicitly naming the country as he underlined China-US friendship forged in “blood and fire” during the war.

“During the War of Resistance Against Japanese Aggression, Chinese soldiers and civilians rescued hundreds of American pilots at the cost of hundreds of thousands of Chinese lives,” he said during Thursday’s state dinner.

“Such a friendship, forged in blood and fire, will surely live on through the generations.”

Xi also recalled the wartime “Hump” aerial resupply route over the Himalayas, citing the perilous joint operation as a testament to how the two countries united to sustain Chinese resistance against Japan.

The Hump became a vital lifeline for China after Japanese forces severed the Burma Road in 1942, cutting off military and humanitarian supplies, while Chinese soldiers and civilians helped to rescue hundreds of American pilots forced down along the route.

That cooperation came at a heavy cost for China, which suffered enormous civilian losses during the war, Xi noted.

It was the second time Xi had drawn on their shared history within hours. During the state welcome ceremony in the morning, he noted how soldiers on both sides had “fought shoulder to shoulder against fascist aggression”

“[The] Chinese people have never forgotten that part of history, nor have the American people,” he asserted.

The timing of the remark was particularly sensitive, given Japan’s stepped-up calls to remove the UN “enemy state” clause following its recent invocation by Chinese and Russian officials as tensions with Tokyo escalate.

Reacting to Takaichi’s speech at the General Assembly, the Chinese foreign ministry said on Wednesday that the clauses were “a legal mechanism in the UN Charter. For 81 years, these clauses have served to prevent the resurgence of fascism and militarism and defend the post-war international order. They are still relevant today.”

Trump’s use of the word “allies” in describing past US-China cooperation against Japan caused unease among Japanese media.

The Japan Times, for example, said the remarks were “likely to raise eyebrows in Tokyo, a US ally currently embroiled in a festering dispute with Beijing and facing pressure from Trump on both trade and defence issues”.

China-Japan ties have been marked by recurring tensions over Japan’s wartime occupation and atrocities in China, as well as disputes over Tokyo’s handling of that history.

Relations sharply deteriorated last year after Takaichi said that a Taiwan contingency could be a survival-threatening situation for Japan, justifying a military response. Beijing demanded an apology and imposed a slew of retaliatory measures, including curbs on rare earth exports.

Takaichi met Trump on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly on September 22, just ahead of Xi’s arrival in Washington. According to NHK News, she told Trump that she wanted to “seize the opportunity to demonstrate to the world” the strength of the US-Japan alliance.

Beijing sees Taiwan as part of China to be reunited by force if necessary. Most countries, including the United States – Taiwan’s main international backer – do not recognise the self-ruled island as an independent state. However, Washington is opposed to any attempt to take Taiwan by force and is committed to supplying it with weapons.

In Washington, Xi also highlighted the shared economic and cultural history of the two nations, tracing the relationship to America’s earliest years.

After the US gained independence, it “turned its eyes to China”, Xi said in his dinner address. The two countries began trading, he said, “exchanging goods to meet each other’s needs and draw on our complementary strengths”.

George Washington, he noted, had personally acquired a large collection of Chinese porcelain.

Xi then fast-forwarded to 1971, when “ping pong diplomacy” and the “political wisdom embodied in a small ball moving the globe” helped Mao Zedong, founder of the People’s Republic of China, and then-US president Richard Nixon open the door to rapprochement.

The redwoods Nixon later sent as a state gift “took root in Zhejiang province, where I worked for several years”, Xi added.

Trump’s speech was similarly filled with historical references to US-China friendship, as the gala dinner capped a day of pageantry, politics and protocol.

“President Xi and I both understand that we represent different systems, but the ties between our people endure, and we’ve never gotten along better,” he said.

“Together, we can continue to build a relationship that promotes prosperity and security for future generations.”

Trump invoked figures and events ranging from Sun Yat-sen, the founding father of modern China, to America’s declaration of freedom 250 years ago.

“Long ago, European explorers first discovered the American continent as they searched for a new way to reach China in the East,” Trump said, as he spoke about the first formal Chinese delegation to travel to the US and China’s first state dinner in Washington.

Despite the early historical reflections, including one about the first trade ships arriving on the banks of the Pearl River from New York, Trump took a midpoint detour to focus on the White House ballroom renovations – a frequent talking point for the president.

Going admittedly “off script”, Trump invited guests in the East Room to take a look at the new White House ballroom “in honour of President Xi and Madame Peng”, despite the project still being under construction.

The new ballroom would host more than 1,000 people, substantially more than the East Room, Trump said, claiming US presidents had wanted this for more than 150 years.

Trump then returned to the symbolism, gifting Xi a sculpture of a bald eagle that he said was made by an American artist and designed by him and the first lady, Melania Trump.

“The bald eagle is a national symbol of the United States and an embodiment of the free and soaring spirit of America, and we hope it will find a nice place, a wonderful place in Beijing,” Trump added.

The bald eagle has been featured on the Great Seal of the United States since 1782. It was officially designated the national bird of the US when Trump’s predecessor Joe Biden signed an act of Congress into law in December 2024.

In his closing remarks, Trump returned to history, referencing America’s 250th anniversary, marked on July 4 this year.

He raised a toast to his Chinese counterpart and his wife, as well as the long relationship between China and America that had bridged “thousands of miles and spanned hundreds of years”.

“May all of our people know a future of harmony, peace, and success,” Trump said.

The speeches were made to a packed room of invitees, including Chinese and US government officials, American business leaders and some Supreme Court justices.

Several prominent American CEOs made the guest list, including Nvidia’s Jensen Huang and Tesla’s Elon Musk, but no Chinese business leaders were present.

This stood in stark contrast to the Beijing summit in May, when Trump brought a delegation of nearly 20 top executives, many of whom were spotted at the state banquet hosted by Xi.
