Why warm welcomes can’t stop Malaysia’s doctor exodus from Borneo

SCMP | FREE | 1300 words | 2026-10-11

On Facebook, public hospitals in Malaysia’s Bornean states have been rolling out “Selamat Datang” [welcome] banner posts to embrace medical staff reporting for duty.

Tawau Hospital on Sabah’s east coast recently published a group photograph welcoming newly arrived medical officers. Kinabatangan Hospital posted similar banners hailing arrivals across various departments, while in neighbouring Sarawak, Kapit Hospital accompanied its greeting with a portrait of a newly arrived doctor.

But behind the warm online welcomes lies a dilemma for Malaysia’s healthcare system: more than a third of doctors offered permanent posts in Sabah and Sarawak have failed to report for duty.

The no-shows highlight the difficulty of persuading medical professionals to relocate to the country’s Bornean states. Doctors cite family separation, uncertain career prospects and difficult working conditions as major hurdles – with many questioning whether to stay even after heeding the call.

Speaking in parliament on Thursday, Malaysian Health Minister Dzulkefly Ahmad said 382 out of 1,055 medical officers offered permanent appointments in East Malaysia had not reported for duty.

While the 36 per cent no-show rate was an improvement from 42 per cent in 2025, Dzulkefly said it was still not a result to be proud of.

Hard choices

For Dr S*, accepting a permanent appointment in Sabah meant leaving her family in Kelantan to build a new life alone.

The doctor moved from Kota Bharu to Sabah’s east coast in late September after her family encouraged her to accept the permanent offer. She said she had initially considered remaining on a contract in her home state.

Malaysia introduced fixed-term contracts for junior doctors in 2016 amid a shortage of permanent positions, making permanent appointments highly sought-after for their greater job security.

But accepting the post came at a price: moving from Kelantan to Sabah cost S nearly a month’s salary. Even after subsidies and reimbursements, she was still about 2,000 ringgit (US$489) out of pocket, mainly covering upfront rent and basic necessities.

“I have been adapting to the new environment, culture and language. The workload is demanding because we have a shortage of medical officers,” S said. “But I also have support from my specialists, nurses and fellow doctors … Even small things, like invitations to share meals, are very meaningful when you are far from family.”

Despite the support, S does not envisage staying indefinitely. “For now, I see about two years as the maximum period I can serve away from my family,” she said, adding that while she might consider staying up to five years if her family could join her, her ultimate goal was to return to Kelantan.

Other doctors have chosen not to take the leap at all. Dr AN*, another contract doctor, declined a permanent appointment in Sarawak to continue serving in Kelantan.

“My own health needs and my mother’s illness factored into my decision to stay in Kelantan as a contract [doctor],” she said.

AN appealed the placement decision, but after this was rejected, she chose to remain a contract employee and reapply for a permanent position in the future. Asked if she would have accepted under different circumstances, AN said: “My decision is final. I want to continue serving in my home state.”

The limits of incentives

The experiences of S and AN reflect the reasons outlined by Health Minister Dzulkefly in his response to Pulai MP Suhaizan Kayat in parliament.

Besides family separation, health and financial and logistical challenges, Dzulkefly said that doctors often declined postings due to a preference for urban facilities, concerns over future specialist training and difficulties ensuring eventual transfers back home.

To address these concerns, Dzulkefly said he had directed the ministry to study and implement a dedicated 30 per cent quota for specialist training for medical officers serving in Sabah and Sarawak.

“Today, our Hadiah Latihan Persekutuan [Federal Training Award] has 1,650 places, so 30 per cent – around 500 places – will be specifically for those who are in Sabah and Sarawak,” he told parliament.

Sarawak MP Kelvin Yii welcomed the announcement but added that stronger career incentives should be accompanied by better pay, improved accommodation and clearer posting terms.

“Each posting should state the specific location and the duration of service from the start,” Yii said in his statement on Thursday.

Dr A. Iswaran, who now works in private practice in Kuala Lumpur, described a different frustration: wanting to stay in East Malaysia but being unable to secure a permanent role.

After serving as a contract house officer and medical officer in Sarawak and Sabah, Iswaran contacted health authorities in 2021 offering to remain in Sabah if allocated a permanent position. His request was not granted and he later declined an extension of his contract.

Official correspondence he shared directed him to the standard federal appointment process, explaining that permanent roles fell under the health ministry subject to approval from the Public Service Commission.

Iswaran also recalled having to rent private housing with colleagues because staff quarters were not provided, noting that the additional allowances failed to cover rent, utilities and security deposits. During all of this, Iswaran was single. “I only had myself to look out for,” he said.

Dr R. Dinesh, a private doctor from the Klang Valley, was posted to Sarawak in 2014 – before the 2016 introduction of the contract system for junior doctors.

“If you [were] single and male, you [were] gonna be posted to Sabah or Sarawak,” Dinesh said, recalling the expectation among his peers after they completed their housemanship training in Putrajaya. Though he preferred Peninsular Malaysia, he decided to see the move as an adventure.

That included squatting at a friend’s place for a fortnight while starting work because there was no initial lodging and local hostels were full, and having to wait two months for relocation expense reimbursements.

Beyond reporting for duty

To ease the transition for new hires, Dzulkefly told parliament that the ministry had begun releasing placement results earlier and allowing eligible officers to claim full transfer allowances covering travel, meals and hotel accommodation.

But the Malaysian Medical Association (MMA) warned that official placement numbers might not necessarily reflect the reality on the ground.

In a statement on Thursday, MMA president Dr Sivanaesan Letchumanan said that feedback from the association’s Sabah branch suggested several hospital departments were experiencing a net reduction in medical officers. As a result, some doctors were forced to work on-call shifts every other day, with individual shifts lasting between 24 and 33 hours.

“Are the reported placements translating into an actual increase in doctors serving at healthcare facilities where they are most needed?” the statement said.

The association called for a facility-level breakdown of arrivals and departures, including clarification on whether official figures included doctors already serving in Sabah who had chosen to remain.

Addressing broader healthcare reforms, the government committed under Malaysia’s 2027 budget, tabled on Friday, to offer permanent positions to more than 9,000 contract doctors next year.

While the MMA welcomed the commitment in a statement on Friday, it stressed that job security alone will not solve regional shortages unless backed by a comprehensive workforce-retention strategy. The association also called for better incentives, improved working conditions and clearer career-development opportunities in Sabah and Sarawak.

“Ultimately, progress must be measured not merely by the number of doctors reporting for duty but by whether healthcare facilities have sufficient manpower to deliver safe, timely and quality care to the rakyat [public],” the MMA added.

For doctors like S in Sabah, while her colleagues’ support has helped her settle in, accepting a posting was different from deciding to stay.

“In the long run, I still want to return to serve in my home state and be closer to my family,” S said.

*Names have been changed to pseudonyms as the doctors are employed by the Ministry of Health and are not authorised to speak publicly.
