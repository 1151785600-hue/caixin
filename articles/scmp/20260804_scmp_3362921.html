Massive Attack’s Singapore ban tests legal limits of artistic freedom

SCMP | FREE | 1042 words | 2026-08-04

The banning of British band Massive Attack from Singapore for pro-Palestine acts during their concert has split opinion in the city state, with some lamenting what they see as laws that are too strict and others urging foreigners to comply with local rules.

At their gig on Wednesday, the two-man band held up a Palestinian flag and shouted “Free Palestine” along with members of the audience.

Under the Foreign National Emblems (Control of Display) Act, it is illegal to display non-Singaporean flags in the city state. Holding a protest without a permit is also prohibited under the Public Order Act.

On Friday, police and the Infocomm Media Development Authority (IMDA) said in a joint statement that the force investigated Robert Del Naja and Grant Marshall for supporting a political cause and unfurling a foreign flag during the concert. Police administered stern warnings and banned both members from re-entering Singapore.

The IMDA is also investigating a possible breach of licence conditions, including one stipulating there should be no display of flags in support of any causes.

“Notwithstanding the event organiser’s prior acknowledgement of the licence conditions, during the performance, two band members held a foreign flag on stage, and one of them also shouted ‘Free Palestine’,” the authorities said.

The IMDA will not grant any future application by the band to perform in Singapore.

On Sunday night, the Massive Attack members said on social media that they were “surprised and disappointed” to be detained by police, isolated and separately questioned, as well as subjected to hotel room searches and temporary passport confiscation.

“Before we set foot on the stage and again at the end of the show, large sections of the auditorium organically led chants of ‘Free Palestine’ – presumably aware but undeterred that this spontaneous expression alone could violate their government’s censorship laws,” they said.

The band members argued they did not imagine that “merely holding up the flag of a sovereign state recognised by 157 countries (Palestine) would violate any law” and called the experience “surreal”.

“On reflection, we are proud to have made this impromptu expression with our fans in Singapore who clearly felt a moral imperative to show solidarity with the people of Palestine in their ongoing reality of illegal occupation, apartheid and genocide, and we salute their courage in doing so,” they said.

This Week in Asia has contacted concert promoter Lushington Entertainments, an affiliate of publicly held Hotel Properties Limited, for comment.

‘Return gesture’

Concertgoers bemoaned the ban, noting that the atmosphere was orderly and the band’s flag waving was in response to the audience’s “Free Palestine” chants. The concert also touched on other themes such as AI, surveillance, corruption, war and the exploitation of workers, they said.

“[It was] a situation taken completely out of context, because the action of waving the flag wasn’t initiated by the members of the band, it was a return of their gesture to show solidarity with the audience and it felt right,” brand consultant Daniel Koh said.

While he agreed that artists should adhere to the legal systems of the country they performed in, Koh said there were always varying degrees and levels of legalities. “Trying to segregate politics from art and vice versa is a slippery slope,” he said. “I don’t see this as a ‘threat’ to domestic peace and social order but there will be naysayers.”

Another concertgoer, Janice Fong, recalled the band told the audience that they were unable to show some content on the accompanying screens – proving an intention to abide by Singaporean laws.

“I worry that this entire episode may make IMDA more cautious when assessing future performances and artistic works. If that happens, what initially seemed like progress for artistic expression in Singapore could ultimately result in two steps backwards for the range of content that audiences are allowed to experience,” said Fong, who works in finance.

Crystal Lim-Lange, chief executive of Forest Wolf leadership consultancy, questioned whether Singapore was too quick to apply rules without considering the context.

“The balance between respecting the law, preserving public order and allowing artistic expression isn’t always straightforward, and blanket enforcement may not always produce the best outcome,” Lim-Lange said in a social media post.

She did not condone laws being ignored or artists having free rein, she said, “but if Singapore wants to be seen as a global arts hub, these are the kinds of conversations we need to be mature enough to have”.

Slippery slope

The problem would be where to draw the line, though, criminal lawyers said.

Sunil Sudheesan, head of criminal law at Quahe Woo & Palmer, called the authorities’ treatment of the band “normal protocol” for foreigners who broke local laws, and it was common for culprits to be banned.

Singapore was definitely not anti-Palestine since it had donated extensively to humanitarian aid, he said. “If the band waved the Israeli flag, that same treatment would have ensued.”

Should leniency be exercised for artistic expression, this could open the floodgates to other laws being broken, he added.

Many of Cory Wong’s foreign clients had been deported or denied re-entry into Singapore after their case was over, the director at Invictus Law Corporation who specialises in criminal law said.

“The issue is not whether Singapore is for or against any involved parties, but it would be undesirable for Singapore to be used as a collateral or proxy staging ground by foreign actors,” he said.

Last August, American comedian Sammy Obeid accused Singaporean authorities of trying to censor his stand-up show, which often criticised Israel’s actions in Gaza, after his scheduled performance was cancelled.

Officials, however, said the permit was rejected because the application was lodged too late and ordered a correction order under Singapore’s fake news law. When Obeid did not comply, the government issued targeted directions to Meta Platforms and X, requiring them to put out correction notices to Singapore users who accessed, or would access, the posts.

In 2019, Swedish black metal band Watain was banned from performing in Singapore, with the authorities revoking its licence just two hours before showtime. They cited security concerns and the band’s history of “denigrating religions and promoting violence”.
