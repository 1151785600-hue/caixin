Under higher scrutiny, will Singapore remain Chinese firms’ first overseas port of call?

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1978 words | 2026-11-12

With domestic profits narrowing and production capacity expanding, China’s firms are continuing to widen their overseas footprints in search of new, more lucrative markets. In this series, we examine China Inc.’s next phase of “going global” and the complex, challenging international environment its companies have chosen to enter.

On a weekday afternoon, the basement food hall of a central shopping centre was packed. Office workers queued for Chongqing noodles, while a different noodle joint from a major Chinese hotpot chain drew its own crowd.

At opposite ends sat two restaurants specialising in fish: one founded in Shenzhen and the other serving the dish Sichuan-style. Across the aisle, a milk tea shop from Xiamen served iced drinks alongside a busy Luckin Coffee counter.

In almost every way, the scene resembled a commercial district in a major Chinese city, except for its location: the heart of Singapore.

A wave of Chinese firms in a wide range of sectors, from food and drink to technology, has flooded the Southeast Asian nation over the past few years, amid sluggish domestic demand at home.

Singapore has emerged as a top destination for Chinese companies seeking new growth markets abroad, offering a springboard for wider global expansion. But competition is also heating up in the city state, alongside scrutiny over the companies’ mainland ties – with a renewed bout of attention almost certain to arrive in the wake of the Nvidia scandal.

“Singapore is a great place to test your business in overseas markets because it offers a mix of Western tastes and consumer habits,” said Chris Pereira, the Singapore-based founder of iMpact, a consultancy that helps Chinese companies expand abroad.

Regulatory predictability allows firms to undertake long-term capital planning, Pereira noted, providing brands with a lower-risk environment to adapt their businesses.

It’s a good place to verify that the market accepts your product

Chris Pereira, iMpact

With some of the world’s top-ranked universities alongside prominent executive MBA programmes, Singapore also boasts a robust talent pool. Many founders from mainland China have also studied in the city state themselves, Pereira said.

“It’s a good place to verify that the market accepts your product, and it’s relatively safe to do that in a smaller market closer to home,” Pereira said.

Singapore, home to just 6 million people, has long been a major recipient of Chinese outbound direct investment.

China’s outbound financial direct investment into the country stood at US$16.9 billion last year, mainly in retail trade, manufacturing and business services, according to official data published in September – exceeding its flows to the rest of Southeast Asia, or to the European Union and the United States combined.

With an English-speaking multiracial population anchored by an ethnic Chinese majority, alongside a high concentration of multinational corporations, Singapore offers Chinese entrepreneurs familiarity and international exposure.

That mix has established the city state as a primary testing site for Chinese consumer brands expanding overseas.

Xiao Noodles, a Chongqing noodle chain founded in Guangzhou in 2014, made its international debut in central Singapore in late December. The company has opened three more outlets since and plans to expand across Southeast Asia and globally.

Luckin Coffee, which began its overseas expansion in Singapore in 2023, has since ventured into Malaysia and the United States. It opened its 100th Singapore outlet in September.

Several Hunan and Sichuan dining chains have followed, filling shopping malls and neighbourhoods across the city. Many retain their Chinese language-heavy menu and draw a steady crowd despite broader belt-tightening among local consumers.

Ava Cheong, a 27-year-old marketing manager, orders from Yunnan-founded tea chain Chagee at least once a week and holds top-tier status in its loyalty programme.

“I like the flavours, which are quite distinct from what other brands in Singapore offer,” Cheong said, adding that she also sought out the chain’s outlets when travelling abroad.

Chagee operates 46 stores in Singapore, having used the city state as an operational test bed before entering other markets like South Korea and the United States.

Eugene Lee, chief marketing officer of Chagee (Asia-Pacific), noted that Singapore stood out for its status as a financial hub and its “highly-developed and internationally-minded consumer market”.

“Singapore has been an important learning market for us because it gives us the opportunity to understand how Chagee translates outside China,” Lee said.

Chagee has established its regional research and development lab in the city state, he said. Lee added the company had adjusted operations based on what it had learned there, including having bigger physical store spaces that allow for dining-in and introducing alternative milk options.

The international focus is generating clear financial returns. Chagee’s overseas gross merchandise value more than doubled in the second quarter of the year, even as sales in Greater China edged down, according to the company’s latest earnings report.

Expansion abroad offers much-needed relief to Chinese brands operating in a domestic market where fierce competition has eroded margins across several sectors.

“There is a saying among Chinese entrepreneurs: the domestic market is for scale, and the international market is for profit,” said Wang Yanbo, an associate professor at the University of Hong Kong Business School.

That trend extends well beyond dining, as Chinese firms in fashion, automotive, new energy, shipping and technology seek their own slice of the pie.

Fast-fashion retailer Shein and HoYoverse, the global publishing arm of hit video game Genshin Impact, have both shifted their global headquarters to Singapore in recent years.

Particularly for digital and technology companies, having Singapore’s oversight on corporate governance and data architecture helps assure international clients and investors who are wary of Chinese legal jurisdiction, Wang said.

“Singapore’s greatest strength lies in its institutional framework; it is one of the very few societies in Asia characterised by genuine rule of law,” he added. “When Singapore makes a commitment, that commitment carries credibility.”

These factors also made Singapore a more compelling launch pad than Hong Kong for some Chinese enterprises with global ambitions, he added, as many in the international community perceived Hong Kong to be too close to the mainland.

This dynamic has contributed to the rise of so-called Singapore-washing, in which mainland firms move their corporate bases to Singapore to distance themselves from geopolitical risks. While the practice itself is not new, Western scrutiny has intensified in recent years.

“This heightened particularly during the Covid period, when relations between Beijing and Washington were at their lowest point,” Wang said, noting that international sentiment towards China and Chinese businesses was also deteriorating then.

ByteDance, the Beijing-based owner of TikTok, accelerated an expansion drive in Singapore in 2020, amid rising global popularity of the short-video app and threats of a ban during the first term of US President Donald Trump. Singapore became one of TikTok’s global headquarters alongside Los Angeles, and Singaporean citizen Chew Shou Zi was named chief executive officer in 2021.

But the platform continued to face a years-long tussle with American politicians over data privacy concerns and potential mainland influences, before finally closing a deal to divest its US operations earlier this year.

More recently, Nvidia chief Jensen Huang’s admission of an extramarital affair with Chinese businesswoman Alice Huang – who has been accused of spying for Beijing – has brought greater attention to Megaspeed, the Singapore-based company she led. A spokesman for Beijing’s foreign ministry rejected the espionage claims as “groundless fabrication designed to smear China” in a press conference on Monday.

The US has been investigating Megaspeed for allegedly playing a role in diverting advanced Nvidia AI chips into China. Singapore authorities also opened an investigation into the company late last year for suspected breaches of domestic law, without specifying the alleged offences.

Meanwhile, Beijing is also tightening oversight of home-grown firms attempting to relocate critical operations abroad. In April, Chinese authorities blocked and forced the unwinding of Meta’s US$2 billion acquisition of Manus, an agentic artificial intelligence start-up that was founded in China and had shifted its headquarters to Singapore the previous year.

Analysts noted that while these high-profile interventions were unlikely to tarnish Singapore’s broader commercial appeal, the profile of Chinese businesses seeking to establish a presence in the city might shift.

Amid an intensifying US-China technology race, sectors such as AI and advanced semiconductors have become politically sensitive.

“It is not going to be easy, especially for Chinese ventures in strategic sectors, to move overseas and set up a separate entity now without paying attention to compliance back home,” Wang said, adding that the era of mainland tech firms in these sectors using bases in Hong Kong and Singapore to secure exits through US capital had largely drawn to a close.

Johnson Ma, director of risk and compliance at Dow Jones, noted that Singapore would continue to attract Chinese businesses in industries less susceptible to geopolitical risks, such as retail and logistics.

“Singapore itself is keen to maintain its neutrality; it is neither aligned with the United States nor does it wish to become too closely aligned with China,” Ma said.

Pereira of iMpact observed that fewer Chinese firms were attempting to use Singapore to obscure their mainland roots, given how easily corporate records and ownership structures could be traced.

“It is impossible to hide nowadays,” Pereira said. “You cannot avoid compliance rules just by pretending that you are from Singapore.”

Operational and compliance costs within the city state itself are also climbing, Ma noted.

Regulatory scrutiny over inbound capital has intensified in the city state following a landmark US$2.3 billion money-laundering bust in 2023 – the largest in Singapore’s history and among the largest in the world.

The 10 arrested individuals – all from southeast China’s Fujian province, though several of them held foreign passports – had funnelled funds through bank accounts, real estate and shell companies.

The incident prompted Singapore authorities to tighten vetting across the financial sector, raising the compliance barrier of entry for Chinese companies, Ma said.

Operational costs were also rising as more players crowded out the local market, he added.

The key is to integrate more fully into the local community and establish a model of mutual benefit

Johnson Ma, Dow Jones

Equipped with vast economies of scale and stronger bargaining power over suppliers, many of these large Chinese chains hold a clear advantage over smaller local firms.

Their rapid expansion has raised concerns among local consumers and business owners that imported competition could squeeze out home-grown firms, triggering growing debate online.

In the e-commerce sector, for instance, Chinese giant Pinduoduo’s aggressive push into Singapore in recent months sparked a round of discount competition, with Alibaba Group’s Taobao running similar campaigns such as free shipping on single items. Alibaba owns the South China Morning Post.

The intensifying competition was set to increase pressure on the city’s bricks-and-mortar stores and local e-commerce operators, such as Sea’s Shopee.

The moves mirror the aggressive tactics that have drawn regulatory pushback at home. Beijing has cracked down on “involutionary” competition – a race to the bottom within some industries that has driven down quality and prices – in recent months, slapping e-commerce giants with fines and summons.

In July, the State Administration for Market Regulation and the Ministry of Commerce issued draft amendments to the country’s E-Commerce Law, adding penalties such as bigger fines, business suspension orders and encouraging industry self-regulation.

Unlike earlier waves of Chinese enterprises expanding abroad to seize new markets, companies today should prioritise regulatory compliance and domestic partnerships, Ma said, adding that this could take the form of joint ventures with local partners or knowledge transfers.

“The key is to integrate more fully into the local community and establish a model of mutual benefit,” Ma said.
