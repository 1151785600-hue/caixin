1. The crisis around the Strait of Hormuz has exposed vulnerabilities in global energy systems, prompting governments and companies to redefine the energy transition around security, resilience and affordability, rather than merely expanding renewable power.

3. Sebastian Wolf, chairman and CEO of WLF Energy, said Europe's energy crisis fueled inflation and revealed the fragility of its power system. Europe has ample sustainable-energy resources but needs to manage them better through energy storage and improved electricity market design, particularly as renewables grow and storage becomes central to balancing supply and demand. WLF Energy is integrating energy operations, battery storage and market activities.

4. Wolf noted Europe's view of industrial localization is changing. Policymakers once assumed critical energy industries should be built entirely within Europe, but recent efforts show that constructing a complete domestic supply chain can drive up costs. "Maybe doing everything in Europe is not the right way," he said. Europe once led in battery research but has fallen behind China in industrialization and scale, and China's experience demonstrates the value of clear industrial strategy and long-term planning. Wolf cited sodium-ion batteries, where Chinese companies have commercialized the technology while Europe remained cautious. He added that Chinese companies entering Europe need local partners, while European businesses must shift from acting as "teachers" to "students," especially in commercializing renewable technologies.

5. In Southeast Asia, the more pressing challenge is rapidly rising energy demand amid inadequate infrastructure, according to Lou Jianhong, vice president for international markets at Guangdong Mingyang Wind Power Industry Group. Many countries with abundant wind and solar resources lack sufficient grid infrastructure. Future projects must integrate generation, transmission, storage and green hydrogen rather than focus on a single product. ASEAN countries need stable long-term policy frameworks and clear installed-capacity targets to give investors confidence, he said.

7. Rueban Manokara, carbon-finance director at WWF and global lead of its carbon-finance working group, said the energy crisis is inseparable from climate and environmental risks. The world faces a "triple crisis" of energy insecurity, climate change and nature loss, and extreme weather is already affecting energy access. He urged governments to avoid short-term responses to supply shortages, since rushing to build without considering environmental effects could create new systemic risks.

8. Manokara said regional grid and supply-chain integration in ASEAN has begun to make tangible progress, and linking country strengths—such as Indonesia's geothermal capacity and Vietnam's wind resources—could strengthen resilience. A broader ASEAN power network could become foundational infrastructure. Long-term off-take agreements are essential for financing energy projects, and carbon markets could accelerate Asia's transition. Given the region's relatively young coal fleet, market mechanisms are needed to allow early retirement of some plants without jeopardizing energy access or affordability.
