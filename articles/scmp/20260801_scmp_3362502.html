EU-China trade war: Beijing turns the screws as Brussels tests limits of restraint

SCMP | FREE | 1146 words | 2026-08-01

Last Thursday, the European Union added 14 Chinese companies to its latest round of sanctions, accusing them of providing support for Russia’s war against Ukraine – but few of the names were recognisable to anyone outside narrow trading and compliance circles.

Officials were taken aback less than 24 hours later, when Beijing – which had been notified of the ragtag group of traders and freight forwarders to be listed by the EU as far back as June – fired back ferociously. Unlike the long notice afforded to their own diplomats, China gave Brussels just a one-hour warning, according to people familiar with the exchange.

Europe’s own listings had been carefully calibrated, intentionally omitting major Chinese actors to ensure its own member states would support the package amid fears of retaliation. Beijing, on the other hand, went for the jugular.

Added to a list that effectively bars them from buying certain goods from China were 14 EU defence organisations, including the continent’s biggest defence company, Rheinmetall, and a prominent Polish university.

The firms named are also effectively cut off from receiving those same Chinese-origin materials via overseas suppliers, an extraterritorial reach that does not exist in the EU’s own measures, and which could send a chilling effect through their supply chains, Brussels officials fear.

“They have threatened for years but until now they barked a lot and didn’t bite. This time they didn’t bark much, but they bit,” one senior official said.

In Brussels, the view is that the retaliation has less to do with the Russian sanctions and more to do with their simmering trade war, and in particular, an October deadline the European Commission has set for progress in talks aimed at rebalancing bilateral commerce with China. If this fails, the bloc has vowed to take action.

More broadly, Beijing’s flex illustrates how differently the two sides are approaching the three-month negotiating window before October – and may also offer clues as to their respective stomach for a fight.

After successfully forcing the US to back down in a brief but fierce trade war last year by weaponising its rare earth dominance, Beijing is once again seen to be pursuing “escalation dominance” – a concept rooted in Cold War nuclear strategy, now increasingly used in economic statecraft.

The term refers to the ability to out-escalate an opponent at every step. EU officials see Chinese counterparts now trying to ratchet up the pressure in an effort to force Europe to back down – and strike fear into its 27 member states before they make tough decisions over what to do about what is commonly described as the “China shock 2.0” in the autumn.

“It signals the willingness to escalate and the belief that Beijing has escalation dominance, which is totally coherent with China’s current EU policy,” said Mathieu Duchatel, director of international studies at Institut Montaigne, a Parisian think tank.

Brussels, by contrast, is moving cautiously.

Officials view the three-month negotiating window as an opportunity to see whether Beijing is prepared to address long-standing concerns over market access and industrial overcapacity. They have been surprised that Beijing has made positive noises through more than 10 rounds of talks since a ministerial meeting last month.

But privately, it has been described as a proof of concept: if China fails to deliver meaningful concessions by October – as is expected – it will strengthen the case for a far more interventionist trade policy.

In the background, Europe is quietly continuing to build an industrial policy designed to wean itself off reliance on China, but it is keen to avoid major fireworks ahead of October – or at least launching any of its own. In meetings with business representatives, trade chief Maros Sefcovic has assured them the EU will play things carefully, according to people present.

“It makes sense to avoid putting oil on the fire,” said a second senior official. “We have given them an ultimatum for October – we will see what happens by then.”

Investigations into unfair trade in China’s chemicals sector that could lead to emergency tariffs and quotas have been paused, online media platform The Capitol Forum reported, lest they upset negotiations.

This would not be the first time measures have been scrapped or paused to protect dialogue formats. In April, the EU removed sanctions on two Chinese banks, which had been listed last year, after the commerce ministry committed to stop them using crypto transactions to ship goods covered by existing EU sanctions to Russia.

The decision was controversial: some wanted to keep the obscure financiers on the list, since it had clearly gotten Beijing’s attention and therefore presented rare leverage. Sources confirmed to the SCMP, however, that the removal happened after China threatened to cancel talks on the financial sector, which officials involved were determined to preserve.

“We are cooked,” said Alicia Garcia-Herrero, an economist specialising in China’s economy at Belgian think tank Bruegel.

“Officials are prioritising the dialogue track over immediate industrial defence for a sector that is already under severe import pressure.

“For me it is quite clear: Europe is signalling that the political cost of further friction with Beijing currently outweighs the cost of delayed protection for its chemical industry,” she said, referring to the safeguard probes.

China, on the other hand, faces “no equivalent internal constraint and is willing to escalate against high-visibility European names the moment secondary sanctions touch its entities”.

After last Friday’s gambit from Beijing, business associations’ phones rang off the hook, as companies listed wondered if they could still access Chinese minerals required to make military goods, while those further down the supply chain fretted over whether they could still sell products to those named.

As of Thursday, the European Commission was still awaiting an explanation from Beijing. There are fears that the move could hamper the bloc’s rearmament efforts, as well as its plans to provide military support for Ukraine.

Crucially, the move shows that Beijing is willing to exploit its economic chokepoints to cow its opponents, a trait that is the envy of some European bureaucrats.

Working-level talks will continue in August, even as most EU officials leave Belgium for their summer holidays, with the level of negotiations to pick up in September – even if expectations are managed in Europe as to how much ground China is ready to yield.

September will see a visit of the EU’s top trade enforcer Denis Redonnet to the Chinese capital, followed by Sefcovic’s own trip, planned for early October. The EU’s national leaders are then set to discuss the China shock again in October and December.

Back in Brussels, the October deadline is not seen as an end point for tensions with China, rather the beginning of a new stage – and a test for whether Europe’s dialogue-first approach survives contact with Beijing’s escalation.
