Her nude became a sticker: Hong Kong women say image abuse thrives despite law

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1712 words | 2026-09-04

Five years after a nude photo was taken without her consent, a woman opened Instagram to find the image had been turned into a sticker and sent back to her in a chat.

Terrified that the sticker could already be circulating widely, the victim reached out to Emilia Wong Yue-kiu, a women’s rights activist and adult content creator who advocates for bodily autonomy.

But Wong was pessimistic that reporting the case would help, noting it was just one of countless similar messages for help she had received in recent years.

“Senders of the sticker may not even know where they got it from, and have no idea who the girl is, but they sent it around nonetheless. What offence did they commit then?” she said.

Advocates say Hong Kong’s voyeurism laws have been insufficient to tackle the circulation of intimate images obtained without consent and the surreptitious online chat rooms where members post such images.

They are urging city authorities to establish faster takedown mechanisms to better protect victims.

Legal framework and enforcement gaps

Hong Kong’s laws against voyeurism – encompassing four main offences – were enacted nearly five years ago in October 2021, to plug a legal loophole in tackling acts such as upskirting and revenge porn.

These four offences are voyeurism, unlawful recording or observation of intimate parts, publication of images originating from the two aforementioned acts, as well as the publication or threatened publication of intimate images without consent.

Official data obtained by the South China Morning Post shows that among the four voyeurism-related offences, the number of prosecutions in relation to arrests per year for publishing or threatening to publish intimate images without consent has been the lowest.

Between October 2021 and March this year, authorities recorded 545 cases, made 258 arrests, launched 46 prosecutions and secured 33 convictions.

That means only 17.8 per cent of arrests for this offence led to prosecutions over that period.

Cheryl Yip Cheuk-yi, advocacy officer at the Association Concerning Sexual Violence Against Women, said her organisation’s experience with victims and its work to take down intimate images have shown that the need to prove an intention to cause distress, humiliation or alarm to the photographed individual under the law was a high threshold to reach.

For cases involving online groups where people share or leak intimate images taken without consent, she said proving that someone who did not take the image – and may not know the person depicted – intended to cause distress could be difficult.

“Perhaps they were thinking that this is a leaked image, and I’m merely sharing it with other people. The requirement to prove their motive may not be something that could be handled here,” Yip said.

She added that subtle browbeating could also be challenged in courts, citing an example of someone saying “see you on a forum”, which a victim might interpret as a threat to post intimate photos but which could be dismissed as not constituting a threat.

The advocacy officer also described the legality of resharing intimate images obtained without consent that a person did not take as a “grey area”, saying the law offered little clarity on such actions.

Data from the organisation’s sexual violence victim support service showed that 63.3 per cent of image-based cases – 178 out of 281 – between 2019 and 2023 involved repeated incidents rather than one-off occurrences.

Yip suggested city authorities reference the United Kingdom, which created a specific offence for sharing such images without consent, even if there was no malicious intent involved.

“No matter what a person’s intention may be, publishing or threatening to publish intimate images without their consent would create the same level of harm,” Yip said.

Legal interpretations and limits

But barrister Michelle Wong Lap-yan, who has advised victims of sexual violence, said it did not matter whether the person knew the victim or whether the person had taken the image in voyeurism-related cases.

“One of the guilty mental elements regarding the unconsented publication of intimate photos taken without the victim's consent is that the person is reckless as to whether the image originated from the commission of the unconsented taking of intimate photos,” Wong said.

For example, if a photo showed clear signs of being taken without consent – such as an upskirt image inside an MTR train – a person ignoring those circumstances could be found reckless in court, she said.

The barrister added that publishing intimate images that were taken with consent, but without authorisation for publication, was also a crime.

But in the case of a non-consensual nude photo turned into a sticker and circulated, Wong admitted that it could be difficult for prosecutors to prove whether one could deduce that the photo was taken without consent, as well as the circumstances around how the sticker was sent and circulated.

Advancing technology poses new threats

Both Yip and activist Wong highlighted advancing technology as another threat – both in surreptitious filming equipment and in the increasingly elusive nature of online chat groups where such images are circulated.

The latter, especially, has made collecting evidence for a case and taking down non-consensual images more difficult than before, according to both advocates.

Wong said filming equipment has become much more sophisticated and accessible in the five years since the law took effect, making filming equipment ever harder to detect, but much easier to obtain.

For instance, hidden cameras could be fitted on shoes or inside bags, while some appear as accessories placed near phone cameras that would refract the lens so as to film in 360 degrees.

Wong said many of these tools were sold on e-commerce platforms for less than HK$100 (US$12.75).

Other tools included software that keeps phone screens blank to view, despite a filming function being switched on.

“With these, one can simply take a bag and place it underneath a girl’s skirt for upskirting,” Wong said. “You don’t even need to point a phone at the person to take photos.”

While online chat rooms for sharing explicit sexual content were not new to Hong Kong, Yip and Wong said these groups have increasingly hidden their activities behind layers of evasive mechanisms to avoid detection and potential criminal liability.

Activist Wong, who uncovered an upskirting group with tens of thousands of local members in 2018, said the scale of such groups had greatly shrunk since the voyeurism laws came into place, with group administrators making access to illicit photos more secretive.

Unlike the large, publicly accessible groups with boisterous discussions and doxxing she had uncovered eight years ago, these groups now have only a few thousand members, compared with tens of thousands in the past, she said.

Some required referrals from trusted members before admitting newcomers and used cryptocurrency for payments, Wong added.

Chat room organisers have also adopted self-destructing messaging methods, which have become widely popular in recent years.

“They would now put out really descriptive prompts about a video they would post, then say they would upload it for five minutes before the content was deleted. If members could catch that window, they could see it,” Wong explained.

Wong added that bans on online discussion within the groups were effectively another way to evade criminal liability, as there would be no trace of individual members commenting on shared images or requesting payment details for access.

Yip said some groups were no longer run by individual administrators but by bots or other built-in systems, making it more difficult for her organisation to request the removal of images or prove they had been posted.

Directly posting intimate images on forums or publicly accessible online messaging groups has in fact become rare, according to Ip.

“Now, they would post a link, which would be connected to a bot once clicked. Users would have to type some keywords to access the non-consensual image. This is a grey area, as they could say they had never posted the picture,” the officer explained.

Faster takedowns are urged

Yip called on Hong Kong authorities to establish faster mechanisms for removing non-consensual content to strengthen protections for victims, some of whom may be reluctant to enter lengthy judicial processes that can take years.

The officer said that, for most victims reporting cases to non-governmental organisations or the police, the most pressing concern was stopping the circulation of their images.

Expedited civil-law channels for removing non-consensual intimate images online exist in some overseas jurisdictions.

Singapore launched its Online Safety Commission in June. The commission can issue removal directions and restrict perpetrators’ accounts, with legal authority over online platforms, administrators, internet access providers and app distribution services.

Various Canadian provinces also provide for civil claims and urgent court orders to remove images.

Activist Wong said Hong Kong could draw on these overseas mechanisms, but cautioned that authorities would need to address how any system would operate on foreign platforms and within jurisdictional limits, given that online activity easily crosses legal borders.

Yip noted that, to her knowledge, Hong Kong authorities had not invoked a court disposal order available under the law to require a person to take steps to remove the intimate image concerned at any stage of legal proceedings.

“As an NGO, we have no legal authority to demand these platforms take any action. If these orders are issued with legal power, these platforms have to follow up,” Yip said.

Barrister Wong said online platforms were not subject to a specific statutory duty to pre-screen user content. Their civil liability for non-consensual intimate images would arise only if they were notified of the content and failed to remove it promptly.

“But whether it is effective against foreign platforms is questionable. Primary criminal responsibility rests with the individual uploader under the Crimes Ordinance,” Wong said, noting that city courts had warned of the need to balance freedom of expression against platform liability.

But activist Wong said Hong Kong society could not simply rely on the law to address the invasive threat posed by upskirting and image leaks, and that a broader cultural shift in attitudes towards women was needed.

“This is intrinsically bullying, which cannot be reversed by law. We need women’s social status to rise, better sex education and less taboo on discussing sex,” she said.
