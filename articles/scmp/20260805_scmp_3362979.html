Bachelet-led panel urges Latin America not to choose sides in US-China rivalry

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1576 words | 2026-08-05

Latin American countries should resist pressure to choose between the United States and China and use their vast reserves of lithium, copper and other strategic resources to strengthen their negotiating power, according to a high-level commission co-chaired by former Chilean president Michelle Bachelet and former Colombian president Ivan Duque.

But the commission acknowledges that the region’s room for manoeuvre is sharply constrained in security, technology and critical infrastructure, areas in which Washington remains dominant and has sought to curb Beijing’s growing influence.

The report, released on Tuesday by the UN Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean, argues that countries can work with different partners on different issues – maintaining security relationships with Washington while diversifying trade and investment towards China and other economies.

Presenting the report in Santiago, Duque said Latin America needed “a geopolitical vision of our own, not dictated to us”, arguing that countries should not become trapped in fixed alignments or automatically take sides between rival powers.

Governments should instead retain the flexibility to align with different partners according to their strategic priorities, shared principles and opportunities for trade and investment, he said.

Bachelet said the region had gained leverage because intensifying competition among global powers had increased the value of its resources.

“The world has changed, and the countries of Latin America and the Caribbean have many things that the world needs,” she said.

The commission identifies Latin America’s natural resources as a potentially powerful negotiating tool.

The region holds an estimated 46 per cent of the world’s lithium reserves, 35 per cent of its copper, 28 per cent of its graphite and 23 per cent of its rare earths, according to the report.

It is also the world’s leading net food-exporting region, hosts nearly half of the planet’s terrestrial biodiversity and possesses about one-third of its freshwater resources.

China becomes a new option in Latin American negotiations

Qian Liu, a Chinese business executive who served on the commission, said Beijing’s increasing economic presence provided Latin American governments with another option as they negotiated with major powers.

“The rise of China is providing the world with a different option and opportunity, and competition is good,” said Liu, founder and chief executive of Wusawa.

China has become South America’s largest trading partner, she noted, while Chinese and other Asian companies have shown strong interest in expanding their operations in the region.

But possessing large mineral reserves was not the same as capturing their economic value, Liu warned. Much of the region’s mineral production was still exported in raw or lightly refined form.

She urged governments to negotiate access to critical minerals as part of broader investment packages incorporating infrastructure, local processing, workforce development and technology transfer.

Regional coordination could also help countries move from being price takers towards becoming strategic partners with greater influence over the rules governing critical-mineral markets, she said.

The report similarly warned that the region’s resources would not automatically translate into economic development or greater global influence.

It called on governments to move beyond the extraction and export of raw materials by requiring foreign investment to support local processing, technology transfer, supplier networks and more advanced manufacturing.

China has already established a strong investment presence in the “lithium triangle” of Argentina, Bolivia and Chile, as well as in the copper industries of several countries, the report said.

US and China compete over strategic infrastructure in Latin America

Beijing is also a major source of infrastructure financing. More than 20 Latin American and Caribbean countries have signed memorandums of understanding to participate in China’s Belt and Road Initiative, while seven had joined the Beijing-backed Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank as of March.

At the same time, the US has intensified efforts to prevent Chinese companies from gaining control of strategic infrastructure and assets in the western hemisphere.

The commission said decisions involving energy, critical minerals, digital infrastructure and other strategic sectors were increasingly subject to outside pressure as national security considerations became intertwined with economic relations.

ECLAC executive secretary Jose Manuel Salazar-Xirinachs acknowledged that some governments remained apprehensive about addressing geopolitical questions viewed as “delicate and even risky”.

But declining to confront those questions was itself a choice, he argued.

“Paralysis and inertia are not neutral; they are decisions taken by virtue of inaction,” Salazar-Xirinachs said, calling for deliberate and coordinated action supported by a long-term vision.

“It is possible to be prudent and strategic at the same time. Indeed, that is exactly what we have to be at this time.”

The report draws an important distinction between the region’s economic and security relationships.

China is now the largest trading partner for much of South America and ranks among the three largest sources of imports for almost every Latin American and Caribbean country, it said.

Mexico, Central America and the Caribbean, however, remain much more closely tied to the US through trade, investment and remittances.

In security, Washington remains “by far the dominant external player”, supported by an extensive network of military training, joint exercises, intelligence-sharing and cooperation against organised crime.

China has nevertheless expanded its defence diplomacy in the region. Since 2020, the number of Latin American and Caribbean military personnel attending Chinese military colleges has been more than five times the number enrolled in US institutions, the report said, citing previous research.

Stronger pressure to align with US on security, tech and infrastructure expected

Despite Beijing’s growing engagement, the commission concluded that Latin American governments would face considerably stronger pressure to align with Washington on security, advanced technology and critical infrastructure than on trade.

That assessment comes amid the Trump administration’s renewed assertion of US primacy in the western hemisphere and warnings that Washington will oppose Chinese control of ports, telecommunications networks and other strategic facilities in the region.

Rather than pursuing the same alignment across every aspect of foreign policy, the commission recommends “variable-geometry” partnerships: coalitions with different countries based on the issue being addressed.

A government could, for example, cooperate closely with Washington on security while maintaining extensive trade and investment ties with China and working with the European Union or emerging economies on climate policy, digital governance or development financing.

The commission said Latin American governments needed to develop greater “geopolitical muscle” – analytical capabilities within government, business and academia to identify their dependence on foreign powers, anticipate pressure and protect critical economic vulnerabilities.

It also called for stronger regional coordination, but stopped short of proposing another broad Latin American organisation.

Political divisions make comprehensive regional consensus unrealistic, it said. Governments should instead form smaller coalitions around specific areas where they share interests, such as critical minerals, energy, food security, digital standards, infrastructure and the fight against transnational organised crime.

Duque said the commission’s recommendations were intended as a practical road map rather than a geopolitical doctrine and did not require unanimity among the region’s ideologically divided governments.

Fears over global fragmentation, erosion of multilateralism

The commission was co-chaired by Bachelet, a centre-left former president of Chile, and Duque, a conservative former president of Colombia. Its 13 members included political leaders, economists, business executives and foreign-policy specialists from Latin America, China, the US, Europe, India and Africa.

The ideological contrast between its co-chairs underscored the commission’s broad political spectrum. Both said members nevertheless reached broad agreement.

Bachelet is currently a candidate to succeed Antonio Guterres as UN secretary general. She was nominated in February by the governments of Chile, Brazil and Mexico.

She said the erosion of multilateralism and the growing fragmentation of global governance were of particular concern to her.

The report therefore provides a window into her approach to the increasing divide between Washington and Beijing and the role that Latin America and other developing regions could play in an increasingly fragmented international system.

The commission described the world as moving away from a US-dominated order towards an asymmetric form of multipolar competition centred on the US-China rivalry.

China’s share of global gross domestic product, measured by purchasing power parity, rose from 4 per cent in 1990 to 19 per cent in 2024, while the G7’s combined share fell from 45 per cent to 30 per cent, the report said.

Bachelet also highlighted the commission’s conclusion that US soft power had declined in recent years while China’s had increased, reflecting a broader redistribution of economic, technological and political influence.

Nevertheless, the commission warned Latin American governments against replacing dependence on one great power with dependence on another.

The region’s goal, it said, should be to preserve flexibility, reduce vulnerabilities and extract greater development benefits from the growing international demand for its resources.

“Global powers are already acting,” the report said. “The United States, China and the European Union are deploying comprehensive strategies to secure access to the region’s resources.”

The central question was whether Latin American countries would respond collectively and strategically or continue to negotiate with major powers individually and reactively, the commission said.

Without a deliberate strategy of its own, the report warned, the region would not remain neutral. It would instead be shaped by the priorities and actions of more powerful countries.

“Our geopolitical future does not depend on what is set by a small number of countries,” Duque said.

Instead, he argued, it would depend on whether Latin America could identify its strengths and opportunities and establish its own role in the world.
