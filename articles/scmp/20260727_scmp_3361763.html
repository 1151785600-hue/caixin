How China is using film diplomacy to project a better image across Africa

SCMP | FREE | 1050 words | 2026-07-27

Beijing’s soft power drive to win African hearts through cinema was on display recently in Bujumbura, where Chinese dragon and lion dances merged with sacred Burundian drums to open Chinese Film Week.

The Chinese embassy in Bujumbura, the economic capital of Burundi, said the July 15 event offered “a beautiful example of cultural exchanges between the two countries” and that both sides were determined to “make cinema a vehicle for Sino-Burundian friendship”.

Zhu Kewei, China’s ambassador to the East African country, called cinema “a force capable of bridging language barriers, borders and cultures”, adding that “film carries a nation’s historical memories, cultural traditions, values and the emotions of its people”.

“Many African friends have told me that they came to know and understand China through Chinese kung fu movies and martial arts stars,” she said at the event, referring to the famous Bruce Lee and Jackie Chan movies of the 1970s and 1980s.

In addition to the film screenings, China and Burundi plan to expand cooperation through creator exchanges, artist mobility, university partnerships, co-productions and skills transfer.

As part of the China-Africa Year of Human and Cultural Exchanges, the festival in Burundi lined up seven Chinese films, including The Wandering Earth II, Changan and The Snow Leopard.

Screenings took place in major cities like Bujumbura, Ngozi and political capital Gitega.

China’s film diplomacy is a pan-African endeavour, with Beijing in recent months boosting the number of screenings across several countries, including Egypt, Ethiopia, Kenya, Morocco, Nigeria, South Africa, Uganda and Zimbabwe.

Observers say the expanded screenings and cultural exchanges are part of a wider effort by Beijing to change how Africans view China and build lasting ties with local media and institutions.

Alessandro Jedlowski, an associate professor in political sociology and African Studies at Bordeaux Institute of Political Science (Sciences Po Bordeaux), said China’s travelling film weeks aligned with a broader strategy to reshape African perceptions of the Asian economic giant.

Beijing had invested heavily in media circulation to improve the international image of its footprint in Africa, Jedlowski said.

“These events allow China to present everyday, non‑military narratives that contrast with geopolitical framings and help normalise Chinese modernity as familiar and accessible.”

Beyond public perception, the screenings strengthen institutional connections, he added, suggesting that power arises from a complex, unpredictable dynamic rather than a rigid, top-down strategy.

“China seeks to embed itself in African media ecologies not as a monolithic actor but as one capable of adapting to local contexts.”

In Nairobi, a film about hybrid rice pioneer Yuan Longping headlined a cultural showcase last month, using the scientist’s life story to draw attention to China’s agricultural cooperation with African countries.

The film was paired with Hi, Mom, a blockbuster comedy-drama, and the historical drama Mao Zedong and Qi Baishi, which traces the relationship between the Chinese leader and the master painter.

Zhang Zhizhong, a senior diplomat at the Chinese embassy in Kenya, described films as “a powerful expression of a thriving civilisation and an important window into a country’s culture”.

“Chinese cinema has seen a growing number of brilliant and thought-provoking masterpieces [in recent years],” Zhang added.

Last year, the Chinese animated film Ne Zha 2 took the world by storm, grossing US$2.2 billion to rank fifth in all-time global box office takings while becoming the highest-grossing animated film in cinema history.

“I hope that, through these films, our friends in Kenya can experience the true face of China, the pulse of its development and the charm of its culture,” Zhang said.

The films screened in Kenyan capital Nairobi came from Hunan province, the birthplace of Mao Zedong and the cradle of hybrid rice.

Zhang said Hunan had been approved by the Chinese government as a pilot zone for in-depth China-Africa economic and trade cooperation.

The central Chinese province hosts the China-Africa Economic and Trade Expo every two years and is at the forefront of cooperation with Africa.

“But what you may not know is that Hunan is also a cultural powerhouse in China,” Zhang said. “I have no doubt that Hunan will continue to lead the way in cultural and people-to-people exchanges with Africa.”

Meanwhile, in Morocco to the northwest, the African tour of the revolutionary drama My Maan Ling began on June 29 at the Confucius Institute of Hassan II University in Casablanca, marking its first public screening on the continent.

Inspired by the true story of Hainan province’s all-female Red Army detachment in the 1930s, the film honours the revolutionary ideals and heroism of its fighters.

Additional screenings were later scheduled at Mohammed V University in Rabat and various state-owned enterprise sites in Morocco.

Jedlowski noted that Chinese filmmakers did more than just show their movies in Africa, saying they were also actively collaborating with African partners to co-produce films.

In his research, Jedlowski has come across several initiatives in which Chinese media companies collaborate directly with local entities.

For instance, StarTimes, a large pay-TV operator and digital television infrastructure provider, has become a major player in the distribution and production of African content.

The company produces local drama series such as Alagbara in Nigeria and is developing entertainment formats exported across multiple African countries.

Various Chinese state‑funded frameworks, such as the China–Africa Film and Television Cooperation Project and the Silk Road Film and Television Bridge Project, also support dubbing African films into African languages and encourage joint production, Jedlowski said.

A notable example is the planned Sino-Nigerian feature 30 Days in China, produced by Corporate World Entertainment, FilmOne Entertainment and HuaHua Media.

Although the project was interrupted by the coronavirus pandemic and has yet to be released, it is a clear instance of active co‑production that could serve as a model for feature projects, according to Jedlowski.

That said, audience response has been mixed.

Jedlowski observed that while dubbed dramas found enthusiastic viewers, they also drew criticism, such as from Nigerian filmmakers protesting foreign series dubbed into Yoruba or Hausa.

“Some viewers appreciate relatable everyday narratives. Others prefer Nollywood or local productions,” he said, referring to Nigeria’s film industry.

“Acceptance depends on how well they align with local expectations, linguistic identities and existing media preferences.”
