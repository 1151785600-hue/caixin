Dr Ron Roukema on education, leadership and life at Hong Kong International School

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1578 words | 2026-10-18

I WAS BORN in October 1972, in New Jersey, in the United States. My parents were both immigrants – young adults who migrated during World War II, in the early 1940s. My father is Dutch and my mother is Italian, from Sicily. As children, we were always told that if we worked for the state, we’d be well taken care of.

MY MUM WAS a nurse and she met my father in New York. She worked in a hospital, and he was a police officer. My father retired when he was in his 40s after he broke his back. We then moved to North Carolina, which we chose because it was the exact halfway point between my two sets of grandparents (in New Jersey and Florida).

MY FAMILY IS made up of educators. I’m the youngest of five siblings. I have three older sisters, and my parents adopted the son of my father’s friend, when I was about 12. My eldest sister is a university professor and runs a business that does training with government departments. My middle sister retired in one state, moved to another and continues to teach in public schools. My youngest sister is a psychologist and also runs a business working with the government. My brother’s birth father was also in the police force; they were having problems at home, so he lived with us until he was 20. It was just like having a bigger brother, but also a friend who was a role model in some ways. I know he’s a firefighter now in the mountains, but I haven’t seen him in 15 or 20 years.

I LOVE SCHOOLS in general. I grew up in a family where everybody enjoyed going to school. I was in about 20 organisations and clubs in secondary school, playing sports and joining activities to make friends. I was voted “Most Involved” and I was also the lead in the school musical, captain of the wrestling and baseball teams, and field conductor for the marching band. Every now and then, I’d choose classes to be with friends, only to regret it when the classroom turned into more play than study.

GROWING UP, WE didn’t really have the funds to pay for university. I got a full scholarship as a teaching fellow, to teach in the state of North Carolina for four years after graduation, in exchange for tuition. During university (at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill), I continued to play baseball and also did cheerleading. I got my master’s at East Carolina University and my doctorate at North Carolina State University.

MY WIFE AND I went to the same secondary school, but we didn’t meet until university – which is good, because I grew up a lot between the time that I met her and the time that I graduated. I was a fourth-year student, and she was a first-year student. She’s an accomplished photographer and has taught photography at different levels. We’ve been married for 28 years.

MY FIRST JOB was as a fill-in teacher of kindergarten classes at Southern Pines Elementary; that lasted about three months before I realised that was the wrong age group for me. I had kids tell me, “I have to go to the bathroom.” I’d reply, “Well, just go down the hall and go to the bathroom.” But they were like, “No, no, no. We have to walk as a class.” Learning to teach kindergarten was very different. After that, I went back to Pinecrest High School to teach secondary-school history.

MY FIRST ADMINISTRATIVE job was the last year that Southern Pines Middle School existed (2000). I was tasked with converting the academic framework into an International Baccalaureate Primary Years Programme and helping my former colleagues transition, either to other schools or to different areas of education.

ACTUALLY, I NEVER wanted to go into administration. I love teaching. But Joyce Edwards, my associate principal at the time, wanted me to go along with her because she thought I asked good questions. Joyce gave me two options: be the middle-school principal at the new school we’d just built, or move to Durham, in North Carolina, and get a doctorate and go to work at this new school out there.

SO, I MOVED to Durham, in 2000. I helped introduce technology programmes at Eno Valley Elementary School as an administrator, where they told me it would take three to five years to build a technology programme. We built it in seven months.

IN 2007, AROUND the time that my wife and I were expecting our first child, I was offered a job at an international school in Beijing. But instead of going there, we headed to Shanghai American School, where I served as the founding principal of the middle school at the Pudong campus. I worked there for several years before taking my first headship, reporting directly to the board, at Marymount International School, in Paris, France.

WE WERE HAPPY in Paris. We stayed for four years. It was a great school that aligned with my beliefs and philosophies. When our second daughter was born, we started thinking about moving back to Asia. One of the things that drew me towards Hong Kong International School (HKIS) is my Christian faith and the way the school embraces Christianity.

WHAT’S THE PURPOSE of knowledge? I think that’s why religion is important to me, because to understand and to learn – it has to be for a greater purpose, and that greater purpose, in the end, is understanding God’s word and what he hopes for us. In the meantime, it’s also about using your knowledge to serve others and develop a culture that people want to be part of, and to understand that everything we do and everything that happens is for a greater purpose.

MY FIRST IMPRESSION of Hong Kong was in 2007 – right here in this very office, purely by chance. HKIS was hosting a meeting for middle-school principals, and this office I’m in was previously a boardroom. I joined HKIS as the upper-primary principal in 2014. About a year later, they asked me to move into a larger role, as the associate head of school, or what they called the provost at the time, because of my background in education and the knowledge that I had built up over 20-plus years of experience, which was what the school was hoping for and needing at the time. So, I supported them in that role.

DURING THAT FIRST visit, we brought Eva, our oldest, and went to Hong Kong Disneyland. On a trip to Stanley, I ran into the then-head of school, Richard Mueller. We were having lunch at Pizza Express with my wife’s cousins when Richard and his wife walked by and we started chatting. That brief encounter showed me how close and open people in Hong Kong are across both the expat and local communities. It made me feel that I would be happy here, both on an academic and personal level.

I AM HORRIBLE with raising my own children, in the sense of being a parent and an educator at the same school where they go and I work. My oldest graduated two years ago, my youngest is in Year Five and my second daughter is in Year Seven. Whenever I go to an open house or a play to support my kids, I usually get pulled away. It’s hard to just be a dad there, because other parents don’t see me for my kids – they see me for them.

STILL, FOR THE most part, my kids have loved having me around. I walk my son to school every day, and he’s very happy to do it. Whenever my oldest saw me in secondary school, she’d run over, give me a hug and make sure her friends got to say hello.

I WORK IN Hong Kong, but I live in North Carolina. Every summer and Christmas, we go home and see family and friends that we went to school and university with. We have a nice home there – the kind of place people want to be. No dates, no deadlines, but we know that’s eventually what we’ll go back to. It’s just exciting to be around people who have the same origins that I have.

I’M PART OF what we call the “US Gourmet Eating Club”, where we go eat fast food in Hong Kong. It’s just a bunch of people; some are associated with the school, some are not. We get together for new restaurant openings. We’re looking forward to the opening of (American fast-food restaurant) Dairy Queen here in Hong Kong – we joke around and call it “gourmet”.

MY JOY OF working in schools started before I had kids, and it will continue after they have grown up. I feel like I’ve made an impact in each role I’ve held. When I was a teacher, it was daily conversations to help people make decisions that were impactful. Most of my conversations today are about helping students develop lifelong skills, so that they can be successful in the future. I’m interested in seeing how communities come together and grow.
