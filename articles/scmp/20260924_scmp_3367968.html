Could Chinese explorers have settled New Zealand before the Maori?

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2020 words | 2026-09-24

For generations, the narrative of New Zealand’s human settlement has held that Polynesian voyagers – the ancestors of the Maori – arrived and settled the islands in the 13th century, followed by European explorers in the 17th century.

This timeline underpins New Zealand’s history and its political model. The Treaty of Waitangi, signed in 1840 between the British Crown and about 540 Maori chiefs, served as New Zealand’s founding document, paving the way for Britain to claim sovereignty and incorporate the country into the British Empire.

Outside the halls of universities and government institutions, two independent researchers are on a quest to discover pre-Maori Chinese presence in New Zealand.

Citing underwater shipwrecks, carbon-dated structures and anomalous artefacts as evidence, scholar and author Sheng-Wei Wang has proposed a theory that a Chinese “treasure ship” fleet met its end there around 1435-1436 owing to the impact of a meteorite.

Wang also contends that Chinese mining and trading activities date back to 190BC, challenging the accepted 13th-century Maori settlement timeline and suggesting that these pre-European contacts have been overlooked or suppressed.

While mainstream academics and Maori groups voice scepticism, Wang and others argue that acknowledging this lesser-known history could reshape New Zealand’s national identity and improve its relations with China.

Speaking this month at the Commonwealth of World Chinatowns conference in Penang, Malaysia, Wang raised the question of whether fleet leader Zheng He died in New Zealand during his final voyage.

Zheng was a Chinese admiral who led seven massive naval expeditions across the Indian Ocean during the early Ming dynasty.

While Zheng is widely believed to have died in 1433 in Calicut, or today’s Kozhikode, a coastal city in southern India, after setting sail in 1431, his exact fate was still unverified, Wang said.

“We know that Zheng disappeared during the seventh voyage. There were different kinds of proposals about what happened to him.

“Could he [have died] while he visited the Americas? Or in India near Calicut – he was ill, and because the weather was so hot that his body couldn’t be brought back to China? Or just like in this case that he died because of the unexpected tragic event?”

Wang said a meteorite was believed to have crashed in the Tasman Sea, southwest of New Zealand.

“My estimate of the date is around 1435 or 1436, when a squadron of the Ming Treasure Fleet reached New Zealand after they explored the Americas during their seventh voyage to the Indian Ocean and beyond,” she said, adding that the impact carbonised all the ships in the harbour.

“One of the possibilities is that the whole fleet disappeared there, which may have included Zheng because there is no satisfactory evidence to show where or when he died,” she said. “There was no convincing record, so I am proposing that.”

Wang’s research draws on field surveys by the late independent British surveyor T.C. Bell, who used magnetic anomaly surveys to detect underwater and buried structures throughout New Zealand. The method measures local variations in the Earth’s magnetic field to locate metal objects.

“When Bell went to New Zealand for surveys in 2003, 2006 and 2017 … he was able to detect 78 shipwrecks of various dimensions,” she said.

“Twenty-four of them are of the super-junk or treasure-ship size. They are around over 100 metres (328 feet) in length, about 40-something [metres] in width, and all these large ships are characterised by their unique double-fluke anchors.”

The specialised Chinese anchor consisted of a central wooden bar connected to two huge rocks, Wang said, and was designed to cut into the sea floor to secure the ship in place.

She proposed a route for Zheng’s last voyage that “went to America for several months and came back by rounding the southern part of South America, Cape Horn. They followed the Humboldt Current, then the [South] Equatorial Current and they could have gone to Asia”.

“Naturally they would have been brought westward to New Zealand.”

Wang described the seventh fleet as on its way back to China. Carbon dating results indicated a Chinese presence in New Zealand predated the seventh Ming voyage, extending from as early as 190BC through 1600, Wang said.

She pointed to a “scrub-covered, small-moated Chinese fort platform” at Governors Bay, South Island, which was carbon-dated to 190BC, and the years 800 and 1600.

“In my opinion, in Bell’s opinion, [New Zealand] became the headquarters of Chinese business trading ships.”

“There were already many Chinese who had lived there for thousands of years,” Wang said, explaining they were attracted by “coal mining and other metallic ores in New Zealand”, including “very rich ores” of gold and silver.

She also noted the presence of canals, smelter sites and barrack blocks that she believed bore Chinese characteristics.

“It is clear that a Chinese presence in New Zealand started at least from 190BC and lasted until the tragic [meteorite] event took place,” she said. “The next wave of Chinese immigration started in the 19th century.”

Wang also cited stone carvings as possible evidence, with a massive gateway rock on the North Island bearing an elephant petroglyph and linear script.

“New Zealand does not have native elephants, and the Maori had no history of carving elephants either,” she said. “Even the Europeans did not do much elephant carving. This is very interesting. But the Chinese knew elephants.”

However, according to Li Yuelin, president of the Seven Seas Institute of Chinese Calligraphy in Illinois, the workmanship on the elephant rock carving “seems quite primitive and its origin remains uncertain”.

Li, who is also a physicist at the Argonne National Laboratory near Chicago, said that by the period Wang identified, from 200BC to 1000AD, the Chinese writing system had already evolved well beyond the petroglyph stage into a mature script, and the carved symbol did not match early oracle bone forms for “elephant”.

“Regarding the carving itself, the tool marks and overall technique seem relatively rudimentary and do not exhibit features typically associated with Chinese stone inscriptions from the period in question,” he said.

As for a piece of rock bearing a symbol resembling the Chinese character for mountain, found by Bell’s son Dave in the Moeraki cliffs in the South Island, Li said the motif could not “be confidently associated with the Chinese character”.

A 3D scan showed a raised form where the surrounding material was removed so that the motif stood above the surface, he added, rather than a traditional intaglio method typically preferred in Chinese stone inscriptions in which the strokes were cut below the surrounding rock for efficiency.

“Even if we consider the form as a raised, positive-relief character, the background around such carvings is usually cut back to a fairly even depth to make the strokes stand out clearly,” Li said.

“That kind of consistency is not present here; the surrounding relief is irregular and lacks the controlled excavation one would expect from a deliberate raised-relief inscription.”

Nevertheless, Wang maintained that “the hidden history of New Zealand has to be uncovered so that the government will have to implement more balanced policies for a lasting peace and future”, especially regarding the country’s relations with and protection of the rights of the indigenous Maori people.

In that vein, Robert Jones, director of the forthcoming New Zealand Museum of Anomalous Antiquities, said a growing collection of enigmatic artefacts discovered across New Zealand could indicate pre-European Chinese contact largely overlooked by mainstream academia.

“While we can explain away some evidence and anomalies in isolation, when you count the sheer quantity of anomalies, it begs the question: when does it become a pattern?” Jones asked at the conference in Penang.

He and his team presented replicas of several artefacts, including the Korotangi, a dark green serpentine stone bird discovered in 1878. The bird of sorrow is a sacred artefact believed to have been brought to New Zealand on the Maori Tainui canoe.

“The carving displays extremely fine feathering detail and shaping, which is consistent with the kinds of things that could only be produced by metal tools, not the traditional stone tools that were used by the Polynesians,” Jones said, adding that the stone likely originated from Indonesia or China.

Jones also singled out the Tamil Bell, a bronze ship’s bell discovered in about 1841 by a missionary in a Maori village where it was used as a pot for cooking potatoes. The bell has an inscription in Tamil, a language mainly spoken in southern India, Sri Lanka and Singapore.

Jones raised a theory that the artefact could be linked to the treasure fleets of the Ming dynasty under Zheng.

“If the 15th-century dating is true, the bell would tie neatly into what researcher Gavin Menzies proposed in his book 1421: The Year China Discovered the World, in which he theorised that a Tamil ship could have appeared with one of Zheng He’s treasure fleets for protection from pirates or pursuing trade opportunities,” he said.

“Subsequently, it was marooned, with its cargo later dispersed and all looted by local Maori.”

Jones further cited the discovery of porcelain crates in Northland, a region in the North Island, that he said matched the style of blue-and-white porcelain produced during the Ming dynasty Yongle Emperor period (1402–1424), while Zheng’s voyages were known to carry Chinese porcelain for trade.

“[The discoverer’s] description of translucent plates through which the sunlight could visibly pass matches the type of eggshell porcelain that was first introduced during the Yongle period,” he said.

Jones acknowledged that such theories faced opposition, including from indigenous Maori who have secured settlements from the New Zealand government based on their status as the country’s first inhabitants.

“We can be charitable and remove the possibility of any cover-up of these Chinese connections,” he said. “But we can also infer that public officials in New Zealand would be scared to pursue lines of inquiry that could potentially undermine the Eurocentric accounts of our history.”

“The significance of recognising these connections is not simply historical but existential, especially as we enter into a new multifaceted cold war with China, which I might add, is currently New Zealand’s largest trading partner.”

“We are still almost completely alone in searching for the true provenance of these artefacts,” he added. “The Chinese connections, however, are certainly not so easily dismissible as others have since tried to claim.”

For Jones, uncovering this hidden past is both culturally and geopolitically vital. He argued that New Zealand’s traditional Eurocentric perspective had historically entangled the country in “foreign imperialist wars that had nothing to do with us”.

“The reality is New Zealand is a nation in the South Pacific, and China is a major power in the Pacific, and as our main trading partner and having genuine historical ties, we are much closer to China than we are to [Britain],” he said.

“If New Zealanders could appreciate that China is a part of our history, and that the Chinese are not some ‘other’ out there who are foreign or alien … I think Kiwis would actually appreciate our museum initiative and uncover greater clarity in their sense of self.”

While stating that he and his team were not credentialled academics, Jones said they were committed to rigorous inquiry.

“It is usually outsiders who have unique perspectives who contribute very interesting things. We do not have any qualifications. We are just curious, but we try to follow the scientific process and proper procedures of archaeological excavations as closely as possible.”

Mainstream historians remain sceptical of pre-Maori theories, but Jones described his museum as seeking to fill a void for discussion and research into New Zealand’s early history.

“We do think there is a genuine interest among young people,” he said. “There is this hesitancy because people know that this is a very sensitive thing.”

“Hopefully, once the institution is up and running, there will be that legitimacy and that authority that people will be able to defer to and have confidence that they can research this without being completely destroyed economically, socially or politically.”
