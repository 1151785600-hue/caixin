How US became trapped in ‘dreadful situation’ of its own making over Iran war

SCMP | FREE | 1089 words | 2026-08-06

The United States is staring a historic “strategic defeat” in the face five months after launching the Iran war at Israel’s behest, according to Middle East observers.

The Islamic Republic is unlikely to fully relinquish its new-found hegemony over the Strait of Hormuz, despite US President Donald Trump’s rhetorical mix of daily ultimatums and optimism about a deal, they say. Doing so would be giving up its leverage over the global economy – by far its greatest weapon in a war it cannot win militarily.

Having concluded that its June 17 memorandum of understanding with the US is all but dead, Iran is prepared to absorb all the military punishment the Trump administration can dish out.

Far from capitulating, the Revolutionary Guards and their Axis of Resistance allies across the Middle East have continued to escalate the conflict, targeting energy infrastructure and supply chains in the Red Sea, Suez Canal and the eastern Mediterranean. Saudi Arabia, Jordan and Egypt have all been targeted, and Syria could be next.

Late on Wednesday, Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei said negotiations with Oman on a proposed reopening of the strait were “forward-looking” and a deal would be struck “if certain third parties do not obstruct this process”.

While not mentioned, it was obvious who this “third party” would be. Iranian state television earlier reported that the reopening would be contingent “on a change in US behaviour”.

‘Extremely difficult predicament’

The Trump administration finds itself “in an extremely difficult predicament of its own making”, according to Giorgio Cafiero, CEO of Gulf State Analytics, a Washington risk consultancy.

Washington faced a choice between pursuing a war that the US “is very unlikely to win or accepting a diplomatic settlement that would carry significant political costs for President Trump”, he said.

In Tehran, on the other hand, there is a “growing belief that only military escalation can restore deterrence”, and that diplomacy with the current US administration will “probably not produce an outcome that serves Iran’s interests”.

“Taken together, these dynamics leave little reason for optimism that the crisis will de-escalate any time soon,” Cafiero said.

Trump was “desperate”, said Hussein Ibish, senior resident scholar at the Washington-based Arab Gulf States Institute. The president had “manoeuvred himself into a dreadful situation” where anything he did pointed to “one of two bad outcomes”: acquiescing to Iranian power in the strait, or waging a years-long on-and-off air war.

A climbdown would be a “huge strategic defeat” for the US. Ibish said it would be the “first major step of the decline of the US dramatically as a global power” – undermining its standing in a way reminiscent of the decline of British and French power after they joined forces with Israel to briefly seize control of the Suez Canal in 1956.

But intensifying the war would create a “new structure of open-ended, kinetic and aggressive containment against Iran” turning it into “another forever war, even without boots on the ground”, Ibish said.

The groundwork for such a conflict is already being laid in the Middle East. Iraq, the region’s second largest oil exporter, is now a launch pad for drone and missile attacks against Saudi Arabia and Jordan by militias which, while officially part of the country’s armed forces, are armed and led by the Guards – much like the Lebanese Hezbollah.

In a departure from its policy of neutrality, Saudi Arabia responded to drone attacks against its energy infrastructure on July 29 by carrying out a joint air operation with the US against pro-Iran militias across Iraq.

Similarly, Riyadh and Yemen’s Houthi rebels have exchanged tit-for-tat air strikes with growing frequency since the Iran-allied militia imposed an embargo on Saudi shipping in the Red Sea on July 20.

Following the latest clash on Wednesday, the Houthis escalated their aggression by sinking an Indian ship trying to sail past the port of Hodeidah, which the militia controls. After four years of relative peace between the Houthis and the Saudi-backed government, the civil war in Yemen looks on the brink of resuming.

Stuck in the middle

This all underscored “just how vulnerable” the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) states – Saudi Arabia, United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Kuwait, Oman and Bahrain – were to a regional conflict involving an emboldened Islamic Republic viewing them as complicit in the Iran war, Cafiero said.

Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman’s intervention on Sunday to persuade Trump not to carry out his threatened escalation against Tehran did not merely seek to prevent more Iranian attacks against the GCC, Cafiero said. Rather, it was rooted in the GCC’s recognition that regional order “ultimately requires a workable relationship with Tehran”.

The GCC states realised that by striking them, Iran was attacking the entire global economy and putting enormous pressure on the US to “end the war on whatever terms”, Ibish said.

“It is [the Iranians’] ace card, and they are not going to give it up, so the Gulf countries know they remain in the crosshairs now and into the foreseeable future,” Ibish said. This situation was “exactly the disaster they foresaw” but failed to stop.

The only way for GCC members to mitigate the blowback is to “develop and pursue their own bilateral dialogues with Tehran”, he said. At the same time, they have to keep Washington on their side to supply them with the “very expensive and complex” missile defence systems to counter Iran’s “much cheaper but very effective” drone technology.

With Iran and its Axis of Resistance partners bent on implementing their “unity of theatres” strategy, however, it may only be a matter of time before the GCC states and their regional allies – Egypt, Jordan, Pakistan and Turkey – join forces to deter and, if necessary, retaliate.

The seeds of such an alliance were planted on July 30, when Saudi Arabia unveiled plans to establish a multinational maritime defence coalition ensuring freedom of navigation in the Red Sea, Bab al-Mandab Strait and Gulf of Aden.

“As this crisis has prolonged and expanded since late February, Riyadh’s approach has evolved,” Cafiero said. While continuing to prioritise diplomacy, he said the kingdom has shown a “greater willingness” to use carefully calibrated military responses aimed at deterring attacks.

Despite wanting to avoid being drawn into the war with Iran “in a major way”, Ibish said the Gulf states at some point “are going to have to relent and get involved”.

“What the exact trigger is, nobody knows but them,” he added.
