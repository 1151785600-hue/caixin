Chickens in Malaysia are producing fewer eggs. Is haze a leading cause?

SCMP | FREE | 1327 words | 2026-09-28

This year’s haze crisis has added to the heat woes squeezing livestock farmers in Malaysia and Indonesia, as they struggle to keep their animals cool, fed and productive.

While it is difficult to separately assess the effects of haze-related smoke from heat, water shortages, feed and disease, farmers and industry groups say the cumulative strain is severely affecting animal health, productivity and their finances.

In Selama, Perak, poultry keeper Zaiful Zakaria, 42, had begun installing ventilation fans at his open-sided chicken sheds before the haze hit Malaysia in August.

The fans were meant to draw out heat accumulating inside the sheds and protect the well-being of his mainly ornamental and heritage-breed chickens. Zaiful later also began installing a water-misting system.

For a basic fan-and-mister arrangement, Zaiful estimated he had spent about 500 ringgit (US$120) per shed.

Other refinements, such as solar panels and an automatic feeder, would cost more – up to 2,000 ringgit per shed, but these could save him money and effort in the long term.

Another breeder, Mohd Hazarulhizam Mohd Shafie, shared a photo of his newly renovated shelter for his heritage chickens in Alor Setar, Kedah. He estimated the total cost to be 50,000 ringgit, including built-in exhaust fans, solar panels and auto-misters.

“The figures are not final, but the haze and the weather were definitely reasons I rebuilt my reban [coops] to better protect my birds,” Mohd Hazarulhizam said.

The haze followed weeks of hot and dry conditions across parts of the region, with fires in forest and agricultural areas in Indonesia sending smoke into parts of Malaysia. The resulting conditions have placed livestock under several stresses, ranging from heat and poor air quality to a lack of water and feed.

For farmers and veterinarians, it is difficult to determine whether a sick animal, reduced weight gain, falling egg or milk production is linked to air pollution, heat, water or feed shortages, disease – or a combination of them.

Farmers and industry groups have reported significant changes to animal health and productivity during the hot and hazy period.

Zaiful, who heads the newly established Malaysian Poultry Association, which focuses on heritage breeds and small-scale farms, said the association had received several reports of breeders losing large numbers of their flocks.

“There were no veterinary reports to establish whether it was the smoke, weather or other reasons for those deaths. Many might keep silent out of embarrassment,” Zaiful said.

Small-scale farmers, or those with less than 500 birds, and backyard farmers, he said, were particularly exposed because birds were often kept in basic, open-air sheds. Their owners had less access to veterinary and animal husbandry advice compared with commercial producers with climate-controlled sheds, backed by consultants and tighter biosecurity, he added.

Small-scale breeders are not required to register with Malaysia’s Department of Veterinary Services.

Livestock veterinarian Vijay Jayam said a similar divide existed among cattle, dairy and goat farms.

Larger dairies are generally better equipped in terms of ventilation and biosecurity, while smallholders might douse their animals with water to help them cool down.

“Farmers should prioritise ventilation and clean drinking water,” Vijay said, but added he would not attribute lower milk yields or growth to haze alone, without considering other factors like their feed, housing, disease and an animal’s growth stage.

Ari Ramash Supramaniam, a dairy and goat farmer, said milk production from the five cows out of his 23 Australian Friesian-Jersey crossbreeds had fallen by four to six litres a day. He estimated that the lost milk output had reduced his daily earnings by up to 60 ringgit.

“For a small farmer, that is a lot,” said Ari, who started his farm about six years ago. He linked the decline to the cattle grazing less because of daytime heat.

To compensate, he cuts and carries grass back to the barn. Ari also gives the cows clean water mixed with molasses and ice to help them gain energy and beat the heat. In their fodder, he also increases concentrate feed and mineral supplement.

“Once those animals are back, they will consume and finish the drinks within a few minutes,” Ari said.

The cost of supplements to ride out current conditions can be costly, as Ari uses about 5kg of molasses a day, at 1.80 ringgit per kg. The price of a 50kg bag of feed had risen between 3 ringgit and 5 ringgit during the El Nino period, he said.

He also buys eight 30kg (66 pounds) bags of fresh grass a day at 12 ringgit each to feed his animals at night, after temperatures ease. All these efforts help prevent a delay in his goats reaching a weight fit for sale, but he has to work and spend more to maintain productivity.

Ari has built his shed above ground level to allow natural airflow without having to use fans or turbines.

“Still, I have noticed saliva around the mouths of the goats and cows from the heat stress, and stressed animals will not contribute to expected results for farmers,” Ari said.

Jeffrey Ng, an adviser to the Federation of Livestock Farmers’ Associations of Malaysia, said the organisation began recording weaker production up to two months ago. Some broiler producers reported that birds took up to an additional week to reach their normal sale weight.

The heat and haze were overlapping pressures, Ng said, although he added that the federation had not received reports of increased poultry deaths associated with the current haze season.

Malaysia’s Deputy Domestic Trade and Cost of Living Minister Fuziah Salleh was reported as stating the ministry had received reports that hot weather was increasing mortality among laying hens, causing egg production to fall.

With demand unchanged, the tighter supply had contributed to higher egg prices, although she said the increase remained under control.

Across the border in West Kalimantan, Sakiman has been struggling to cope with the drought. By the end of August, he had spent two weeks finding enough grass and water for his six animals in Mempawah regency.

The cattle farmer, who keeps six heads of cattle and sells male calves and bulls for racing and meat, said normally available grass had dried out and he had to look for fodder or buy it by the bundle early in the morning.

“They are not eating as much as they used to, but it’s enough to keep them going,” Sakiman said.

Water from the Mempawah River had also turned salty and was no longer strong enough to reach the irrigation ditch he relied on, he said.

“Now I buy about two drums [of water] at a time – one for household use and another for the cattle. They last about five days, and each drum can cost about 150,000 rupiah (US$8),” Sakiman said.

Smoke particles may pose an additional risk, particularly in open-sided poultry sheds.

A modelling study by researchers at Thailand’s Chiang Mai Rajabhat University projected that three days of exposure to PM2.5 – micro-pollutants at 2.5 micrometres or less in diameter – at 150 micrograms per cubic metre could reduce average daily broiler weight gain by 5.2 per cent.

“These are predictions, not observed farm losses,” said Kulisara Marupanthorn, a veterinarian and project supervisor for the research.

In separate fieldwork being prepared for publication, her team measured PM2.5 at bird-breathing height across three 35-day broiler cycles. Birds in the higher-pollution cycle had poorer respiratory, immunity and production outcomes. The researchers could not attribute those outcomes to PM2.5 alone as there were also differences in temperature, humidity and rainfall.

Marupanthorn advised small farmers to keep their water clean and curb dust. She warned that closing curtains against smoke could backfire if it trapped heat and humidity.

Zaiful said he worried about mounting financial losses because of lower production.

“Sometimes some breeders only have 500 ringgit to start their breeding business with and if they lose their chickens to heat stress or illnesses, that is a lot of money to them,” he said.
