The mystery of one man’s fortress in Longgang

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1802 words | 2026-08-10

On the northeastern fringes of Shenzhen, standing among the low-rise tile houses of Longgang district, is a monolith that refuses to explain itself.

Erected during the late 1970s and 80s, the seven-storey building towers over the market town, a monument to something known only to its long-gone creator. Vegetation grows wild on the roof. Iron fencing and rusty cages crown its top. At night, it stands unlit; by day, its bare concrete facade looms over the narrow surrounding streets with the air of a wartime watchtower, attracting young explorers who ride Shenzhen’s Metro Line 3 to Shuanglong station, crossing the city just to see “Longgang’s Weird Building” for themselves.

A glance at the near-windowless exterior provides only hints of the man behind the Weird Building: Wen Yaoguang, a Longgang native who appears to have made his fortune in Hong Kong.

What is known of Wen’s life has been pieced together through oral accounts from neighbours, local lore passed between households and recounted to inquisitive visitors. As filmmaker Zhang Shengjia discovered while conducting research for his 2025 documentary, The Haunted Folk Fortress, Wen was, seemingly, an ordinary man driven by an extraordinary vision.

It is thought that Wen was born in Longgang and, sometime before World War II, crossed into Hong Kong, where he found work as a seafarer and later took over some farmland. He lived in Hong Kong through the Japanese occupation (1941-1945). During the city’s economic boom in the 60s, he is said to have received a sizeable land-requisition payout. After 1978, when mainland China began to open up under Deng Xiaoping, Wen returned to Longgang with his fortune and began to build.

Although it has never formally been opened to the public, the curious have found their way inside the Weird Building over the years. Their online accounts and footage – available through a virtual-reality tour – offer further understanding of its interior.

Longgang’s Weird Building is remarkably compact, with a footprint of roughly 36 square metres. Pass through the front entrance grilles and you are met with steep stairways leading both down to a dark, now-flooded basement and up to a small living area and kitchen.

Wen devised alternating-tread stairs as a space-saving solution; left and right feet land on separate, staggered steps, halving the width of each staircase. Throughout the property, staircases are adapted to their particular location; some veer off at unlikely angles, while others do not resemble conventional staircases at all.

Despite the building appearing to have few windows, much of the interior is well-lit and the upper floors offer panoramic views. What looks from the outside like solid concrete is, on one facade, in fact sheets of stainless steel punched with holes measuring roughly 5mm across.

Cast-in-place concrete furnishings are embedded into the structure of the third floor and most of the fourth floor is given over to bathtubs and water tanks, the purpose of which is unknown.

There may be as many as 20 bathtubs and eight toilets in the building, estimates Feng Guochan, executive chief architect at the Shenzhen General Institute of Architectural Design and Research, who had been allowed access by the local government to survey the building. Precise counts are difficult given the non-linear floor plans, says Feng.

The rooftop terrace, with its pavilion, is believed to have once served as a garden.

Rather than bricks or prefabricated blocks, Wen chose to pour everything – walls, floors, curved concrete benches arranged around tables – in situ.

“Choosing this approach is quite a challenge,” says Feng. Cast-in-place concrete requires a lot of formwork and carries the risk of blowouts, where wet material bursts through moulds. “And it’s very slow.”

That may explain why the building took more than a decade to complete but the permanence of concrete is perhaps the point, as the furniture is the building, fused with the structure. “When primitive people lived inside caves, they organised their lives according to the contours of the cave,” says Feng. “Instead of this being imposed by nature, [here] it occurs through one’s own thinking.”

In many ways, Longgang’s Weird Building echoes the qualities of Chinese vernacular defensive architecture, examples of which include the Kaiping diaolou of Guangdong province (fortress-like residences bearing elements of Western architectural traditions built by Chinese who returned from overseas in the 20s and 30s) and the Hakka paolou (fortified watchtowers erected on the peripheries of walled villages in Shenzhen).

Structures built by returnees “often reflect what the owner experienced abroad”, Zhang tells me. “As [this] owner was once a seafarer, the interior of the Weird Building not only reflects this experience at sea – with cabin-like spatial layouts – but continues the functional traditions of diaolou. It has massive water tanks, plots for raising chickens and growing vegetables – all built for self-sufficiency in a crisis.”

While the rest of China was opening up, Wen seemed to want to seal himself off, “like he was preparing for the end of the world”, says Feng.

In Zhang’s documentary, one local resident recalls Wen sitting under a banyan tree on a street corner, contemplating how his building could be modified: “I believe he is much like those of us who enjoy creative work – he is an artist and architect, and he possessed enough passion to persevere with the construction.”

Wen was probably also inspired by feng shui, says Zhang: “It’s rumoured that a feng shui master advised him to keep building to ward off bad luck, and there was definitely some consideration to feng shui principles indoors, too.”

Whatever the inspiration, the idiosyncratic construction has spatial complexity. Floors of differing heights flow into one another and bring to mind Austrian architect Adolf Loos’ Raumplan concept, which conceives of a building as an intertwined three-dimensional puzzle.

Feng describes the structure as resembling a Chinese puzzle lock – each space interlacing with the next, with no continuous floors. It is unlikely that Wen encountered Loos’ work, but the parallels are striking.

“I suspect he never used a floor plan to build this house, because it’s impossible to draw one,” says Feng.

As with Raumplan designs, Wen’s bland facade conceals lavish interiors. The upper floors are adorned with ceramic-tile paintings of Guilin scenery, portraits of women, scenes from traditional Chinese opera and aluminium panelling bearing images copied from the Louvre museum in Paris, France. Wen decorated an entire room with tiles inscribed with moral teachings rendered in calligraphy that were bought in Qingyuan, Guangdong. “In the traditional framework of the diaolou, Wen built a rather hedonistic interior, and you can see that from the design and the structure of the space, centred entirely around his life,” says Gu Ling, a curator who hosted a screening and panel discussion of Zhang’s documentary in February. “There were a lot of these so-called artwork or decorative pieces, and they were all rather valuable.”

Wen had his own way of doing things, adds Feng. “This person is simply someone who’s broken away from the ordinary, who’s always courageously pursuing his own self.”

Feng believes that the Weird Building was possible only because of the times in which it was constructed: after reform and opening up had begun but before a series of regulations introduced between 1982 and the 1990s placed restrictions on urban land ownership and construction.

“It’s a self-built home from a unique era, and the fact that it’s a self-built home within an urban setting makes it such a rare specimen,” he says. “There will never be another Weird Building. You need the right conditions for it to emerge, and currently, those conditions don’t exist.”

The fascination with Longgang’s Weird Building is part of a broader impulse among young Chinese, particularly in the Greater Bay Area, to seek out and document local history before it is swallowed by development and forgotten.

As people who have known eccentrics such as Wen, Zhang Suying, who built a castle out of scavenged material in Longnan city, Gansu province, and Yang San-er, who took 18 years to build a unique timber-framed house in Pingtung, Taiwan, grow elderly, oral histories are becoming increasingly hard to find. The Haunted Folk Fortress is Zhang’s attempt to decrypt Wen’s story before it is lost to time, but his quest has been challenging and his attempts to track down archival footage have proved largely futile: Hong Kong’s ATV filmed the Weird Building but the broadcaster went bankrupt and it’s not clear how its archive can be accessed; TVB also covered it but declined to share the material. Nothing of Wen’s life in the city surfaced in Hong Kong’s public records, either.

It is thought that Wen died around 2000 and, at some point after his death, his family sold the building to a developer that subdivided it into rental units. Online, Zhang found dozens of mentions by people who claim to have lived in the Weird Building or met the owner – but none responded to his requests for an interview.

One key interviewee among the local residents who would talk to Zhang was the father of his cinematographer, who used to haul sand and cement to what was then just a construction site and spoke at length about the man behind the architectural anomaly: “People all said the same thing – that he was strange. Before reform and opening up, there weren’t even buildings taller than three floors. So when he built that big house, everyone thought, ‘Wow, this guy must be really rich to build something like that.’”

Other interviewees speak of construction methods and materials, including green ceramic tiles that were imported from Hong Kong. Their recollections form the backbone of the film, the final version of which remains a work in progress.

It is pure luck that Wen’s structural legacy still stands. In 2013, when local authorities gazetted a nearby Hakka walled village as intangible cultural heritage, the Weird Building happened to fall within its radius of protection.

“Without it, the Weird Building would be gone,” says Gu.

However, “a certain level of conservation is required, since the building has a unique character; the government hopes to develop the entire area, including the Weird Building, and carry out restoration work”. Unfortunately, those plans have been put on hold due to a lack of funds amid a downturn in the mainland property market.

For Feng, who has studied the building for more than a decade, what endures is something more personal than architectural history: “I see a freedom in this old man that I kind of envy. He pursued his own spiritual world, and sensitively expressed his desires.

“People should be able to express themselves through their space.”
