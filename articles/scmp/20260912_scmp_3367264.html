Philippines bets big on rail, but can it fix a broken transport network?

SCMP | FREE | 1158 words | 2026-09-12

A two-hour commute for a 14km (8.7-mile) journey. For Julius Dalay and millions of other Filipinos, this is just how public transport works in Manila: a mishmash of jeepneys, trains and buses that commuters are left to figure out for themselves.

Dalay, an environmental planner, is resigned to the chaos. Reaching his destination usually involves hailing a jeepney, then cramming into a packed train and enduring a slow bus crawl along the capital’s main thoroughfares.

“You either wait the jeepney out, or you pray to God that the vehicles aren’t packed,” he said.

The Philippine government now says it wants to change that, with the roll-out of its biggest transport spending plan in years.

President Ferdinand Marcos Jnr’s administration is proposing to more than double the transport department’s budget to 300.96 billion pesos (US$4.8 billion) next year, as it races to push forward major railway projects meant to transform mobility across the congested capital and its surrounding provinces.

The Philippines was ranked Asia’s most congested country in the 2025 TomTom traffic index, with Manila placed 14th among the world’s 492 metropolitan areas and Davao City faring even worse, at 11th.

But transport planners and commuter advocates warn that money alone will not fix Metro Manila’s problems. The bigger challenge, they say, is making sure existing buses, jeepneys and other modes of public transport are not neglected while costly new projects – still years in the making – get built.

For Dalay, the daily commute has turned into even more of a headache since state-owned operator Philippine National Railways suspended services to make way for the North-South Commuter Railway, a 147km (91-mile) link that is slated to connect New Clark City to Calamba in Laguna province by 2029.

Services between Malabon in the northwest and Alabang in the south were paused two years ago, pushing commuters onto other rail lines and forms of transport – despite their partial resumption this June.

Dalay, who founded the advocacy group Commuters of the Philippines, called the budget increase “necessary”, but warned against putting all the government’s financial eggs in one basket at the expense of other transport systems.

He points to the EDSA Carousel, a dedicated bus rapid transit system running along the capital’s main expressway, as a case in point.

“Some projects, like the carousel, are getting zero budget – this is detrimental. We can’t neglect the existing systems for the sake of future ones, simply because people need transport now,” Dalay said.

Creating a transport ecosystem in which different forms of transport compete for funding rather than complement one another was a “major shortcoming” of the government, said Patricia Mariano, director of AltMobility PH, an advocacy group for pedestrian and commuter rights.

“Every mode serves a specific purpose and meets different mobility needs,” she said.

Rail baggage

The Marcos administration’s decision to sharply increase transport spending comes as the government faces mounting costs for major railway projects, many of which rely on a mix of internal funding and substantial foreign loans.

Rail projects in the Philippines could take years to move from planning to operation, said Manuel Jose Camagay, a mechanical engineer and transport, urban and logistics specialist.

The Metro Manila subway was one such example, he said, with the project delayed partly because the capital’s development had for decades been planned around roads and private vehicles rather than rail infrastructure.

“All developed economies, especially in Europe and Japan, prioritised rail or mass transit as the effective solution to development instead of single-car driving,” he said.

Camagay suggested that the proposed budget increase might reflect pressure to accelerate railway construction before Marcos leaves office in 2028.

Transport researcher Sandy Mae Gaspay, an associate professor at UP Diliman Institute of Civil Engineering, said financing would still be a challenge given the nature of the loans involved.

“It is the Philippines’ obligation to provide the counterpart funds for these loans,” she said, adding that railways were “disproportionately more expensive to build compared to other transport projects”.

Gaspay warned that projects such as the North-South Commuter Railway and the Metro Manila Subway could not meet the transport needs of the wider capital region on their own.

“They don’t replace the need for a genuinely networked system across Metro Manila and its surrounding regions,” she said. “That network shouldn’t be understood as rail alone. Buses and jeepneys still carry the bulk of daily trips and will continue to for the foreseeable future, so a real network has to integrate rail with these other modes.”

Camagay said commuters would eventually benefit from the railway projects, but argued that bus rapid transit systems could deliver faster gains in urban mobility in the meantime, without the need for extensive construction.

Corruption was also a concern, he added, warning that simply building infrastructure would achieve little if public works were compromised by dishonest practices.

Mariano said the government should also look beyond the capital’s transport demands to other parts of the country.

“Considering the rapid growth we’ve been seeing, investments and road space would be best allocated to rail or other mass transit … rather than roads and highways,” she said, adding there was “a strong case for mass transit in other metropolitan cities like Cebu and Davao.”

A new hope

Even with the Philippines’ chronic public transport issues, analysts welcomed the sheer scale of the projects now under way as a positive shift away from piecemeal infrastructure planning.

“What gives me optimism is that there are many ongoing initiatives that are leading to a more integrated outlook,” Gaspay said – though she added that weak coordination between government agencies and local authorities remained a major obstacle.

“I’m always hopeful that better days are coming,” said Gene Lambert Giron, an assistant professor on land-use planning at the University of the Philippines Open University. “But the big question is when?”

Gene Lambert Giron, urban planning specialist

Giron contrasted the Philippines’ progress with that of other countries in the region.

“Imagine, years ago, the economy and infrastructure of Vietnam were behind us. Look at Vietnam now,” he said. “Even Laos, a country which is not as economically prosperous as the Philippines, has long-distance railway infrastructure. Indonesia, also an archipelago, has a functioning long-distance railway infrastructure.”

A study published last year by the Philippine Institute for Development Studies found that the country’s public transport system seriously lagged behind that of its Southeast Asian neighbours.

Manila’s ambitious transport agenda would only succeed if it stayed focused on the seamless movement of both vehicles and people, Mariano.

“Our hope is for the administration to dream bigger: to pursue a mobility renaissance, with a greater focus on the needs of the nearly 90 per cent of Filipinos who rely on public and active transport in their daily lives,” she said.
