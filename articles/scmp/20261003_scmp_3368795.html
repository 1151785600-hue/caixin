Hong Kong’s Kellett School celebrates 50 years with bold 10-year plan and new Sixth Form Centre

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1572 words | 2026-10-03

Plutarch, the Greek philosopher and writer, once likened the mind to a fire to be kindled rather than a vessel to be filled. Sparking curiosity remains a key goal in education, especially for the teachers at Kellett School, one of the oldest non-profit British international schools in Hong Kong.

This year, the institution marks its 50th anniversary, having grown from two primary classes in Wan Chai into an all-through school that now operates across three campuses on Hong Kong Island and in Kowloon.

Regarded as one of the city’s top international schools, Kellett is pursuing an ambitious road map under principal and CEO Paul Tough, who took the helm in 2024. “Kellett Vision: 2035” is a 10-year, six-pronged strategy with a clear focus: equipping students for an ever-evolving world, centred on pillars such as high-quality education, inclusivity and challenging students to push the boundaries of their potential, learning in and beyond the classroom, celebrating individual and collective well-being and achievements, fostering an environment of acceptance and respect, and making a difference within the wider community and in the world.

With the upgrade of its Pok Fu Lam campus and the opening of the new Sixth Form Centre, designed to bridge the gap between secondary school and university with a mature, independent learning environment, the first year of Kellett’s second half-century is off to a solid start. Along with the Kowloon Bay campus, these three locations form the backbone of Kellett’s educational approach.

Kellett’s Sixth Form Centre is “about much more than additional space”, Tough explains. “It represents the kind of learning environment that we believe young people need as they prepare for university and the world beyond. We wanted a setting that encourages independence, collaboration and intellectual curiosity.”

Beyond the blueprint of bricks and mortar, his plans for Kellett’s next decade are articulated through a commitment to the strategic priorities. “Kellett Vision: 2035” places high-quality teaching and learning at the fore, while championing individual and collective well-being as the bedrock of the school experience.

Central to this evolution is the cultivation of a flourishing community – an ecosystem where academic achievement is bolstered by a “support for all” philosophy, anchored by the creation of dynamic learning environments that place Kellett at the vanguard of education. “Academic excellence remains central to who we are, but young people will also need qualities such as adaptability, creativity, resilience and sound judgment,” says Tough. “Success in life is about much more than examination results.”

Kellett answers this need with the introduction of bespoke, non-examined courses for students in Years 10 and 11 – a crucial period leading up to Britain’s General Certificate of Secondary Education (GCSE) exams. Focusing on life skills and designed to complement traditional academic subjects, these courses, integrating such subjects as entrepreneurship, financial literacy, service, global citizenship and sustainability, will be externally validated to ensure a richer learning experience.

As universities increasingly assess a student’s intellectual rigour and practical adaptability alongside traditional exam results, Kellett closes the distance between classroom theory and real-world application with hands-on learning opportunities.

Activities also take shape as outreach, partnerships or sharing best practices beyond the classroom, leveraging the city’s unique environment for immersive, experiential education. “Some of the best learning happens beyond school walls,” says Tough.

Roll-outs inspired by “Kellett Vision: 2035” have seen students taking part in projects such as providing English-language support in Wah Fu Estate and engaging in sustainability initiatives with a local farm cooperative and cross-border exchanges with schools in the Greater Bay Area, enabling them to contribute to the wider community. More programmes are expected over the next decade.

With input derived from conversations with staff, students, parents, governors, alumni and members of the community, Tough designed the forward-thinking strategy to take the school to new heights over its next 50 years. He sees it as a continuation of Kellett’s story rather than a departure from it.

“My role was really to listen; the vision didn’t arrive fully formed from my office,” he says. “It emerged from hundreds of conversations and a genuine desire to build something that reflects both our heritage and our future ambitions.

“Our students are thriving, our community is strong and demand remains high. At the same time, education is being reshaped by technological change and Hong Kong is positioning itself as a leading international education hub. It felt like exactly the right time to ask ourselves what role Kellett should play in that future.”

Future-proofing the education that Kellett students receive – especially at a time when change is only accelerating – is undoubtedly a priority. Over his career in education, which began in Britain and has taken him to Japan and Hong Kong, Tough has seen many changes in and out of the classroom. But the biggest impact of all has come from technology.

Our job is to help young people make the most of technology while developing the qualities that technology can’t replace

Paul Tough, principal and CEO of Kellett School

“AI will undoubtedly change education, but I actually think it makes the human side of education even more important, because the future will place a premium on qualities such as creativity, communication, collaboration, leadership and ethical judgment. Our job is to help young people make the most of technology while developing the qualities that technology can’t replace,” he says.

Tough has also observed how technology has transformed how students absorb information and learn: “With technology and social media, information is generated in smaller, more rapid chunks; the way that they truncate their learning into smaller chunks enables them to move on very quickly.” While this fast-paced digital consumption has helped students process information efficiently, reading for pleasure has lessened, says Tough. Yet, he praises the younger generation, arguing they should be given more credit: “Students today are full of questions, keen to learn about the world, ready to challenge norms and conventions, and understand why they are there in the first place, which I think is all very healthy.”

Many of us have fond memories of our favourite teachers. On his own education journey, Tough says, he was inspired by three instructors in particular: his parents, a Welsh language teacher and an economics and politics teacher; and his history teacher, who motivated him to specialise in history himself.

In the midst of his studies, Tough realised that teaching would be the next natural step for him. “My first ‘eureka!’ moment was when I was a postgraduate and tasked with teaching some modules as part of an undergraduate course. I had never stood up in front of an audience to share what I knew and help develop their knowledge. So, when I was doing that for the first time, I realised I wanted to be a teacher.”

After a couple of years teaching in Britain, Tough moved to Hong Kong in 1999. Aside from a five-year tenure in Japan, he has been here ever since.

“Arriving in Hong Kong and working at my first international school made me realise that working in that environment was how I wanted to pursue the rest of my career. An environment like Kellett provides opportunities and holistic experiences for children and makes them well-rounded individuals,” says Tough.

His time as a history teacher also instilled in him the conviction that the duties of an educator and a principal extend far beyond school hours. “It’s my duty to help students navigate different periods in their life. I have to ensure that the children have an advocate, a champion and somebody who helps them work through mistakes. It has defined how I lead schools,” he says.

Kellett is unique among international schools in Hong Kong as a parent-governed, independent and non-profit institution, allowing it to take a long-term view and focus entirely on what is best for students, he says.

As evident from “Kellett Vision: 2035”, Tough wants the school to keep pushing boundaries and stay on the cutting edge: “I often challenge myself, our staff and our board with a simple question: ‘Are we being bold enough?’ Kellett is a successful school and success can sometimes make organisations cautious. I don’t think our responsibility is simply to preserve what we have. It’s to build on it. It means being prepared to ask difficult questions, explore new possibilities and think carefully about what our students will need in the future, not just what they need today.”

Buildings matter, facilities matter and strategy matters, but culture matters most

Paul Tough

Most importantly, he has not lost sight of the humans at the heart of Kellett School. “One lesson I’ve learned throughout my career is that schools are ultimately about people. Buildings matter, facilities matter and strategy matters, but culture matters most,” he says. A strong school creates “a sense of belonging while maintaining high expectations”, and, in his experience, “meaningful change happens when people feel involved in shaping it”.

Backed by a strong community and framework, Kellett is well positioned to keep innovating and evolving. No matter what the future holds, the principles that anchored its past will guide its future. As Tough puts it, “Building a great school is about remaining true to the values that have always defined Kellett, while having the confidence and ambition to prepare our students for a very different future.”
