US midterm elections 2026: all you need to know

SCMP | FREE | 1414 words | 2026-11-03

Americans go to the polls on Tuesday, November 3, in midterm elections that could reshape the final two years of Donald Trump’s second term as president.

Control of both chambers of Congress is in play, along with 36 governorships. Polls suggest Democrats go into the vote with an advantage, but the outcome will hinge on a few dozen closely fought races.

More than 100 million people are expected to vote if turnout matches the 2022 midterms. By October 12, a record US$3.8 billion had been spent on political advertising, according to Reuters.

What are the midterms?

US presidents serve four-year terms. Midterm elections are held halfway through, every four years, in November. The president is not on the ballot.

Voters instead choose members of Congress, the national legislature that passes laws, approves federal spending and confirms the president’s top appointees, including judges and cabinet members. They also elect governors, state lawmakers and thousands of local officials.

The midterms are often treated as a referendum on the sitting president. The president’s party usually loses ground.

In 2018, during Trump’s first term, Democrats gained a net 40 seats in the House of Representatives and took control of the chamber.

What is being decided?

Congress has two chambers, and Republicans currently control both.

The House of Representatives has 435 members, all elected for two-year terms, so every seat is on the ballot. Republicans currently hold 218 seats and Democrats 214, meaning Democrats need a net gain of four seats to take control.

The Senate has 100 members serving six-year terms, with about a third elected every two years. This year, 35 seats are being contested. Republicans hold 53 seats and Democrats 47, so Democrats also need a net gain of four.

The House has two vacancies and one independent who sits with the Republicans. The Senate’s 47-member minority includes two independents who caucus with the Democrats.

If the Senate ends up split 50-50, Vice-President J.D. Vance casts the deciding vote, so Republicans would keep control.

This year’s Senate contests include two special elections in Ohio and Florida to fill seats vacated by Vance and Marco Rubio, who became secretary of state.

Voters in 36 states will also choose governors, who run state governments and oversee much of daily policy, from policing to healthcare. In 15 of those states, no incumbent is on the ballot.

Why it matters: if Democrats win either chamber, they can block Trump’s legislation and use congressional committees to investigate his administration.

Who runs the election?

Unlike Hong Kong, India, Britain or most other democracies, the US has no national body that organises or oversees its elections.

The US Constitution gives the 50 states primary authority over the “times, places and manner” of elections. Congress has some power to set national rules and has used it for landmark laws such as the 1965 Voting Rights Act. But the states and their counties do the actual work of holding elections.

The Associated Press counts more than 10,000 separate voting jurisdictions across the country, each operating under its state’s laws and, in some places, local rules too. Most are counties, where an elected official such as a county clerk typically oversees the vote with a mix of paid staff and volunteer poll workers.

That means rules differ from place to place. Some states require photo identification at polling stations, and others do not. Some mail a ballot to every registered voter, while others allow postal voting only with a valid reason. Voting machines, ballot designs and deadlines all vary.

In most states, the official who oversees elections is the secretary of state, an elected post that has nothing to do with foreign affairs.

Election security experts say the patchwork system makes large-scale fraud very difficult. Rigging a national result would require many officials in many counties, often from both parties, to break the law together and keep quiet.

The question of who controls elections has become a political flashpoint this year. In March, Trump signed an executive order directing the US Postal Service to deliver mail ballots only to voters on government-approved lists. Trump has said, without evidence, that mail voting enables fraud.

States challenged the order in court. In September, the US Supreme Court refused to let the Postal Service restrictions take effect for this election, meaning mail voting will work largely as it has in the past.

Why won’t we know the result on election night?

US television networks and news agencies such as the Associated Press “call” or project winners on election night using partial counts from local electoral authorities and statistical models. Those projections are not official results. Close races can take days to call, and certifying the official results takes weeks.

Several factors contribute to the wait.

Postal ballots take longer to process: officials first record each returned envelope and compare the voter’s signature with the one on file, a check that also guards against anyone voting twice. Only then can officials open the envelope and scan the ballot.

Some states cannot start early: about half of the states allow officials to process postal ballots days or weeks before election day, so those votes can be released soon after polls close. Others, including the swing states of Pennsylvania and Wisconsin, do not let officials open postal ballot envelopes until the morning of election day.

Ballots can arrive after polling day: in California, postal ballots count if they are postmarked by election day and arrive within seven days. In June, the US Supreme Court confirmed that states may count such late-arriving ballots.

Voters can fix mistakes: in Arizona, for example, voters whose signatures are questioned have up to five days after the election to confirm or correct them.

Some races need extra rounds: Maine and Alaska, both with competitive Senate races, use ranked-choice voting. Voters rank candidates in order of preference, and if no one wins a majority, the count continues in subsequent rounds. In Georgia, a race in which no candidate wins a majority goes to a run-off, which could push the result into December.

The order in which votes are counted can also cause dramatic swings. Republican supporters tend to vote in person, while postal ballots counted later often favour Democratic candidates. Early returns can therefore favour Republicans before the lead shifts, a pattern analysts call the “red mirage”.

Trump has seized on slow counts before, accusing Democrats, without evidence, of trying to “steal” California’s primary election in June, for example.

The final balance of power may not be clear for some time. California, which has several competitive House seats, gives counties until December 3 to certify their results. If Alaska’s Senate race is close, NPR reported, the whole country could wait weeks to learn which party controls the Senate.

What are the big issues?

The cost of living: in a recent Reuters/Ipsos poll, about one in two registered voters said rising costs would weigh most heavily on their vote. Asked which household expenses Congress should focus on, respondents most often pointed to healthcare, petrol, groceries and housing.

Tariffs: Trump’s import levies have become unusually prominent in the campaign. A conservative think tank, the National Taxpayers Union Foundation, estimated they would cost the average household US$820 this year.

Democrats blame tariffs for rising prices. Republicans have run advertisements accusing Democrats who oppose the tariffs of siding with China over American workers.

Immigration: voters have tended to trust Republicans more on border security, according to Fox News polling this year, which also found 59 per cent of voters thought Immigration and Customs Enforcement, the federal agency carrying out Trump’s deportation drive, had been too aggressive.

Redistricting: the boundaries of House constituencies are normally redrawn once a decade, after the census. But after Trump urged Texas Republicans last year to redraw their map to win more seats, both parties joined an unusual mid-decade scramble. Ten states now have new maps.

An NBC News analysis in June found the new boundaries could give Republicans up to 16 seats, against six for Democrats. Forecasters have since suggested the effect may be small. The national mood favours Democrats strongly enough to offset much of the gain, Decision Desk HQ, an election data firm, said in September.

Where the race stands: Democrats lead by about 8.7 percentage points on the “generic ballot”, a poll question asking which party voters prefer for Congress, according to the PollCheck average on October 27. A Reuters/Ipsos poll the same day put Trump’s approval rating at 32 per cent.
