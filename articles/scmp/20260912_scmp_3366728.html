Indo-Chinese cuisine in Hong Kong: a delicious dialogue of cultures and flavours

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1882 words | 2026-09-12

On September 12 and 13, world leaders and ambassadors are due to gather in New Delhi for the 18th Brics Summit, with President Xi Jinping expected to be among them – his first visit to India since 2019. It is a moment of cautious optimism in a relationship that has see-sawed between tension and thaw for the better part of a decade, marked by such events as the 2017 Doklam (Donglang) stand-off and the 2020 clashes in the Galwan valley.

But as politicians cook up deals and diplomacy, two chefs in Hong Kong would rather let the food do the talking. In July, chef Palash Mitra of Prince and the Peacock and chef Theign Phan of Grand Majestic Sichuan fired up their tandoors and woks, trading notes on the almost 2,000-year-old conversation their cuisines have been engaged in, long before anyone put it on an agenda.

Growing up in Kolkata, the capital of West Bengal state in eastern India, Mitra never thought of Chinese food as a separate cuisine. It was simply stitched into the city’s culinary fabric.

He remembers the Chinese food carts his family would visit on special occasions, and “Shubhon”, the man behind the wok, whose real name was Chen Shiobin. By day, Shubhon worked at a rifle factory; by night, he sold street bites, peddling in fluent Bengali, bargaining at the market in Bengali, laughing in Bengali.

“He was as much a part of Kolkata as the tram lines and the Durga Puja pandals. He was simply one of us,” says Mitra.

It’s easy to think of Indian and Chinese gastronomies as detached empires. But beneath the surface, a dialogue has been under way for more than a millennium. On ancient maps, the two are interwoven through the maritime lanes of the spice trade and the mountain passes of the Silk Road. It is believed that India initiated the exchange, sending black pepper to China during the first two centuries AD. Later, the favour was returned: China dispatched cassia bark and star anise along the same routes. By the time Hakka and Cantonese settlers arrived in Kolkata – then named Calcutta – more than 200 years ago, and adapted their woks to local ingredients to give birth to a composite cuisine now beloved across the subcontinent, Chinese and Indian communities had been using food as the language of negotiation for generations.

“Indo-Chinese is not a hybrid that belongs nowhere,” Mitra asserts. “It belongs specifically to Kolkata, a city that has always absorbed the world and made it its own.”

Global trade had shaped Kolkata for centuries. Portuguese merchants brought chillies, potatoes and cashews in the 1500s, and the English East India Company followed in the 1600s, chasing the spice trade. By the time the city was established, in 1690, other settlers, such as Armenians, had already brought their own kitchens and their own gods.

Kolkata’s Chinese community, concentrated in Tangra, the “new” Chinatown, peaked at 40,000 around the time of World War II. Settlers had arrived in waves, starting in the late 18th century, first making a home in Tiretta Bazaar, the “old” Chinatown, and then again throughout the 19th century and early 20th century, fleeing war and invasion. Many worked in the tanning industry, a profession that higher-caste Hindus considered “polluting”, leaving a niche that the Hakka filled. As tanneries in the Tangra neighbourhood closed or relocated over the years, the community increasingly turned to food to sustain their livelihoods.

Like many new immigrants, the Chinese adapted their cuisine, adding more chillies, more garlic and more ginger to suit Indian palates, while keeping the non-negotiables – wok, high heat and soy sauce – close to the hearth. A menu at an Indo-Chinese restaurant is what Mitra calls “gloriously Calcuttan”. While early Kolkata institutions such as Eau Chew pioneered the genre in the 1920s, the now-classic Hakka wheat noodles dish was later popularised by the grass-roots, family-run kitchens of Tangra. In 1975 came chicken Manchurian, invented at the Cricket Club of India, in Mumbai, when the Kolkata-born chef Nelson Wang paired the Indian trinity of ginger, garlic and green chilli with soy sauce and cornflour instead of garam masala. Chilli paneer – cubes of firm cheese fried with peppers and green chilli – emerged in the late 20th century as a vegetarian adaptation of familiar Indo-Chinese poultry recipes.

Mitra carried those memories with him when he arrived in Hong Kong. “It was only when I was dining at Luk Yu Tea House that I realised chilli chicken did not exist in China,” he says. “[Chicken] Manchurian was an Indian invention. Hakka noodles, as we knew them, did not belong to any province on a map [of China]. Instead, I found dim sum and Cantonese broths.”

It was the culinary equivalent of an American landing in Hong Kong and discovering fortune cookies were never Chinese to begin with. “Looking back, I laugh, because I had unknowingly grown up loving a cuisine that bore very little resemblance to the Chinese food I encountered in Hong Kong. Living on both sides of that divide has been a rare gift,” he reflects.

Phan speaks that same dialect of living between cultures – born in Malaysia, raised in Singapore, now cooking Sichuan food in Hong Kong. She recalls a childhood of tucking into roti prata at hawker centres, fish-head curries with her father and banana-leaf meals where she learned to eat with her hands.

“[Indian food] was always bold, flavourful and communal,” she says. “It was only after I started cooking professionally and travelling that I realised how incredibly diverse Indian cuisine is. What I knew growing up was just a small glimpse.”

Phan’s father is Hakka, so learning about the Kolkata community resonated with her. She had seen the same dynamic play out in Peranakan cuisine, born from Chinese-Malay intermarriage.

“Neither cuisine was created because people wanted to invent something new; they developed naturally because communities settled in a new place and began cooking with what was around them, while holding onto parts of their own identity,” she says.

At a time when a quick scroll on social media can (virtually) transport you to a Chongqing hotpot joint, it’s easy to forget the ingredients themselves have already travelled farther. While huajiao (Sichuan pepper) is native to Sichuan province, chilli peppers arrived from Mesoamerica. It took centuries of seafaring and cross-continental trade to bring them together in a single pot.

Mala, one of Sichuan’s most famous flavour profiles, is defined by the chillies that hit the tongue first, delivering a building burn that lingers, and the huajiao that numbs the tongue, a tingling that feels almost electric. It’s a dialogue between two different kinds of fire, connected through ancient routes.

But just as it would be a mistake to generalise Indian food as “curry” – a British catch-all for a whole universe of Indian cooking – it would be equally reductive to sum up Sichuan cuisine, which has 24 official flavour profiles, such as the sweet, sour, salty, savoury, spicy and numbing guaiwei (“strange flavour”) and the fish-free yuxiang (“fish-fragrant”), as just mala.

With such vast and layered traditions on both sides, how do two chefs even begin to find common ground? “Both cuisines understand the importance of balance,” says Phan. “Even when a dish is spicy, it’s never just about heat. You’re looking for aroma, depth, acidity, texture and fragrance all working together.”

In Indian cookery, this plays out in the methodical blooming of whole spices in oil before adding aromatics and garam masalas. In Sichuan fare, the same instinct runs through layers of aromatics and the slow heat of chillies and peppercorns.

Both traditions also share a deep understanding of fermentation – the funky depth of doubanjiang (fermented broad-bean paste) meets the sharp tang of Indian pickles and kasundi (fermented mustard). More importantly, they are unafraid of fire. “Whether it’s a tandoor or a blazing wok, both cuisines rely on high heat to create smokiness, caramelisation and complexity,” Mitra adds.

When the two chefs travelled through India together earlier this year, these parallels became tangible, and the trip became the foundation for two four-hands dinners at Prince and the Peacock and Grand Majestic Sichuan. In Mumbai, Phan ordered crab cooked two ways and found herself naming Sichuan flavour profiles: garlic paste and fragrant-spicy. “I could taste cardamom, peppercorns, cassia bark, coriander seeds – not just chilli, but layers of dry spices working through the sauce,” she says.

In hotel restaurants across the country, the international menus told the same story: Sichuan stir-fried vegetables, triple Sichuan fried rice (an Indo-Chinese dish with “Manchurian” gravy), kung pao prawns, mapo tofu. “It really shows India’s love of Sichuan food – the flavours have been embraced so naturally,” says Phan.

For Mitra, the trip was also a homecoming: “I probably ate Hakka noodles almost every day we were in India.”

Mitra’s cravings naturally found their way onto his four-hands menu – but with a twist. During recipe trials in Jamnagar, in Gujarat state, he was making a bowl of Hakka noodles when a leftover Goan xacuti spice blend caught his eye. He added a little without thinking and found that “the coconut rounded everything out, while the stone flower brought an earthy note that worked surprisingly well with the soy [sauce] and chilli” – and so the spice blend stayed. Both flavours are products of migration and adaptation; xacuti carries Portuguese influence into Goa, while Hakka noodles carry Chinese technique into Kolkata.

Chilli paneer was another classic discussed from the start. “We always knew we wanted chilli paneer on the menu because it’s such an iconic Indo-Chinese dish,” Mitra says. But Prince and the Peacock doesn’t have the kind of set-up that produces wok hei (“breath of the wok”), so he turned to the grill. Paneer hits the hot metal with a hiss; onion and capsicum blister and pop as the skewers turn. “It still tastes recognisably like chilli paneer, but the grill gives it a different character,” he says. It’s the same spirit that built Tangra’s kitchens: when you don’t have the right tool, you invent a new way to use the one in front of you.

At Grand Majestic Sichuan, Phan had the woks to spice things up. In Mumbai, she had a Bombay duck chilli fry and thought about how to translate its fragrant heat, sweetness and savouriness through a Sichuan lens for the collaboration, eventually landing on the flavours of kung pao. What emerged keeps the crisp-fried fish and the punchy chilli of the original, but reroutes it through the sweet-sour-nutty dialect of kung pao.

Other dishes in their four-hands collaboration, such as the chilled soup of chaas and tangerine peel, look forwards, rather than backwards. Chaas, a spiced buttermilk drink meant to cool the palate, takes on a faint bitterness and citrus fragrance from the dried tangerine peel, a combination Mitra and Phan describe as “completely new”. It is a dish that requires both chefs in the same kitchen, with no particular interest in staying inside the lines.

“Sharing a meal creates a different kind of connection,” says Mitra. “Food has always travelled more freely than politics.”
