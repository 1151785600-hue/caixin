‘Unwritten rules’ become newest target in Communist Party’s war on corruption

SCMP | FREE | 1322 words | 2026-10-30

The Communist Party of China has pledged to vigorously crack down on the “unwritten rules” that enable corruption in the party, deepening its long-running campaign to keep officials in line.

At a Friday press conference, senior officials described the important takeaways from the 20th Central Committee’s Fifth Plenum, which concluded the day before. They underscored President Xi Jinping’s assertion that corruption remains a serious challenge that requires sustained pressure and targeted action against the informal practices that undermine formal rules.

The four-day conclave concluded with the adoption of the “Decision of the CPC Central Committee on Several Major Issues Concerning Persevering with Full and Rigorous Party Self-Governance.”

In a rare move, the document singled out “unwritten rules” as a prominent problem to tackle.

Unwritten rules refer to the implicit understanding that those with power must be compensated in exchange for jobs, services, promotions, contracts, land leases or even to access social welfare or admission to schools. This can take the form of money, sex or the exchange of favours.

These rules have long permeated Chinese society and persist despite the party’s 14-year-long anti-corruption campaign. Measures to stamp out wrongdoing include regulations banning what were formerly commonplace practices, like extravagant business entertainment and gift-giving.

“For a period of time, some unwritten rules have existed to varying degrees both inside and outside the party, undermining the authority and enforcement power of party rules and discipline, which has drawn strong reactions from cadres and the public,” said Xie Chuntao, executive vice-president of the Central Party School.

“The decision emphasises starting by establishing clear rules and puts forward corresponding measures in advancing openness in party and government affairs, strengthening education and guidance, correcting related disorderly phenomena and establishing and improving institutional mechanisms,” Xie said.

Tang Fangyu, the director of the party’s Central Policy Research Office, said that the decision represented the plenum’s most important outcome and that its drafting was led by Xi.

“He personally planned the process, provided continuous guidance, set the direction, and played a decisive role,” Tang said. The work involved soliciting opinions from localities and government departments, commissioning research on key topics, collecting more than 1,000 suggested revisions and further refinements during the plenum discussions, ensuring the document reflected the collective will of the party, he said.

In August, Xi also chaired a symposium with non-party figures to solicit opinions on the draft decision. During this, he underscored the need for heightened risk awareness amid “prolonged governance, periods of peace and complex environments,” state news agency Xinhua reported on Friday.

The decision calls for maintaining high pressure so officials “dare not, cannot and do not want to” engage in corruption, while addressing the symptoms and causes of illicit behaviour.

Soon after Xi took the helm in 2012, state media rolled out a campaign calling for codified rules and laws to replace unwritten rules, warning they had twisted societal values, bred injustice and allowed corruption to permeate different sectors.

In 2014, the fourth plenum of the 18th Central Committee vowed to “resolutely eliminate all kinds of unwritten rules, never permit leniency beyond the law and never allow cases to be handled based on connections, personal relationships or money”. The party’s mouthpiece People’s Daily also published an article that year with the headline: “We must dare to declare war against unwritten rules”.

However, Xi has repeatedly stated that the problem remains a persistent threat. In an October article in Qiushi, the party’s flagship theoretical journal, the president wrote that “when formal rules are not firmly upheld, unwritten rules inevitably flourish; when unwritten rules prevail, formal rules become mere decorations”.

He said that this allowed “political brokers and fraudsters” to rely on personal connections to get ahead in the party.

To curb unwritten rules, he stressed toughening up the enforcement of formal rules, increasing transparency in party and government affairs and strictly punishing violations while holding leaders accountable.

Xi’s speech at the fifth plenum of the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection (CCDI) in January gave some examples of these unwritten rules.

“Some cadres rely on personal connections rather than the organisation, and trust relationships rather than laws and discipline,” Xi said, adding that transactions conducted through personal connections allowed “bribes to drive out legal funds” and that informal instructions written by powerful officials could supersede official government decisions.

An article published by Xinhua in June said that unwritten rules could jeopardise public interests, erode government credibility and destroy the foundation of Communist Party rule.

While the party corruption watchdog has occasionally used the specific term “unwritten rules” in its statements about corrupt officials, the details provided by the CCDI often point indirectly to the “unwritten rules” of bribery through money, sex or advantages for family members.

A recent high profile example is Ma Xingrui, formerly a Politburo member and Xinjiang Communist Party chief.

In July this year, the CCDI accused him of “improperly accepting gifts and money, helping relatives buy houses at discounted prices” and engaging in “power-for-sex and money-for-sex transactions”.

Ma also condoned efforts by his relatives to profit from his influence, “fostering rampant corruption across his family”, it said, adding he accepted huge amounts of money and property through his family members and associates.

Ma also sought “benefits for others in cadre selection and appointment, and improperly arranged jobs for others, personally and through relatives,” it added.

The CCDI said in April that Yi Huiman, the former chairman of the China Securities Regulatory Commission, also traded his power for money and sex.

In a documentary produced by the CCDI in 2017, Jin Daoming, the former anti-corruption chief of Shanxi, said that he tried to turn down bribes when he first arrived in the northern province, but he was told that he would offend others. Eventually, Jin – a 20-year veteran of the corruption watchdog – was given a life sentence for accepting 123.7 million yuan (US$18.6 million) in bribes.

Unwritten rules are so entrenched in China’s social fabric that they are not limited to high ranking officials. An article published by the CCDI in 2023 said supervision should be extended to grassroots cadres who use the little power they have to obtain bribes — in the names of “gratitude money,” “company shares, and “loans” — in exchange for contracts for village infrastructure or changing how land can be used.

Beyond party discipline, the plenum’s decision focused on improving the quality of cadres through rigorous education, selection and supervision, according to Huang Jianfa, vice-minister in charge of daily operations at the Central Organisation Department.

It stressed upholding the correct criteria for selecting officials, while demanding the party “resolutely remove those who are disloyal to the party, who say one thing and do another, and who abuse power for personal gain”, he added.

He noted that it also emphasised the need to balance strict oversight with support for officials, creating systems that both reward and protect officials who take the initiative and act responsibly.

Zhang Fuhai, a senior official with the CCDI, said at the press conference that the resolution would strengthen supervision.

This includes making party committees’ supervision more effective, enhancing supervision at the local level and bringing together various oversight bodies to work as a unified force.

The conclave also endorsed disciplinary penalties against 18 senior civilian and 16 military figures – all full or alternate members of the Central Committee – according to an official communique. The total of 34 marks a new high for recent plenary sessions.

The gathering had the lowest attendance in decades. Only 162 of the 205 full Central Committee members attended, representing a turnout of just 79 per cent. A total of 117 alternate members of the Central Committee were present at the session, compared with 147 last year.

The tough message and measures on discipline come a year ahead of the 21st national party congress, which will see major reshuffles at the top echelons of Beijing’s political and military establishment.
