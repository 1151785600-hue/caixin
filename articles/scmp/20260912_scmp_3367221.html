When Indonesia burns, these women run towards the fire

SCMP | FREE | 1084 words | 2026-09-12

Irma smells the fire before she sees it: a column of thick, yellow-tinged smoke rising above the tree line near her small wooden home.

She pulls on heavy rubber boots, checks her equipment and drives straight into the impenetrable smog emanating from wildfires devouring the peatland and ancient forests of Borneo.

“I am terrified that the flames will reach residential areas. It is extremely dangerous,” the 39-year-old mother told This Week in Asia, moments before disappearing into the murk that has settled over the Indonesian province of West Kalimantan.

Irma, who like many Indonesians only goes by one name, is one of roughly 150 Muslim women who make up Power of Mama, an all-female volunteer firefighting unit that heads out every day during the dry season to battle blazes smouldering deep beneath the island’s peatlands.

They fight fires lit, deliberately or otherwise, by the agricultural burning of poor farming communities clearing land the cheapest way they know how. This year, those fires arrived earlier and burned harder than most can remember, fuelled climate anomalies and drought conditions.

“We are the ones who feel its effects most,” said Irma, who is the group’s coordinator, explaining that it was women who were left to care for housebound children through increasingly long stretches of the year.

“The haze causes the airports and schools to close, and it makes our community ill. I thought I had to do something about it.”

Indonesia has an estimated 206,000 square km (79,500 square miles) of tropical peatland, making up about 10.8 per cent of its total land area and the most carbon-dense store of its kind anywhere on Earth.

When the peatlands burn, they release thousands of tonnes of greenhouse gases into the air, fuelling a vicious cycle that drives global warming and worsens each year’s drought.

Smog from the wildfires then drifts over Singapore, Malaysia and further afield – poisoning the air and damaging lungs as it does so.

Peatlands are normally waterlogged, but most of Borneo has not seen heavy rain since late June.

Decades of deforestation and drainage for palm oil plantations and other intensive agriculture have helped create a wildlife crisis that each year consumes vast tracts of the country, destroying the last natural habitat of some of the world’s rarest species, from orangutans and hornbills to pangolins.

From her village in Ketapang regency, Irma has witnessed the damage first-hand.

“We used to see wild boars, deer, muntjacs and even sun bears; we had hornbills, but now it seems as if they have disappeared,” she said.

Authorities say more than 202,000 hectares (780 square miles) of Indonesia burned between January and July – an area nearly twice the size of Hong Kong.

The Sipongi monitoring website of Indonesia’s Forestry Ministry had recorded 8,638 hotspots across Kalimantan by the end of August. Ketapang, where Irma lives, accounted for about one in five of those fires, placing her volunteer group right on the front line.

Hard hats and hosepipes

Wearing hard hats or motorcycle helmets over their jilbabs, the Power of Mama volunteers haul long hosepipes into singed forest and fields, their bright red uniforms the only splash of colour against the scorched earth.

Most wear thin surgical masks or cloth coverings for protection while they carry out their gruelling, unpaid work.

The small creeks and rivers that once served as natural firebreaks had been drying up since June, Irma said – forcing the volunteer firefighters to linger at the edge of forests or plantations as they try to douse the flames wherever they can.

The group was formed in 2022 by Yayasan Inisiasi Alam Rehabilitasi Indonesia (YIARI), the local affiliate of global non-profit International Animal Rescue. Translating to the Indonesian Nature Rehabilitation Initiatives Foundation, YIARI operates with a mandate that extends well beyond firefighting.

“This isn’t just about putting out fires,” YIARI founder and CEO Karmele Llano Sanchez said. “It’s about seeing these women become more confident and taking their rightful, powerful role in society.”

Members are trained to fly surveillance drones and lead community outreach as well as fight the fires, which can be very difficult to put out.

“You can’t see the flames,” Sanchez explained. “But the burning goes on, and often a light breeze is sufficient to rekindle the fire.”

Back on patrol, Suriati, 32, and Ratinah, 56, both of whom also go by a single name, pick their way along a narrow track towards the edge of the forest.

“We often fall off our motorbikes,” Ratinah said. “Particularly during the rainy season when it’s slippery and muddy.”

Fire is not the only obstacle. In a conservative, male-dominated society, the women’s authority is regularly tested by the neighbours they are trying to protect.

“Sometimes they won’t accept it,” said Suriati, a housewife who runs a small grocery stall alongside her firefighting duties. “They feel that we are being patronising. They say ‘what do housewives know’? With the older people in particular, we cannot simply forbid them … we have to treat them gently.”

When the rains finally do arrive, the volunteers will turn to advising farmers on controlled burning and nitrogen-restoring alternatives to slash-and-burn agriculture, rehabilitating degraded mangroves and teaching schoolchildren about how to steward the land their parents have been setting alight.

The night shift

For Ely Marlina, 46, the fires are personal. She joined the volunteers after the 2019 fire season, when Ketapang regency alone logged more than 1,000 hotspots and doctors reported an alarming spike in chronic breathing conditions among children.

By day, Ely teaches at a junior high school. By night, she fights fires.

It was already dark when word reached her of a recent blaze less than 3km (1.86 miles) from her home in Sungai Awan Kiri village, West Kalimantan.

“Some of the residents’ houses were about 100 metres away and the distance to the densely populated residential area was only about 200 or 300 metres,” she recalled.

At the scene, she climbed onto a fire truck to rig water pumps, hosepipes and portable tanks, then joined the other members of her team hauling the equipment towards the flames.

“The area was peatland,” the 46-year-old said. “The ground was very dangerous. My leg even slipped and sank half a metre into the peat.”

Hours later, the fire was finally extinguished – a small victory for Ely and her exhausted team, in a battle that begins again with every sunrise.

“Like it or not we have to stay on guard during the dry season,” she said. “The people depend on us.”
