How the Nepal flash flood unleashed a wave of anti-China misinformation

SCMP | FREE | 1030 words | 2026-09-01

To scientists, the cause of this week’s deadly flash flood on the border between China and Nepal is clear: a glacier high on the Nepal side of the Himalayan range collapsed, sending catastrophic amounts of water and debris downstream.

Initial uncertainty over the cause of the event fuelled a wave of misinformation on social media, some of which pinned the blame for the event on Chinese-built infrastructure in the region.

But a war of information over China’s activities in the region continued to brew online even after Beijing and Kathmandu agreed on the scientific analysis.

When news of the flood broke, social media users began to speculate whether the cause of the event was natural or the result of human activity, with suggestions that the disaster had originated from the Chinese side of the border.

Some posts claimed that the flood was caused by the collapse of a Chinese dam in Tibet, while others blamed it on mega infrastructure projects in the region.

The social media account Nepal Correspondence, which claims to be a Nepal-based platform for investigative journalism, said in a post on Wednesday that the flash flood was caused by Chinese construction in the Himalayas.

“This is not only a purposeful ecological disaster but is cruel too,” the post read.

In another post on the same day, Tunku Varadarajan, an Indian-British journalist, said: “China will be the death of everyone and everything downstream of it. Literally, and metaphorically.”

In a preliminary assessment on Wednesday, Nepal’s National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority (NDRRMA) said the cause was a sudden release from a Tibetan river that had been blocked by an avalanche, Nepali news portal Republica reported.

Another report by the Kathmandu Post on Thursday, citing a technical report from the Centre of Hydrology and Water Resources Research, also said a flood had entered Nepal’s Bhotekoshi River from the Tibetan side.

However, satellite analysis later pointed authorities in Nepal and China – and further abroad – to a glacier collapse in Nepal, rather than Chinese infrastructure, as the cause.

The analysis indicated that the glacier collapse occurred on the Nepalese side of the mountains, near the Langtang Lirung peak.

Daniel Shugar, an earth scientist and associate professor at the University of Calgary, told The Guardian on Thursday that initial analysis showed a large chunk of ice at 5,200 metres (17,060 feet) above sea level had collapsed vertically, falling around 1.2km (0.74 miles) into the valley floor.

“This ice and rock then flowed into a river, turning into the devastating debris flow and flash flood that we see in the CCTV footage at the border,” said Bethan Davies, the chair of glaciology at Newcastle University, told the Science Media Centre on Thursday.

That same day, China’s Ministry of Natural Resources confirmed that the deadly mudslide was triggered by a high-altitude glacier collapse in Nepal.

Some initially believed that the ice-rock avalanche had been caused by an earthquake, as the impact of the avalanche was so strong that it registered on seismographs as a magnitude 5.2 tremor.

Brahma Chellaney, an Indian geostrategist and columnist, referred to the earthquake claims in a social media post on Wednesday, saying that a moderate seismic event could trigger a cascading disaster.

He also linked the dangers to Chinese construction.

“This will renew global attention on China’s folly in persisting with the construction of the world’s largest dam in the middle of the Himalayan seismic zone,” he said, adding that its plan to build the dam on the Yarlung Tsangpo River in Tibet was a “water bomb in the making”.

In another post on Thursday, Chellaney wrote: “It is now clear that the Nepal disaster was caused by a natural trigger … but amplified by Chinese hydrological modifications and infrastructure footprint along the Tibetan side of the border.

“In other words, China’s upstream actions did not cause the glacier failure but contributed to the disaster’s severity and cascading impacts downstream.”

Scientists are still not sure exactly what triggered the initial glacier collapse, but catastrophic events like this have happened elsewhere in the region, including a glacier collapse and avalanche in India’s Chamoli district in 2021.

Shugar told Associated Press on Friday that clearer satellite images indicated the bedrock underneath the glacier might have also collapsed, which could explain the glacier collapse.

But that has not prevented a blame game over early warnings.

Republica quoted NDRRMA spokeswoman Shanti Mahat as saying the authority only learned about the disaster after the flood had reportedly already swept through Gyirong Port.

She said that a lack of timely information on the Tibetan side made it impossible to issue an early warning in Nepal.

Parshuram Kaphle, a Nepali journalist, echoed the sentiment in a social media post on Wednesday, saying China had “significantly undermined us”.

“By failing to provide early warning of such a massive disaster, we suffered immense loss of human life,” Kaphle wrote.

Ouyang Chaojun, a researcher at the Chinese Academy of Sciences, told state broadcaster CCTV that the debris flow was moving at about 40-50 metres per second (131-164 feet per second).

“Furthermore, because the debris flow reached the Gyirong border crossing in a very short time – only about 7 minutes – there was no time for evacuation,” he added.

Hydrologist Hannah Cloke, a professor at the University of Reading, told the Science Media Centre that high up in the valley near the source of the flood, early warnings were “exceptionally difficult, no matter how advanced the technology”.

The centre quoted a number of scientists as saying that while it was not known whether climate change was behind Wednesday’s collapse, warming was exacerbating the risk of catastrophic mountain failures and flooding due to melting glaciers, thawing permafrost, and increased meltwater run-off.

“A warming environment can make these kinds of events more common or likely,” Davies said.

Ouyang said that following the mudslide, some of the other slopes in the region had become unstable, making monitoring the region essential.

Concerns have been raised over a newly formed barrier lake upstream from Gyirong county that was formed by the landslide, which CCTV said on Friday was holding the equivalent of 1,000 Olympic swimming pools of water.
