Philippine flood-control scandal resurfaces after latest monsoon deluge

SCMP | FREE | 1138 words | 2026-08-22

The latest floods to hit Metro Manila and parts of the Philippines’ main island of Luzon have turned a familiar seasonal disaster into a renewed test of political accountability for President Ferdinand Marcos Jnr, as anger over allegedly corrupt flood-control projects returns to the centre of public debate.

Analysts said the deluge had sharpened scrutiny of the slow-moving investigation into allegedly substandard and non-existent flood-control works, exposing the gap between Marcos’ anti-corruption message and the lack of visible results in communities that continue to bear the brunt of extreme weather.

Metro Manila was inundated on Monday with 201.7mm of rain in just five hours, leaving residents wading through floodwaters, vehicles stranded and some areas not usually considered flood-prone submerged.

Outside the capital, villages across parts of Luzon were also swamped after weeks of southwest monsoon rains, forcing thousands of families from their homes and reviving a familiar question: why does the country keep flooding despite huge sums spent to prevent it?

That frustration was reflected by Senator Panfilo Lacson, who questioned why communities continued to be submerged year after year, despite nearly 2 trillion pesos (US$32.4 billion) being poured into flood-control projects since 2011.

“1.939 trillion pesos funded the DPWH [Department of Public Works and Highways] flood control management programme under three presidents from 2011 to 2025, and the result is uncontrolled floods,” he said in a statement on Tuesday.

In the town of Macabebe in Pampanga, a store owner barricaded the front of his shop with sacks of rice to stop water from seeping in, an image some local media outlets and content creators portrayed as a sign of creativity and resilience.

But other Filipinos pushed back online, with several posts rejecting the familiar celebration of resilience gaining traction.

“Can we pressure our government to come up with better solutions instead of romanticising Filipino resilience?” one widely shared post said. “Our fellowmen shouldn’t be suffering through this in the first place … as if we don’t pay taxes and we don’t have billions of funds for flood control projects.”

Another post, which drew more than 7,000 reactions, captured the frustration more bluntly: “Rain is natural. Heavy flooding isn’t. Until now, no one has been held accountable for the billions’ worth of corruption in flood control projects. Until now, the government remains reliant on aid.”

Scandal back in focus

The latest floods have returned attention to the country’s investigation into allegedly corrupt flood-control projects, while intensifying criticism of the government’s failure to protect communities from a disaster that returns with each rainy season.

During his state of the nation address in late July, Marcos announced that errant high-ranking officials of the DPWH, contractors and lawmakers had been detained and charged over their involvement in the scheme, a year after investigations began.

In his address the previous year, Marcos said 545 billion pesos had been spent on flood-control works since 2022 across nearly 10,000 projects, but most had been found to be substandard or “ghost” projects, implicating a number of lawmakers, government officials and contractors who allegedly colluded in the scheme.

Last month, the Department of Justice said 841 million pesos had been recovered from those implicated in the scandal and deposited in the national treasury.

As of late July, 45 individuals and two contracting firms had been charged in 26 flood control-related cases in court.

But observers echoed a public sentiment that has grown louder with each flood: progress in holding people accountable and getting answers from the scandal has been too slow relative to its scale.

“More than a year since the issue was raised, there were only a few people who were charged in courts. At this stage, most of the alleged perpetrators should have been facing the judicial process,” said Nathaniel Candelaria, an assistant professor of political science at the University of the Philippines Diliman.

Arjan Aguirre, an assistant professor in Ateneo de Manila University’s political science department, said the investigations had moved too slowly given the scale of the allegations and the amount of public money involved.

“At this point, we should already be seeing clearer findings, stronger accountability and concrete action against those responsible,” he said.

Aguirre said the slow pace of the investigations raised concerns they might not be fully independent or could become politicised, especially if people felt they were being used to target political opponents or pressure lawmakers.

“Ultimately, the credibility of the probe will depend on whether it can demonstrate that accountability is being pursued consistently, regardless of political affiliation,” he added.

Political risk

Observers said the sluggish pace of inquiry would only sow further distrust in the Marcos administration, as the president grappled with modest ratings.

Marcos’ trust rating dropped to 34 per cent in June from 35 per cent in March, while his rival, Vice-President Sara Duterte-Carpio, polled at 57 per cent, according to local pollster Social Weather Stations.

“This year’s flooding could further deepen public anger and distrust towards the government,” Aguirre said.

Every time communities were submerged, Aguirre said people were reminded not only of the billions supposedly spent on flood control, but also that the same vulnerabilities persisted.

Despite the Marcos administration touting its accomplishments in the anti-corruption investigation and Marcos himself promoting a stronger anti-corruption message, analysts said actual shortcomings would continue to overshadow any progress.

“While some people were already facing cases, I do not think the administration is successful in this issue … the people need to know the truth about the flood control scandal and those who were involved, regardless of political affiliations, must face justice,” Candelaria said.

Aguirre said the Marcos administration appeared to be putting considerable effort into improving its image and promoting an anti-corruption message, but this had not been matched by the same urgency in addressing the flood-control scandal and institutionalising reforms.

Marcos risked having his anti-corruption agenda viewed as “political messaging rather than meaningful reform” if people continued to experience flooding while seeing little concrete progress in the scandal, Aguirre said.

With flooding still a constant threat for many Filipinos, the issue looks set to haunt the administration right up to the 2028 national elections, when Duterte-Carpio has indicated she will run for the presidency.

“The Duterte camp has been using the flood control scandal against Marcos from the beginning and I expect them to continue doing so in the 2028 campaign. They have been effective in framing the issue as evidence that Marcos is politicising the investigation and protecting his allies,” Aguirre said.

“The delays we are seeing now only give greater credence to that narrative. If the government fails to produce clear findings, prosecutions, and reforms, the Duterte camp can easily turn the scandal into a broader argument that the Marcos administration talks about anti-corruption but is unwilling to hold its own allies accountable.”
