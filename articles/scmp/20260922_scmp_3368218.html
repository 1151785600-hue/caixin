Easy access to family guns fuels Philippine school shootings

SCMP | FREE | 1172 words | 2026-09-22

The Philippines has suffered three deadly school shootings since June.

In each case, at least one weapon belonged to a relative working in government or law enforcement, whether as an official service issue or a privately licensed firearm.

On Friday, a student at Banga National High School in South Cotabato province shot dead two classmates, wounded several others and then killed himself. He reportedly used the licensed firearm of his father, who works in the Education Department’s risk reduction unit.

Last month, a student at the privately run Ateneo de Zamboanga University’s junior high school live-streamed the attack, killing a fellow student before turning the gun on himself. The service-issued firearm recovered from the scene belonged to the student’s father, a customs official.

And in June, two teenagers aged 14 and 15 killed three students at San Jose National High School in Tacloban, Leyte province, injuring many others. One of the firearms used in the attack belonged to a suspect’s aunt, who works in the police.

The problem of children gaining access to these guns pointed to a “very serious failure of responsibility inside the home” and within the institutions issuing the weapons, said Raihan A. Yusoph, a conflict resolution researcher and assistant professor of history at Mindanao State University.

Yusoph said the string of recent incidents reflected an “apparent lack of institutional learning”, arguing that the Tacloban shooting should have triggered an immediate cross-agency review of service firearms kept at home.

Friday’s attack “showed that the risk also extends to privately licensed firearms in the homes of public officials”, he said, adding that authorities had been treating “each tragedy as an isolated incident instead of recognising a recurring access problem”.

He called for stricter compliance measures to be undertaken such as regular firearm inventories, documented authorisation for take-home possession and independent, public reviews carried out whenever a service weapon was lost.

“Otherwise, attention will fade, individual personnel may be blamed, and the institutional weaknesses that allowed the firearm to escape official custody will remain unchanged,” Yusoph said.

Violent backdrop

Permissive gun laws and a long history of internal armed conflicts against groups spanning communist rebels to Muslim separatists provided the backdrop to the recent violence, said Georgi Engelbrecht, a senior analyst for the International Crisis Group.

He cited his own work in the southern Philippine region of Bangsamoro, which found that firearm availability had fuelled clan-related violence that sometimes spilled over into wider society.

Philippine society had become further desensitised to violent imagery under former president Rodrigo Duterte, whose war on drugs, anti-communist campaigns and siege of Marawi in 2017 meant “blood and violence were often on television screens and sometimes not considered as an exception any more”, Engelbrecht said.

He added that post-conflict trauma was widespread in many parts of the southern Philippines, where schools and rural communities often struggled to provide mental health support.

“Teachers and school workers may not always be well equipped to address these needs,” Engelbrecht said. “Sometimes also there are not simply enough people.”

Yusoph, who has studied firearm proliferation in the southern Philippines, said gun culture there had grown out of a long history of armed resistance, clan conflict and inconsistent state protection – not any inherent local disposition towards violence.

“Firearms do not create every conflict. They change what a conflict can become,” Yusoph said.

“A grievance, psychological crisis, experience of bullying or violent fantasy becomes far more dangerous when a weapon is within reach.”

Officials have also examined the shooters’ online activity for evidence of radicalisation.

Authorities launched investigations into platforms including Roblox after the Tacloban attack, while the suspect in Friday’s shooting reportedly engaged with “true crime” subcultures and was a heavy user of the so-called dark web, Interior and Local Government Secretary Jonvic Remulla said on Saturday.

There was a degree of “mimetic violence” in such attacks, said security analyst Munira Mustaffa, executive director of Chasseur Group and a senior fellow at Verve Research who has researched online extremism in Southeast Asia.

She cited premeditation and a desire for infamy as factors linking the shootings, as well as the suspects borrowing and co-opting elements from online subcultures.

Yusoph agreed, noting that some attackers would “adopt political or religious language to give a larger meaning to personal grievances”, while the motivation of others could be “invisible to investigators” until after the fact.

“Separate attacks can still emerge from a common environment that normalises violence, rewards notoriety, circulates extremist narratives and makes weapons accessible,” he said, adding that the real crisis was the authorities’ “repeated failure to identify escalating threats, control access to weapons, intervene before grievances become violent and build public confidence in institutions”.

Audits and intervention

Much more needs to be done, according to Yusoph, who is calling for an immediate audit of service firearms kept in private residences by police and other armed government personnel.

He also wants to see centralised national databases tracking school violence and tracing firearm sales, alongside school-based threat assessment teams combining educators, mental health professionals, social workers and law enforcement.

“There is no single profile of a school shooter,” he said, urging confidential reporting channels and trained staff capable of evaluating warning signs such as abrupt changes in behaviour, bullying, suicidal ideation and a fixation on weapons or violent online communities.

“At present, policy appears to be developing from one tragedy to the next,” Yusoph said. “A centralised firearm tracing and school violence monitoring system would allow authorities to identify recurring patterns.”

We should not wait for more children to die before treating the pattern as a national warning

Raihan A. Yusoph, conflict resolution researcher

Engelbrecht agreed that managing access to guns was only part of the solution. Rural areas such as those in Bangsamoro needed more community-oriented law enforcement and forums where psychologists could engage more directly with parents and children, he said.

This would help investigators “grasp the deeper answers” that were driving the violence, he added, “beyond only superficial culprits such as games and cults”.

Yusoph said the spate of recent shootings was “already enough to show repeated system failure and a possible process of imitation or diffusion”.

“The firearm did not create the anger, humiliation, isolation, bullying, psychological distress or exposure to violent online material that may have preceded these attacks,” he said. “But firearms gave those conditions immediate lethal capacity.”

Gun availability in the Philippines and Thailand, where a 14-year-old killed seven people and himself in a school shooting last month, certainly increased “the feasibility of the attack”, Munira said.

But she agreed with other analysts that “we need to understand first what’s driving these kids, what is the source of grievance and why it’s manifesting this way”.

“This didn’t use to be a problem 10 to 15 years ago, and now it’s coming in clusters,” Munira said. “Why is it a problem now?”

“We should not wait for more children to die before treating the pattern as a national warning,” Yusoph added.
