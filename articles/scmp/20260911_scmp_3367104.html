What’s a Northern Metropolis-like project doing in Malaysia? Lessons for Hong Kong?

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1971 words | 2026-09-11

Deep in the southern border zone of Johor, Malaysia, separated from Singapore by a narrow strait, a steady flow of commuters moves seamlessly between the two sovereign neighbours.

Beyond the immediate checkpoints, a short 30-minute train ride connects visitors to regional hubs such as Kulai, where a vastly different dynamic comes into view – one defined by the rapid expansion of digital infrastructure.

Inside YTL’s sprawling Green Data Centre Park, massive operational blocks stand beside numerous others under construction. The park supplies high-performance computing power and artificial intelligence (AI) chips to global technology giants, sitting squarely within the Johor-Singapore Special Economic Zone (JS-SEZ), a landmark 350,000-hectare (865,000-acre) project officially established in January 2025.

Covering an area more than four times the size of Singapore, the JS-SEZ aims to generate 260 billion Malaysian ringgit (US$63.9 billion) for Johor’s regional economy by 2030 and create more than 20,000 high-skilled jobs, anchored by the coming 4km Rapid Transit System (RTS) Link designed to slash travel times and cement cross-strait integration.

For Malaysia, the early trajectory of Hong Kong and Shenzhen remains the foundational blueprint.

Lee Ting Han, Johor’s state executive councillor for investment and trade, told the South China Morning Post that despite distinct political frameworks, the wealth gap between Singapore and Johor mirrored the historic economic disparity between Hong Kong and Shenzhen.

“Similarly, it is pretty much like the early days of Hong Kong and Shenzhen, where the economic disparity between the two regions was so wide … what we are trying to do now is see whether there’s a way for us from a business standpoint to make it more integrated from trade relationships to investment culture,” Lee said.

He acknowledged that building the JS-SEZ presented unique regulatory challenges because Johor and Singapore operated as sovereign nations without an overarching central authority such as Beijing to coordinate rules.

However, Johor is studying how Shenzhen strategically transitioned over decades from labour-intensive assembly to capital- and technology-intensive hubs by leveraging Hong Kong capital to build home-grown tech giants in the same vein as Huawei Technologies and DJI.

As Asia’s premier financial and technology centres construct massive border-spanning growth corridors, Hong Kong’s Northern Metropolis and Malaysia’s JS-SEZ offer a compelling case study in mutual learning.

While Malaysia looks to emulate Hong Kong’s long-standing success in frictionless border flows and joint state-directed planning, the city stands to learn key lessons from Johor’s aggressive strategy in nurturing talent pipelines and deploying state-backed infrastructure to secure a low-cost, high-reliability energy grid for power-hungry tech industries.

Key to Johor’s rapid rise is its approach to energy, an area where Hong Kong could draw valuable operational insights. Highlighting the power requirements of data centres, Lee noted that Malaysia’s utilities were largely state-backed.

This allows the government to take a long-term, strategic view by investing heavily in power infrastructure upfront, insulating critical digital hubs from the short-term profit pressures faced by purely private, commercial utility entities.

Malaysia adopted a forward-looking approach by making heavy infrastructure investments ahead of the cycle.

Chew Chong-sin, a former Mengkibol state assemblyman who has analysed the region’s economic integration, noted that by combining Singapore’s global financial and connectivity hub status with Johor’s land, energy, and industrial capacity, the zone aimed to mirror Shenzhen’s early evolution into a technology powerhouse.

However, he cautioned that significant operational hurdles persisted – most notably easing severe bottlenecking at the world’s second-busiest land checkpoint and resolving head-on competition in ports and logistics – even as the coming RTS Link aimed to streamline cross-border mobility by 2027.

Crucially, the zone was positioning itself to absorb global supply chain diversification, offering a neutral, disaster-free base for multinationals and mainland Chinese enterprises looking to de-risk, Chew said.

He added that this momentum had already drawn increased interest from Hong Kong firms, including sustainable aviation fuel producers and hospitality enterprises.

As Malaysia and Singapore finalise a comprehensive 10-year master plan, he said that the zone’s ultimate success would depend on maintaining the strict execution discipline needed to translate high-level blueprints into lasting economic integration.

To spur investment ahead of its full master plan launch in late 2026, authorities introduced sweeping tax incentives starting January 2025.

Firms deploying at least 500 million ringgit in high-growth fields such as AI, quantum computing and aerospace enjoy a 5 per cent preferential corporate tax rate for years, paired with stamp duty waivers.

These incentives have caught international attention.

A representative from a Mexico-based construction and information technology enterprise, who did not wish to be named, confirmed to the SCMP that the company would visit Malaysia to explore data centre sites, citing the country’s strong connectivity within Asean, widespread English proficiency and attractive tax environment.

Following stops in Kuala Lumpur and coming visits to Johor, he commended Malaysia’s proactive tech push.

Turning to Hong Kong’s Northern Metropolis, however, the representative observed that while the city retained distinct institutional advantages, its position within China imposed inherent geopolitical limitations when manufacturing high-end, sensitive semiconductor chips.

The Northern Metropolis has been touted repeatedly as a top priority for Hong Kong’s administration, capped most recently by a visit from Xia Baolong, director of the Hong Kong and Macau Affairs Office, who underscored Beijing’s call to accelerate construction.

The megaproject aims to transform 30,000 hectares into a major housing hub and an economic growth engine.

Both the Northern Metropolis and the JS-SEZ are strategically positioning themselves as multifaceted growth engines that integrate international education hubs, hi-tech innovation corridors and vibrant ecotourism circles.

Joining the call for deeper integration, Kathy Lee, head of research and retail consultancy at Colliers Hong Kong, said the city could take reference from Johor as a benchmark for cross-border development by aligning its land-grant policies with Shenzhen and leveraging universities to spearhead innovation.

She noted that Johor’s experience showed how robust research and technology ecosystems relied heavily on close synergies with academic institutions.

To build a sustainable tech ecosystem, Lee urged authorities to synchronise housing and talent policies with project completion timelines, anticipating a resurgence in demand as new infrastructure came online.

Given the intensive energy requirements of advanced technology and data centres, she recommended that the government initiate joint feasibility studies with mainland Chinese authorities on cross-border electricity supply to secure a reliable, green power source for the city’s tech sector.

Echoing the push for seamless integration, former lawmaker Edward Lau Kwok-fan argued that Hong Kong should draw inspiration from the JS-SEZ by speeding up the implementation of bespoke legislation for the Northern Metropolis to unlock the fluid movement of people, goods, capital and data.

He also called on higher education institutions to take greater initiative.

“I feel universities have not done enough yet. In the future, universities could evolve towards an industry-oriented development path,” said Lau, a member of the Advisory Committee on the Northern Metropolis.

“Universities need to reflect. There is a need to rethink course offerings and review academic programmes going forward.”

In response, Hong Kong’s Development Bureau said that the Northern Metropolis operated as a strategic cross-boundary hub under the “one country, two systems” governing principle, utilising coming Hetao-specific legislation to facilitate fluid cross-border resource sharing within the Greater Bay Area.

The bureau highlighted that by pairing the city’s five global top-100 universities and a planned 1,000-hectare university district with Shenzhen’s industrial scale, Hong Kong provided a platform, backed by common law, capable of rapidly converting lab discoveries into mass production.

The Education Bureau also said in a reply that it was encouraging University Grants Committee-funded universities to revamp their academic offerings, backed by a fund launched in 2023 to drive digital teaching reforms.

The Chinese University of Hong Kong said in a reply that it offered AI degree programmes across its engineering and science faculties, which delivered structured training to foster AI talent equipped for real-world innovation and academic research.

The SCMP also reached out to the University of Hong Kong and Hong Kong University of Science and Technology.

Under the 2025-28 triennial planning cycle, local universities were introducing 27 new undergraduate degree programmes tailored to STEAM fields, the bureau said.

STEAM refers to an educational approach that combines science, technology, engineering, the arts and maths.

Addressing Hong Kong’s internal bottlenecks, lawmaker Aaron Bok Kwok-ming, a former president of the Hong Kong Institution of Engineers (HKIE), stressed that the city had to aggressively tackle its ongoing engineering talent shortage similarly to Malaysia.

To supply future high-value talent for the JS-SEZ, the Johor Talent Development Council acts as a centralised talent engine by partnering with universities, institutions and agencies to deliver industry-aligned upskilling, matching programmes and specialised training.

Bok called for targeted manpower forecasts, particularly in electronic engineering, to pinpoint exact deficit areas as Hong Kong advanced its innovation ambitions.

He urged government leaders to conduct official, comprehensive surveys rather than relying on industry estimates, while establishing structured pathways to entice overseas Hong Kong engineers back home and expanding domestic university training.

According to a manpower forecast report by Hong Kong’s Construction Industry Council released in 2023, the sector is expected to face a shortage of about 6,000 to 6,500 professionals by 2027, with civil, mechanical, structural and environmental engineering listed among the most severely affected specialisms.

Drawing on the forecast report, the HKIE noted in 2023 that the city would face a shortfall of about 3,000 professional engineers over the five years through 2027, stressing that engineers played an indispensable role in supporting the government’s push for new industrialisation and biotechnology.

This deficit is already affecting local businesses. Some tech companies previously said that while they struggled to find veteran talent, such as experienced electronics engineers, locally in Hong Kong, they could easily recruit skilled professionals on the mainland.

Addressing these industry concerns over projected workforce shortages, including the estimated shortfall of 3,000 engineers, engineer and former technology minister Alfred Sit Wing-hang said that emerging technologies such as AI, robotics and building information modelling required more sophisticated technical expertise rather than diluted entry criteria.

Sit added that as Hong Kong’s economic landscape underwent a structural shift towards advanced manufacturing and technology-driven industries, local universities and professional bodies were actively overhauling their training frameworks.

AI had increasingly become a core component of undergraduate engineering curricula, while new certification programmes were being developed to upskill mid-career professionals in fields such as smart industrial engineering and automation, he said.

On the energy front, lawmaker and energy sector expert Chan Siu-hung noted that Hong Kong already possessed top-tier infrastructure for data centres and computing hubs, but suggested the government could accelerate the roll-out of power infrastructure blueprints to match coming industrial investments.

On the city’s digital expansion, he noted that major development zones such as the Lok Ma Chau Loop and the San Tin Technopole were well positioned, with strong power supply nodes.

Responding to queries on energy security, the Environment and Ecology Bureau said that the government had signed Scheme of Control Agreements (SCAs) with the city’s two power companies.

Under these agreements, the suppliers are obliged to support the development of Hong Kong including the Northern Metropolis, by providing, operating and maintaining sufficient electricity-related facilities to meet the needs of various industries.

Chan stressed that actual energy demand would ultimately depend on incoming corporate investments.

“To ensure long-term energy security, Hong Kong’s two power suppliers need to finalise power agreements with mainland supply, securing cross-boundary supply options while advancing low-carbon energy goals,” he said.

“Officials should provide clearer and earlier timelines for these infrastructure plans to give operators certainty.”
