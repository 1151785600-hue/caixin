China’s military goal is to stand neck and neck with the US: ex-PLA colonel Zhou Bo

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 3910 words | 2026-08-08

Zhou Bo is a retired senior colonel in the People’s Liberation Army, a senior fellow at Tsinghua University’s Centre for International Security and Strategy, and the author of the book Should the World Fear China? This interview first appeared in SCMP Plus. For other interviews in the Open Questions series, click here.

The People’s Liberation Army (PLA) is approaching its 2027 centenary, a major benchmark set by Beijing to achieve military modernisation. What is that goal actually about?

China has set two major military goals: one [is for the] centenary of the PLA in 2027 and another [is for the centenary] of the People’s Republic of China in 2049.

Between those two milestones, China aims to basically achieve the modernisation of its national defence and armed forces by 2035. That means the PLA would not yet be fully modernised by 2027 by China’s own high standard.

The stated highest objective is to build a world-class military by 2049. However, we first need to consider how many countries are considered to have world-class militaries. The Chinese government has not specified this.

If there is only one, then it is obviously the United States. But if there can be more than one, then is China not already the second-strongest military power in the world?

That naturally leads to a comparison with Russia. I do not want to belittle Russia, but it clearly did not perform well at the beginning of the Ukraine war. It lacked effective centralised battlefield command, encrypted communications and adequate logistical support. The conflict has since become a war of attrition.

What about the PLA? Its budget is roughly one quarter that of the United States, but about 1.8 times Russia’s. It has the world’s largest armed forces and the largest number of naval vessels. Its state-of-the-art fighter jets outnumber Russia’s by a big margin. China and the United States are also the two dominant powers in applying advanced military technologies.

So, it depends on how we define a world-class military. If there can be more than one, China is arguably already among them. If there can be only one, then the 2049 objective is clear: to stand neck and neck with the United States.

Would it even be possible for China to surpass the United States? Well, it is difficult to verify whether China will actually overtake the United States. The United States has fought many wars, while China has continued to develop peacefully, and I hope that will continue.

Are there any visible indicators of the PLA’s progress towards its 2027 goal, including developments in areas such as its aircraft carrier fleet and defence budget?

There are two sides to this question.

Some developments are very visible. For example, outside observers can easily determine whether China has commissioned another aircraft carrier or whether a carrier is equipped with the so-called electromagnetic launch system.

However, other factors are much more difficult to assess.

For example, we do not know how effectively soldiers would perform in actual combat. Factors such as combat effectiveness, morale and the ability of personnel to operate under wartime conditions cannot be easily assessed unless a war actually occurs.

What China can do, in my view, is to maintain a clear long-term objective, ever sophisticated training and sustain its investment in defence. By sustained investment, I mean steady investment rather than a drastic or sudden increase.

What is valuable about China’s approach to defence spending is that, from the late 1970s, Deng Xiaoping made it clear that military development had to remain consistent with China’s economic development.

You mentioned that some capabilities cannot be assessed in a peacetime context. Does the PLA face challenges in validating systems such as AI without the combat experience the United States has gained from recent conflicts?

The United States has the advantage of being able to verify its systems in ongoing wars.

China therefore faces a dilemma because it has been at peace and consequently has had no opportunity to verify its weapons in actual warfare.

At the same time, we should not forget that peace itself has been the greatest dividend of China’s peaceful rise. It has provided China with decades of a peaceful environment, allowing the country as a whole to rise to its present level.

Sometimes you want to know how sharp your sword is. If you do not use the sword, how can you know how sharp it is? But you cannot simply cut off someone’s head to find out. This is the dilemma facing China.

Nevertheless, even with this dilemma, people should not draw the mistaken conclusion that the People’s Liberation Army cannot fight.

This is something that is often discussed in the West: the PLA may be unable to fight because China has been at peace. I think this question can be clarified quite easily.

For example, among China’s various missile launches, whether submarine-launched or land-based launches into the South Pacific, has any one of them failed? No, they have not failed. The combat capability of Chinese air power has also been demonstrated by Pakistan in its aerial combat with India using Chinese fighters.

People shouldn’t have doubts about China’s military capabilities. They are supported by a defence industry that is second to none, as well as by ambition, careful planning, and stable and sustained investment. I think the goal is totally achievable.

Do you think the anti-corruption efforts in the PLA will ultimately strengthen or weaken the PLA’s capabilities?

The Chinese government’s approach towards corruption is essentially one of zero tolerance. That is why cases of this kind are exposed from time to time.

We do not live in a vacuum, and the PLA is likewise not immune from the wider environment. It is regrettable that such matters can occur within the military.

However, the exposure of corruption and the decision to bring those responsible to justice can also be understood in another way: they demonstrate that we are trying to cleanse the organisation.

As a result, the PLA could become more effective. Developments have also demonstrated that it has not necessarily been weakened by these cases. As I mentioned earlier, we have seen Chinese missile launches in different locations, as well as the effective use of Chinese weapons systems by the Pakistan Air Force, among other examples.

I therefore believe that these anti-corruption efforts are healthy for the PLA.

Some assessments have linked the PLA’s 2027 centenary goal to Taiwan. How credible is this view, and has the risk of conflict in the Taiwan Strait declined since Chinese leader Xi Jinping and US President Donald Trump met in May?

I think the Chinese government still sincerely hopes for peaceful reunification.

This has been demonstrated not only in remarks made by the Chinese leadership, but also, for example, during the visit of the chairwoman of [Taiwan’s main opposition party] the Kuomintang to Beijing. The visit involved not only warm handshakes, but also 10 preferential measures. These measures were, of course, intended for the benefit of the Taiwanese people, and they must have been carefully prepared. This means that they were not simply ideas developed overnight.

People always mention the “stick” element of mainland China without discussing the “carrot” element of its policy towards the Taiwanese people.

The reality is that we want normal cross-strait exchanges. However, the Taiwanese authorities are afraid of such exchanges. It is they who have stopped this kind of cross-strait interaction, including even academic exchanges.

Regarding the discussion of 2027, it is worth noting that, over the past 10 years or so, many American generals and admirals have made conflicting predictions. Has any one of them ultimately been proved correct?

I think this is entirely irresponsible. There is also disagreement at the highest levels of the US government regarding the 2027 claim about Taiwan. For example, the remarks of US Secretary of Defence Pete Hegseth on the situation in the Taiwan Strait are almost the opposite of those of his predecessor Lloyd Austin at the Shangri-La Dialogue.

I believe that China still hopes for peaceful reunification, which is why it has not established a specific timeline. Because China still has hope, it will not provide such a timeline. The absence of a timeline leaves all these people continually guessing, though.

We have not renounced scenarios in which force may have to be used. These circumstances are clearly set out in Chinese law. Nevertheless, I believe that peaceful reunification remains the objective.

Editor’s note: Beijing sees Taiwan as part of China to be reunited by force if necessary. Most countries, including the US, do not recognise Taiwan as an independent state, but Washington is opposed to any attempt to take the self-ruled island by force and is committed to supplying it with weapons.

What is your assessment of US arms sales to Taiwan after the Xi-Trump meeting in May?

I believe that the United States’ arms sales to Taiwan will be more and more difficult in the future, right from now.

If the United States continues to sell arms to Taiwan, China could take three types of action.

First, China could also choose not to honour certain commitments it has made to the United States, including commercial agreements, if it believes that the United States has violated the one-China principle. This could involve products such as Boeing, beef and soybeans. None of these products is indispensable to China.

Second, China could adopt a broader range of countermeasures.

Finally, China could conduct increasingly frequent and sophisticated military exercises around the island to wear down the other side’s nerves.

Eventually, I believe the time will come sooner than the United States expects when it will have to ask itself the ultimate question: what are the actual benefits of selling arms to Taiwan, even if those sales are lucrative, when they create more risks than benefits?

Xi and Trump agreed during their meeting in May to pursue a bilateral relationship of constructive strategic stability. How stable, or fragile, is that relationship?

This concept is significant because this is the first time that China and the United States have agreed upon such a concept. It essentially refers to a form of equilibrium of strength between the United States and China.

I would say that this is more significant for China. Many Chinese people may believe that the United States has bullied China and looked down upon China.

As to whether this stability can be sustained, I believe that the word “constructive” means a great deal. It reflects good wishes for the future, but the question is how the relationship can become constructive rather than destructive.

Given the Taiwan issue, there is no guarantee that this objective can always be maintained. The United States maintains a policy of strategic ambiguity over the Taiwan issue. It is not guaranteed that it would become directly or fully involved in a conflict.

Moreover, the China-US relationship is fundamentally a competitive relationship, although this does not rule out areas of cooperation. The question is which aspect is predominant, and the answer is competition.

There will certainly be many challenges to this form of stability. The Americans may emphasise fairness and equality while China talks about mutual respect. Basically, they are talking about the same thing. But stability most certainly rules out the potential for conflict in the South China Sea and Taiwan.

Regarding Taiwan, do you think the Trump administration – or at least Trump himself – is moving away from the United States’ long-standing policy?

First, he said American soldiers should not fight a war 9,500 miles away. Second, he said that he had not made a decision regarding arms sales to Taiwan.

I think he is inclined to make such a decision, but he may find it very difficult. He didn’t say much on the Taiwan issue in Beijing, but there could be another three opportunities for the two leaders to meet. What would he have to say on the Taiwan issue and the arms sales during those meetings, and how might Beijing react? He has to consider all these matters.

Perhaps this indicates a possible adjustment, although this is only my own speculation. Trump’s proposed arms sale would be the largest in history.

This may mean that the United States intends to sell a larger quantity of weapons on fewer occasions. In terms of frequency, it could make each package larger while reducing the number of individual sales.

If you look at the record, arms sales have become increasingly difficult. Therefore, each time the United States decides to proceed, it may sell a large volume. China would then protest only once, rather than making three separate protests against three different sales.

Militarily speaking, the strength of the Chinese mainland is simply overwhelming in comparison with that of Taiwan. Therefore, it does not really matter what Taiwan buys. Nevertheless, the Taiwanese authorities feel that they have to purchase this insurance no matter how expensive it is.

Does the US effort to rebuild its shipbuilding and defence-industrial base signal a deeper industrial competition with China? How could this affect the long-term military balance and both sides’ ability to sustain a conflict?

From a military perspective, the United States is no longer as capable as it once was of projecting power globally.

Its growing focus on the western hemisphere and the Indo-Pacific reflects the fact that it no longer has sufficient resources to maintain an equally strong presence everywhere.

The US’ shipbuilding industry lags far behind China’s. This impact on its navy is already seen. Some estimates suggest that China’s shipbuilding capacity is over 230 times larger. I understand why Washington wants to rebuild its capacity, including through cooperation with Japan and South Korea, but I doubt this can be achieved quickly.

The picture varies by sector. The United States remains dominant in areas such as AI, but the gap is said to be a matter of months; its broader industrial and manufacturing capacity has clearly weakened.

This raises two questions: has the United States declined, and can it recover?

I describe Donald Trump as “Trump the Declinist”. His promise to “make America great again” implies that America has declined. In his own words, it is “carnage” that helped him to win his first presidency because many voters clearly accepted that argument.

The United States has recovered from periods of perceived decline before, including after the Vietnam war. Some believe it will do so again.

However, I think the situation may be fundamentally different this time. Even during the Vietnam war, the United States still had the world’s strongest industrial base.

Today, that foundation has weakened, suggesting that something more fundamental may have changed.

China conducted a missile test from a nuclear-powered submarine this month and subsequently disclosed information about it. What is your assessment of the test and US reactions?

I think people are somewhat curious about Chinese launches, in part because China actually didn’t have a lot of launches in the past.

A launch of this kind clearly carries strategic implications, although different countries may interpret it differently.

From a military and technical perspective, missile testing is necessary. Even after deployment, periodic tests remain necessary to verify that the system continues to function effectively and that the relevant operational capabilities are being maintained.

However, regarding what US officials reportedly said about China providing only a few hours’ notice before the launch, falling short of the practice of other nuclear powers, I don’t think there is any international standard specifying how far in advance such notifications should be given.

I believe an institutionalised mutual notification mechanism would be desirable, but it is unlikely while the United States continues with the Golden Dome project [a proposed missile defence system integrating land-, sea- and space-based sensors and interceptors].

Golden Dome represents an arms race at the highest level and would [threaten] China-US and Russia-US strategic stability. Although such a concept is broader than military affairs, it is primarily about military stability. During the Cold War, US-Soviet strategic stability was based on an equilibrium between their nuclear arsenals.

However, in my personal view, China-US strategic stability will never be based on numerical nuclear parity. I do not believe China would seek more than 5,000 nuclear warheads to match the American nuclear stockpile.

I have always argued that China should possess enough to ensure that the United States would never consider using nuclear weapons against it first.

If China’s objective is to deter the United States from using nuclear weapons against it, how many warheads would China need?

Even according to the Pentagon’s highest estimate – that China may possess 1,500 [nuclear] warheads by 2035 – the number would still remain far below that of the United States, assuming the estimate is accurate.

My argument is that China should possess enough nuclear weapons to deter any nuclear adventurism by the United States. The required number would depend on two factors: how the US nuclear arsenal develops and how China would need to respond to those developments.

In the worst-case scenario of a conflict across the Taiwan Strait, China would not want to be subjected to nuclear blackmail.

The second concerns the broader global strategic level, where the objective is to maintain strategic stability. This was also mentioned in [China’s] 15th five-year plan. We should preserve global nuclear stability. However, the question is how such stability could be maintained if China possessed only a few hundred nuclear weapons.

People should not be excessively concerned about China’s nuclear weapons because China’s no-first-use policy is one of its longest-standing defence policies.

How will AI capabilities affect the long-term military balance between China and the United States?

American dominance in AI is not a sure thing.

Even where one side may possess an advantage in certain areas, China may be stronger in the large-scale application of AI. This therefore makes the overall situation more complicated.

I think both countries are working very hard on the military application of AI.

The character of warfare has already changed, and military forces must become adaptable. If you observed the military parade last year, you would have seen that the PLA has not merely been watching these developments. It has already taken steps to respond to the emerging challenges.

A challenge is that we are not fully aware of how rapidly artificial intelligence can develop or in which particular fields it will advance most quickly.

During wartime, not only people but also artificial intelligence may learn and develop at the fastest pace. We have seen how rapidly AI has changed the battlefield in Ukraine and in the American war against Iran. This shows us how quickly the entire battlefield is being transformed.

I definitely believe that rules limiting AI’s role, such as in military decision-making, should be established. But sadly, as we saw in many cases, sometimes rules are only made after an incident. During the Cold War, there were quite a few false alarms of nuclear attack. Hopefully we shall never have false alarms triggered by AI.

The US military has continued to strengthen its posture along the first island chain. At the same time, the PLA has increasingly conducted operations beyond the chain. In your view, does the first island chain still carry the same strategic significance that it did during the Cold War?

Regarding the first island chain, I do not believe that it matters as much to the PLA as it once did, because the PLA is much stronger today and the overall US military presence at bases in these countries is not particularly large.

Second, I have serious doubts about whether these US allies could provide effective assistance to the United States in an actual conflict. I think all these countries would seek to hedge against such a possibility.

Japan might be an exception, in the sense that it is legally obliged to provide military bases to the United States. However, whether Japan would go further and fight shoulder to shoulder with the United States is not guaranteed, unless it believed that it faced an existential threat.

That is why, when [Japanese Prime Minister Sanae] Takaichi described such a scenario as an existential threat, she was strongly criticised within Japan itself. There is therefore no guarantee that what she described would actually happen in such a scenario.

There has been stronger military cooperation between Japan and the Philippines recently. How does this development challenge China’s strategic interests?

For military planners, any change in the surrounding security environment is naturally a concern because it adds new factors to their calculations. These are trends that China must watch, but I do not see them as game changers.

In the South China Sea, Japan’s role remains largely symbolic. Despite its assistance to the Philippines, there has not been a Japanese naval presence. I would describe it mainly as a political gesture with limited military implications.

In the Taiwan Strait, we should first remember that even US involvement is not guaranteed. The stronger China becomes, the more reluctant other countries will be to intervene. Japan’s role would therefore be secondary, apart from providing bases under its legal obligations.

Japan would have concerns even about providing bases because it would have to consider its own security. Japan could not simply say, “We are only providing bases and are not involved.”

If US aircraft took off from Japanese bases to take part in combat in the Taiwan Strait, China would regard Japan as involved. Japanese leaders would probably make such a decision only at the last moment.

I believe the Philippines is also aware of the risks. It wants to make use of the United States, but not to the extent that it becomes a target itself. That is why it has imposed conditions on US access to its bases, including limitations on their use for attacks during wartime.

Against the backdrop of strained China-Japan relations, could military encounters between the two increase the risks of a military incident?

Chinese and Japanese naval vessels seldom encounter one another. So, the risk is low. But Chinese and American naval vessels often encounter one another. China, the US and Japan are all members of the Code for Unplanned Encounters at Sea, or Cues, and they also have an air and maritime communication mechanism. These arrangements provide technical rules intended to prevent accidents, although they are not legally binding.

All these rules are technical rules, but politics always exists behind them.

There has therefore long been a tension between safety and security. Technical rules address safety, but security involves broader implications, on which the two sides may never agree.

Returning to this particular case, it would of course be safer if the Chinese and American navies could deconflict at sea. However, even if they maintain a so-called safe distance, the question remains: what constitutes a safe distance?

Unfortunately, there is no regulation or agreement that precisely defines a safe distance.

Safe conduct therefore ultimately relies on three abstract concepts: good airmanship, good seamanship and voluntary judgment – and that judgment is inevitably influenced by politics.

The rules were made because both sides have the bottom line, that is, none of us wants an accident. So, this is contradictory.
