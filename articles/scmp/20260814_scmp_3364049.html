UN atrocity report adds friction to Myanmar junta’s diplomatic push for legitimacy

SCMP | FREE | 1032 words | 2026-08-14

A UN investigative report accusing Myanmar’s military of carrying out a “sustained campaign of deliberate attacks on civilians” has muddied Min Aung Hlaing’s push for regional acceptance – but analysts say it is unlikely to derail his efforts to win recognition for his post-election government.

The report by the Independent Investigative Mechanism for Myanmar (IIMM), a body appointed by the UN Human Rights Council, documents a year-long investigation into alleged “war crimes and crimes against humanity” committed by Myanmar’s military and its linked militias, as well as by armed opposition groups.

It said the military campaign included air strikes on homes, schools and camps for displaced people, alongside arbitrary detention, torture and sexual violence.

Investigators said the military had increased its use of low-cost aircraft, such as motorised paragliders and gyrocopters, in strikes against anti-government groups. “Double-tap” aerial attacks, in which a second strike follows the first, harming first responders, those helping the wounded and fleeing residents, are also on the rise.

According to the report, there was an escalation of abuses in December and January, around the time the country was conducting junta-led elections that saw Min Aung Hlaing elected as president and its military-backed party winning seats in the absence of any viable opposition.

Min Aung Hlaing, now a retired general, led the 2021 coup that overthrew Myanmar’s elected leader Aung San Suu Kyi, who has remained under military custody.

The military government did not immediately respond to the UN report or separate claims by the Arakan Army, a major ethnic armed group in Rakhine state, alleging that 20 civilians had been killed in military-led strikes this month. It has previously said it attacks only legitimate military targets and has accused resistance forces of terrorism.

Legitimacy push

The report comes amid a series of efforts by Min Aung Hlaing to seek legitimacy for his government, years after facing sanctions from the West and a ban from Asean meetings until the country complies with the bloc’s five-point consensus.

The president visited two Association of Southeast Asian Nations members recently: Thailand last week and Laos in July. He has previously visited India and China.

But he has refused to deal with Asean as a whole – rejecting the five-point consensus this month as well as a request for its special envoy, Philippine Foreign Secretary Maria Theresa Lazaro, to meet Suu Kyi.

“Min Aung Hlaing and his military administration are clearly not willing to engage Asean’s terms,” said Mai Van Tran, an assistant professor at the Department of Political Science and faculty associate with the Centre for Southeast Asian Studies at Northern Illinois University.

“Further support for the Min Aung Hlaing-led administration is unlikely to benefit these Asean states and might even facilitate humanitarian consequences that go against these countries’ values.”

But while the report might cloud the junta government’s optics, it was unlikely to affect its president’s push for legitimacy, other analysts say.

“It does not derail Min Aung Hlaing’s plans; however, it does puncture the narrative,” said Dipannita Maria Bagh, chief editor at Spring University Myanmar’s Institute of Human Rights and Democratic Governance.

One report could not stop the momentum, said Joanne Lin, a senior fellow at the ISEAS-Yusof Ishak Institute. “Countries that were already prepared to engage Myanmar, especially immediate neighbours with border, trade and security concerns, are unlikely to reverse course just because of this IIMM’s report.”

Min Aung Hlaing’s political objective “is likely to keep separating the external legitimacy track from the accountability track”, she said. “He might continue seeking meetings, photo opportunities, business forums and security cooperation with countries willing to engage him, while presenting Myanmar as a government dealing with internal conflict and restoring stability.”

Countries looking to re-engage Myanmar are likely looking at pragmatic considerations.

“Thailand’s priorities, for example, have been to deal with practical problems such as border security, transnational crime, online scams, drugs and humanitarian issues,” Lim said. “The report is unlikely to change neighbouring countries’ priorities but might make them more cautious about presenting engagements as normal diplomacy.”

Countries willing to engage bilaterally, such as Thailand, had “shown they will compartmentalise the atrocity documentation from the diplomacy”, Bagh said. “But it raises the cost of that compartmentalisation.”

Awkward position

The report could, however, reinforce the perception that Myanmar is not ready for bigger overtures.

“The rejection of the special envoy and [consensus] in general will complicate the case of recalibrating ties with Myanmar,” Lin said. “For more cautious Asean members, the report could reinforce the argument that fuller re-engagement should remain conditional.”

Bagh said that Myanmar’s rejection of Asean oversight mechanisms was the latest among multiple methods of controlling the narrative and painting a picture of stability.

“The junta is likely to emphasise the election and the formation of a post-election government to argue that Myanmar has entered a new political phase,” Lin said.

Thailand’s re-engagement with Myanmar would put it in an awkward position with the bloc but it had little option as it was directly affected by concerns such as refugee flows, migrant labour, border trade, online scam centres, drugs, haze, pollution and security conditions along the joint border, Lin said.

“From Bangkok’s perspective, some form of working relationship with Naypyidaw is necessary, regardless of Asean’s broader political discomfort,” she said. “The risk is that Myanmar may read Thailand’s position as evidence that it can regain regional acceptance through bilateral channels without making sufficient concessions on violence reduction.”

Thailand’s own foreign minister had to publicly insist the country was not abandoning the five-point consensus, “right as Naypyidaw was rejecting the bloc’s envoy and Myanmar’s parliament was calling the peace plan interference”, Bagh said.

“That’s a junta showing Asean that engagement doesn’t buy compliance. For Asean states more cautious about Myanmar, the lack of compliance from Naypyidaw and the lack of need for an envoy give them a clean, evidence-based argument against full re-engagement. It doesn’t stop the diplomacy, but it makes the case for it much harder to make in public.”
