‘Chilling effect’: Japan’s whistle-blowers weigh high price of speaking out

SCMP | FREE | 1097 words | 2026-08-30

Yutaka Kato* knew there would be risks when he reported suspected misconduct by his employer to Japanese authorities.

What he did not expect, he said, was a 47.88 million yen (US$300,000) damages claim from that employer – the Japanese subsidiary of US-listed medical device manufacturer STAAR Surgical – along with the prospect of years in court and the uncertainty of whether he would ever return to work.

Kato is among a growing number of whistle-blowers in Japan who say laws meant to protect them from retaliation have not stopped companies from pursuing damages claims, dismissals and other actions that can leave them financially drained, professionally isolated and afraid to speak out.

His former employer’s claim was dismissed by the Tokyo District Court in December last year but was appealed to the High Court, with a ruling set to be handed down on September 3.

Kato has challenged his dismissal in a separate lawsuit, with the High Court ruling in his favour, although the company has appealed to Japan’s Supreme Court.

And he admits to being “anxious” ahead of the rulings.

“There is no public financial assistance to support my living expenses and the economic burden I face while the litigation continues,” he said.

“At present, there is also no clear prospect of my actually returning to work. The company may, of course, have a different view as to the reasons for my dismissal. From my perspective, however, I believe that the dismissal had a retaliatory aspect because I had made whistle-blower reports.”

When Japan introduced its Whistleblower Protection Act in 2004, it was expected to protect workers who reported suspected misconduct, including against their own employers.

More than two decades later, critics say the law’s limits have become clear in cases where employers argue that their claims concern conduct surrounding a disclosure, rather than the whistle-blowing itself.

The Act bars companies from dismissing or otherwise disadvantaging workers on the grounds of whistle-blowing and from seeking damages simply because they suffered losses as a result.

But legal experts and those targeted in such cases say whistle-blowers can still face termination, costly litigation and difficulty finding work in the same industry when companies cite related conduct – such as handling internal information – as the basis for action against them.

This Week in Asia reached out to STAAR Japan, which declined to comment on Kato’s case as it is “a pending legal matter”. But Kato’s legal representatives claim he was targeted after he filed reports with authorities.

Kato said the reports he made concerned suspected improper payments to doctors and the handling of patient information linked to surgical videos involving the company’s intraocular lenses.

In a related bribery case, a doctor at Yamato-Takada Municipal Hospital in Nara prefecture was later convicted of accepting payments linked to the use of the company’s lenses and sentenced to 18 months in prison, suspended for three years.

Four people linked to the company, including former executives, also received fines in connection with the bribery case.

The damages claim against Kato centres on the alleged removal of company information, rather than the act of whistle-blowing itself.

Aiko Ohbuchi, a lawyer specialising in corporate law at the Amour Law Office in Tokyo, said companies bringing such claims might not see themselves as exploiting a flaw in the legislation.

Article 7 of the Act provides that an employer “may not seek damages from a whistle-blower on the grounds that the employer has suffered damage as a result of the whistle-blowing”, she said.

This provision does not, however, expressly cover a whistle-blower collecting and taking out information to make a disclosure. Nor does it specifically prohibit an employer from seeking damages on the grounds that it suffered harm resulting from such conduct.

Ohbuchi said she was aware of 12 lawsuits where employers sought damages on the grounds that a whistle-blower collected or took information out of the company, although there were many more cases in which whistle-blowers were “subject to adverse treatment, such as dismissal or disciplinary action”.

She said she was confident Kato would prevail in the damages case.

“The courts have ruled in favour of whistle-blowers where the conduct was necessary to establish the truth of the matters reported and the method used was reasonable and proportionate,” she said.

Amendments due to take effect on December 1 would strengthen the law by expanding protection to freelancers and introducing criminal penalties for dismissal or discipline on the grounds of whistle-blowing, Ohbuchi said, adding that they would also shift the burden of proof in some disputes over dismissal or disciplinary action after a report.

While there was “growing awareness of the importance of protecting” those who spoke out, Ohbuchi cautioned that even a clear-cut victory in court might not dissuade firms from similar tactics in the future.

Other recent cases have underscored the same broader concern: that litigation itself can deter workers considering whether to report suspected wrongdoing.

Nozomi Fukuda, an ex-employee of the company behind Momuri – a high-profile Japanese resignation agency that helps workers quit their jobs – was among former staff who helped bring to light allegations that the agency had improperly referred clients to lawyers in return for fees.

Those allegations led to a criminal case against Momuri’s operator, Albatross, and its former president, according to Japanese media and company statements, but the company also pursued civil litigation against former employees over separate claims that included allegedly false statements about harassment.

Fukuda told Japanese media that the stress of the case caused her to suffer a cerebral infarction, transforming her life and that of her family. She later divorced her husband.

While she continues to feel the effects of her illness, the lawsuit remains unresolved. According to Japanese media, Fukuda has said that had she known in advance the toll it would take, she might have chosen to stay quiet.

Kato said he was frustrated by what he saw as a “significant gap” in the protection system that allows companies to sue whistle-blowers and potentially intimidate others considering speaking out.

“A very large damages claim can itself impose a significant financial and psychological burden on a defendant and potentially have a chilling effect on that person’s speech and actions,” he said.

“If the company ultimately wins and I am ordered to pay around 47.88 million yen, I do not have the financial means to pay that amount.

“In that event, I could potentially face a serious financial burden for decades to come.”

*Name changed to protect interviewee’s identity
