Analysis: Why China’s Banks Struggle to Finance Low-Carbon Transition

Caixin Global | FULLTEXT | 853 words | 2026-11-26 06:38

1. China's push to steer financing toward a low-carbon economy faces a significant hurdle: the lack of standardized emissions data. Without reliably knowing how much carbon is being produced, lenders cannot reward companies for cutting emissions or assess the climate risks of their own portfolios.

To address this, commercial banks are developing carbon-accounting systems to help corporate customers measure emissions, formulate reduction plans, and access financing linked to their environmental performance. However, the underlying data remains incomplete, and accounting methodologies vary considerably between banks.

]

3. The pressure to fix these shortcomings is growing. China's national assessment framework for carbon peaking and carbon neutrality, which took effect this year, is expected to raise demand for emissions reductions across regions and industries. For banks, this creates both an obligation and a potential business opportunity to finance technological upgrades for major emitters, but smaller companies risk being left behind because the costs of carbon accounting and compliance can represent a disproportionately large burden.

4. Reliable data is essential for transition finance, which depends on distinguishing companies making credible progress from those merely presenting themselves as greener. Without reliable data and clear benchmarks, banks risk offering preferential financing to companies failing to meet promised reductions.

5. Some lenders are building dedicated carbon-accounting services. Industrial Bank Co. Ltd., a leading green finance lender, launched a carbon management platform in 2023. It had recorded 54,500 corporate carbon accounts as of the end of September 2026 and expects the number to exceed 70,000 by year-end.

]

6. Other lenders have developed similar services. By the end of last year, China Citic Bank Corp. Ltd. had more than 1,800 users on its platform, while Postal Savings Bank of China Co. Ltd. had provided carbon-accounting services to more than 20,000 corporate clients.

[para.

7. These systems allow banks to identify companies' emissions profiles, compare performance with industry peers, and develop carbon-reduction plans. They also provide a basis for linking financing conditions to environmental performance, such as via transition-linked loans that offer lower interest rates when borrowers meet agreed reduction targets.

[para.

]

8. Industrial Bank launched 138 carbon finance products based on corporate carbon accounts in the first half of this year, with a combined value of 10.5 billion yuan ($1.6 billion), including a carbon quota pledge loan for the steel industry.

9. The attraction for lenders goes beyond regulatory compliance. Offering accounting services and technical guidance strengthens customer relationships and creates opportunities for other services. In a market where price competition is difficult to sustain, expertise in decarbonization could provide a key source of differentiation.

10. However, the industry's ability to turn these initiatives into a meaningful shift in capital allocation remains constrained by the quality of the underlying information. Corporate emissions data can be difficult to obtain, especially when companies are reluctant to share production and energy-consumption figures. Banks often rely on verified data from national carbon markets, estimates from operating data, or industry averages, increasing uncertainty as they move further from company-level figures.

11. Methodological differences create another problem. Banks may cover different industries and asset classes and use different approaches where established standards are unavailable, making the resulting figures incomparable and limiting their usefulness.

12. The issue is particularly apparent in accounting for financed emissions—the greenhouse gases generated by companies and projects that banks support. A 2026 report on major Chinese banks shows that of 65 banks surveyed, only 19 had disclosed emissions data associated with their investment and financing activities by the end of April, with many focusing on corporate loans and high-emission industries.

13. Reported annual financed emissions among the sampled banks ranged from 1 million to 30 million tons. These differences reflect not only portfolio composition but also variations in the assets and industries included in calculations, meaning the figures cannot be taken as a straightforward comparison.

14. A consistent national accounting framework and better data-sharing arrangements would help. Practitioners have called for standardized methods and a centralized system where companies submit information once for use by multiple financial institutions. Yet better accounting alone will not necessarily lead to a larger transition-finance market, as banks also face the costs of monitoring borrowers' progress, assessing technical credibility, and managing risks associated with high-carbon industries.

15. Small and midsize companies face a disproportionately larger burden. Their more modest financing needs mean the additional expenses for carbon accounting, environmental disclosure, and transition plans represent a larger share of overall financing costs. Many also lack specialist personnel needed to measure emissions and develop credible strategies.

16. While large companies have greater resources to meet disclosure requirements, smaller businesses may have little incentive to undertake costly exercises when financial benefits are uncertain. Banks can provide guidance and services, but they cannot shoulder the entire burden of persuading companies to transform their operations.

[para.

17. Industry practitioners have argued that transition-finance standards should incorporate differentiated compliance requirements for small and midsize businesses. Without a proportionate approach, the costs of demonstrating environmental progress could make preferential financing less accessible to smaller borrowers, potentially concentrating support among companies already better equipped to undertake the transition.
