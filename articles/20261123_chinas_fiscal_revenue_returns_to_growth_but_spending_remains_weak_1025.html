China’s Fiscal Revenue Returns to Growth, but Spending Remains Weak

Caixin Global | FULLTEXT | 728 words | 2026-11-23 11:51

1. In October 2025, China's general public budget (GPB) revenue rebounded to growth, rising 3.4% year on year to reach 2.3 trillion yuan (approximately $344 billion). This increase represents a decisive reversal from the sharp decline witnessed in September, according to data released by the Ministry of Finance on Friday. The headline figure, however, masks a highly uneven fiscal landscape, as the rebound was propelled by distinct one-off factors rather than broad-based economic strength. The GPB is recognized as the largest of China's four budgets, making its performance a key indicator of overall government financial health.

2. The most significant contributor to this revenue growth was the surge in personal income tax collections, which skyrocketed by 89.5% year on year to 296.4 billion yuan in October. This dramatic spike is directly attributable to a newly introduced tax policy aimed at offshore trusts. The policy compelled individuals holding assets in offshore trusts to retrospectively declare them and settle unpaid taxes, leading to a large influx of back-tax payments into the national treasury. This one-off measure effectively acted as a fiscal windfall, temporarily inflating revenue figures.

3. Data from the State Taxation Administration underscores the magnitude of this policy effect. By November 11, taxpayers had declared more than 160 billion yuan in personal income tax liabilities connected to offshore trusts, with over 120 billion yuan of that amount collected in October alone. The sheer scale of these collections highlights how the offshore trust crackdown served as a major catalyst for the month's overall fiscal growth, potentially masking underlying weaknesses in other tax categories.

4. The non-uniform nature of the recovery is particularly evident when examining other tax streams. For example, revenue from import-related value-added tax (VAT) and consumption tax increased by a robust 17.8% year on year, suggesting some resilience in trade and import activities. Conversely, domestic VAT and corporate income tax revenue both slipped by 0.1% year on year, indicating that domestic consumption, production, and corporate profitability are still struggling to gain momentum. This divergence highlights a skewed recovery where external trade-related taxes perform better than domestic economic indicators, reflecting the uneven impact of global trade conditions versus weak internal demand.

5. On the expenditure front, fiscal policy remained remarkably cautious. GPB expenditure edged up by only 0.5% in October, following a period of essentially stagnant spending in September. Given that the GPB is the largest and most comprehensive of China's four budget categories, its spending level is a critical barometer for government intervention in the economy. The persistently low growth in spending suggests that policymakers are holding back on fiscal expansion, possibly due to concerns over debt sustainability or a desire to wait for clearer economic signals before deploying stimulus measures.

6. Local governments continue to confront serious fiscal pressures, predominantly emanating from the property sector. Land-sale revenue, a cornerstone of local government financing, fell by 9% year on year to 243.9 billion yuan in October. Although this decline narrowed from the sharper contraction seen in September, the cumulative picture remains stark: over the first ten months of 2025, land-sale receipts plummeted by 25.8% to 1.85 trillion yuan. This sustained drop undermines local fiscal autonomy and restricts the ability of local authorities to fund infrastructure projects, public services, and debt repayments, exacerbating their financial strain.

7. In summary, the October fiscal data paints a complex and somewhat contradictory picture of China's fiscal health. The headline 3.4% revenue growth, celebrated as a return to positive territory, is heavily reliant on the extraordinary income tax collections flowing from the offshore trust policy—an inherently one-off measure. The underlying revenue base shows fragility, with domestic VAT and corporate income tax both slightly negative, and expenditure growth remaining minimal. Meanwhile, the persistent decline in land-sale revenue continues to erode local government finances over the long term. The accompanying chart, titled "China's Fiscal Revenue Returns to Growth," visually displays the year-on-year percentage changes, clearly illustrating the October spike to 3.4% after a significant drop earlier in the year. The chart also notes that January and February data are combined, and it cites the Ministry of Finance, CEIC, and Caixin as sources. Overall, this combination of one-off gains and persistent structural weaknesses implies that China's fiscal recovery is far from robust, and may be prone to stalling once the effects of the tax policy fade.
