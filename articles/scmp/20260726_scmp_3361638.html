Amid Hormuz woes, China tests Central Asian land corridors to bypass maritime chokepoints

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1789 words | 2026-07-26

Beijing is doubling down on overland trade routes across Central Asia as continued disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz and the wider region expose the vulnerability of global shipping chokepoints.

A new link to Afghanistan via Uzbekistan offers a practical example of this long-term diversification push. However, observers caution that security concerns and capacity constraints could make a meaningful shift away from sea freight difficult.

Officially launched in May with pilot shipments, the multi-modal freight corridor allows containers to be moved by rail from China through Kazakhstan into Uzbekistan, and then by road via Turkmenistan to Herat in western Afghanistan.

According to media reports in Afghanistan and Uzbekistan, the 7,400km (4,600-mile) route cuts delivery times by up to two-thirds – taking 30 days instead of two to three months via traditional sea-and-land routes through Iran or Pakistan.

China and Afghanistan share a border only at the Wakhan corridor, a narrow, high-altitude strip.

The rugged terrain, a lack of paved infrastructure and security concerns in China’s western Xinjiang region severely limit direct trade via the corridor. As a result, Beijing relies on safer, established routes.

Goods bound for western Afghanistan earlier typically travelled by sea to Iran’s Bandar Abbas port or via Pakistan before overland trucking. Such journeys often took an average of 31 to 55 days.

Afghan officials have hailed the corridor as a more efficient, lower-cost pathway, citing reduced costs and improved cargo safety despite domestic instability.

They told TOLOnews, an independent Afghan news broadcaster, in May that the corridors were operating regularly and could transform the landlocked, mountainous country into a key regional transit hub connecting Central Asia with South Asia and Iran.

“The transportation of commercial goods through the two corridors, China-Kazakhstan-Uzbekistan-Afghanistan and China-Kyrgyzstan-Uzbekistan-Afghanistan, is ongoing regularly,” a spokesman for Afghanistan’s public works ministry was quoted as saying.

“Given its strategic location, Afghanistan can facilitate regional connectivity and link Central Asian countries with South Asia and Iran.”

Uzbekistan Railways subsidiary Uztemiryulkonteyner confirmed the pilot shipments in May, noting that containers were consolidated at the Bukhara logistics centre before being trucked onwards.

Nargiza Umarova at the University of World Economy and Diplomacy in Tashkent sees the corridor as part of a broader strategic vision linking two railways.

She noted that it was expected to connect with the proposed Uzbekistan-Afghanistan-Pakistan (UAP) corridor and the imminent China-Kyrgyzstan-Uzbekistan railway project.

“It is important to understand that no transcontinental land corridor can compete with maritime transport routes in terms of cargo volume,” said Umarova, who heads the Centre for Strategic Connectivity at the university’s Institute for Advanced International Studies.

“At the same time, however, rail transport enables the diversification of foreign trade flows and significantly reduces delivery times.

“In light of the situation in the Strait of Hormuz, it also offers an opportunity to minimise the risks associated with crises in maritime shipping.”

She described Uzbekistan, which sees itself as a rising transit hub, as playing a “pivotal role” in these projects, reflecting Tashkent’s long-standing drive to diversify foreign trade flows and reduce logistics costs as a doubly landlocked country – a landlocked nation surrounded by other landlocked nations.

“Integrating these initiatives will not only take Uzbekistan to a qualitatively new level of development, but also benefit the entire Central Asian region, which will become a hub for intercontinental logistics,” she said.

Jennifer Murtazashvili, a non-resident scholar in the Asia Programme at the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, offered a somewhat different assessment.

She described the corridor as “mostly a political signal, with modest regional trade value and almost no energy hedge”.

“The corridor moves containers, not oil or gas, and China’s chokepoint problem is an energy problem,” Murtazashvili said.

“You cannot truck the roughly 11 million imported barrels a day China burns across Afghanistan. So, this route does close to nothing for China’s sea-lane energy exposure.”

She added that while the new route mattered for regional trade in grain and consumer goods, “it’s trivial against maritime volumes”.

“What it does do is give Afghanistan and its neighbours an overland alternative to the old China-to-Bandar Abbas sea route,” she noted, while arguing that the corridor’s biggest challenge was not one single bottleneck but a chain of them.

Containers move by rail from China through Kazakhstan into Uzbekistan, then transfer to trucks through Turkmenistan to Herat.

“Each border is a separate customs regime and a separate place to extract rents,” she said, emphasising that overland transport was structurally more expensive than sea for goods that were not time-sensitive.

Murtazashvili stressed that Afghanistan’s risk lay less in “instability” in the abstract than in weak governance.

“The Taliban lack the administrative capacity, contract enforcement and security guarantees that make cross-border transit routine at scale,” she said, adding that “militant groups including ISKP pose a direct threat to infrastructure and personnel, with a documented pattern of attacks on Chinese targets”.

ISKP or Islamic State Khorasan (Province), also known as Isis-K, is a regional affiliate of Islamic State or Isis.

“Afghanistan can be a functioning link for modest regional trade. It can’t be a reliable partner for great-power logistics while it can’t govern or secure transit,” Murtazashvili cautioned.

“Hold this route as an option in a portfolio, don’t depend on it as a lifeline,” she said, adding that in a crisis, each state tended to act in its own interest.

Zhu Yongbiao, director of the Centre for Afghanistan Studies at Lanzhou University in China, said the Afghan corridor underscored Beijing’s diversification efforts, as the Hormuz crisis had exposed just how much of China’s energy supply and trade still depended on sea lanes vulnerable to conflict.

But he cautioned against overstating the strategic importance of the Afghanistan route.

The combined rail and road transport network had operated in various forms for years, beginning as a commercial initiative under the previous Afghan government rather than as a direct response to Hormuz tensions, Zhu noted.

“It has no direct link to the situation in the Strait of Hormuz.”

He also pointed to the corridor’s limited capacity and focus on containerised goods rather than bulk energy shipments such as oil.

Zhu argued that Afghanistan’s ambition to become a transit hub was undermined by severe infrastructure gaps, limited economic viability and sanctions by the United States and its allies including counterterrorism penalties targeting the Taliban and its funds.

“On the map Afghanistan appears to have potential, but in reality the conditions do not support it,” he said.

“Even without considering politics, sanctions or security, the problems are significant and feasibility is very low because the infrastructure and economic viability are weak, so the corridor is more of a supplement than a main artery.”

He also pointed to “core internal obstacles”: the Taliban government’s lack of broad international recognition, ongoing governance struggles and tense border clashes with neighbouring Pakistan.

“The main obstacles come from Afghanistan itself, not external factors. Without internal change and infrastructure improvement, the problem cannot be solved,” Zhu warned.

China has yet to officially recognise the Taliban government that took power in 2021, but Beijing maintains an embassy in Kabul, hosts Taliban leaders and engages in security talks as well as investment and economic cooperation.

Beijing has also considered utilising the ancient Wakhan Corridor as a potential direct link between Afghanistan and Xinjiang, underscoring its long‑term interest in cross‑border connectivity.

Despite these challenges, rail traffic between Afghanistan and Uzbekistan has grown sharply.

According to TOLOnews, railway officials in Uzbekistan said that January to May trade volume nearly doubled year-on-year, with more than 41,000 freight wagons moving 2.7 million tonnes of goods between the two countries.

The Hormuz crisis, triggered by US and Israeli strikes on Iran since February, has revived long-standing concerns in Beijing about the “Malacca dilemma”, shorthand for China’s dependence on maritime chokepoints such as the Strait of Malacca for oil and gas imports.

While maritime routes remain essential, Beijing has spent years building cross-border railways, port links and inland corridors to ensure no single chokepoint dominates its energy security.

While the Afghan corridor will not resolve China’s broader exposure to Gulf instability on the energy front, it forms part of Beijing’s wider effort to broaden supply and transport options, from the flagship China-Pakistan Economic Corridor to the Middle Corridor via Kazakhstan and Turkey, as well as proposed trans-Afghan links.

Zhu said the cross-border corridor projects fitted into Beijing’s wider Belt and Road Initiative, which he described as a resilient network designed to avoid reliance on single routes amid geopolitical turbulence.

Many alternative routes bypassing Afghanistan were already advancing, he noted, with Kabul at risk of being marginalised given its high investment costs.

“Afghanistan’s image as the ‘heart of Asia’ or ‘crossroads of Eurasia’ is more imagination than reality,” he said.

“If this situation continues, its role as a transit hub will decline further. Within [the] belt and road’s network, Afghanistan is at best an add-on, not a critical node.”

He contrasted Afghanistan’s limitations with the stronger potential of Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan to emerge as transport hubs, because of their superior infrastructure, governance and stability.

But Umarova was more optimistic.

She argued that despite the risk of instability in Afghanistan amid tensions along the Afghan-Pakistani border, the Taliban government had shown strong political will to “integrate the country into regional connectivity projects”.

She suggested that Pakistan, which has direct access to the Indian Ocean, should be part of a China-Uzbekistan-Afghanistan-Pakistan transport corridor to “create alternative opportunities for China to access global markets by bypassing the Strait of Malacca and Hormuz”.

Its importance, she argued, went beyond immediate crises, such as the military conflict in the Middle East.

“The project is strategically founded and designed to ensure sustainable transport connectivity between the various poles of the Asian continent, with the active participation of Central Asian states and Afghanistan,” she said.

She said the planned Trans-Afghan Railway, also known as the Kabul Corridor, could provide the shortest land link between Central Asia and the Indian subcontinent, potentially handling up to 22 million tonnes of cargo a year and offering a route 1,200km shorter than the International North-South Transport Corridor through Iran.

The proposed line, first put forward by Uzbekistan in 2018, is intended as an alternative north-south transport and transit route. In Umarova’s view, it could eventually connect major economic centres in Europe, China, Russia and India.
