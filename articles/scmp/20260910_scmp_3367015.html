Singapore MPs back ministerial pay rise, but agree money alone won’t lure talent

SCMP | FREE | 1197 words | 2026-09-10

While Singapore’s officials are among the highest-paid in the world, money alone cannot attract the brightest leaders to public service, lawmakers have said during a debate on the increase in ministerial salaries – a move supported by both opposition and ruling party MPs.

The parliamentary discussion on Thursday, which saw MPs share their personal reflections on stepping into public office, followed an announced rise in ministerial salary, which Prime Minister Lawrence Wong insisted was necessary for leadership renewal and to keep pace with the private sector.

Benchmark salaries of Wong’s cabinet will be increased by more than 60 per cent, ranging from S$1.8 million (US$1.4 million) to S$3.6 million annually. Ministers will receive a one-off rise of up to 9 per cent next month, with subsequent increments subject to individual performance and responsibilities.

Singapore last adjusted the salaries of political holders in 2012, slashing wages by more than 35 per cent. A recommendation to increase the pay following a 2017 review was not adopted.

“If too many capable people conclude that political service asks for sacrifices which are just too great, then over time we will have fewer people prepared to step forward,” Wong said as he rounded up the debate that lasted over 3½ hours.

“Eventually, the quality of our leadership will suffer and Singapore will suffer the consequences.”

He called it a “mistaken framework” that the purpose of the salary structure was to entice people into politics with money since “motivation to serve” and “responding to the call” had to come first.

“We have to persuade good people that Singapore needs them, that the work matters, that they can make a difference. Then, if someone is prepared to step forward, remuneration becomes a practical consideration,” Wong said.

Earlier, opposition Workers’ Party chief Pritam Singh questioned if the city state’s high earners in the private sector were deterred from politics by lower pay or whether the issue was political responsibilities.

He argued that most of Singapore’s cabinet continued to be fielded from the public sector, while “only a small handful” chose to leave the private sector for politics.

“For some, it would not be out of order to suggest that no amount of money will persuade them to trade their suits and ties for grass-roots activities and to immerse themselves in municipal concerns and the rough and tumble of politics,” he said.

Singh in parliament reiterated his party’s view that ministers’ salaries should be a multiple of an MP’s allowance instead of pegged to the median wage of the top 1,000 earners in Singapore, with a discount. The approach of WP – the only opposition party in parliament – would have resulted in more modest increases over time, he said.

Wong argued that the WP’s formula would arrive at a similar figure and if the salary was benchmarked with inflation, a higher sum would be derived.

“For all intents and purposes, within this term of government, we agree on the implementation approach. Beyond that, we will have to consider the circumstances then,” Wong said, noting that another independent review would be done in five years.

Wong also countered the argument that Singapore’s office holders were among the highest earners in the world, saying political office holders in many countries were given pensions, allowances, housing and other benefits beyond their official salary package.

As prime minister, Wong’s salary would be increased from S$2.2 million to S$3.6 million. He pledged to donate the increment to good causes for five years and said on Thursday that he would not keep the tax benefits from his donation.

Minister for Trade and Industry (Energy and Industry) Tan See Leng, a former doctor and medical entrepreneur, shared his personal experience of giving up his career as group CEO and managing director of IHH Healthcare to enter politics in 2020, at the age of 55.

Tan said on Thursday that he made the move after a “growing inner realisation” that his experience in the private sector could be put to good use to give back to the country. He added that no realistic level of ministerial salary could have matched what he was earning in the private sector.

On attracting future talent, Tan said: “If we make entry into the public service come at too high a personal cost for too many capable Singaporeans, then over time we will indeed narrow the pool from which our country can draw its leaders and, members of the House, that would be a far greater cost than any salary debate.”

Singapore’s ministerial salary model ensured that its leaders were “not incentivised to look elsewhere” and act with citizens’ best interests in mind, said Christopher de Souza, a lawmaker from the ruling People’s Action Party.

Prolonged wage stagnation may lead political officeholders to view their time in office as a stepping stone for their professional careers, he said, citing examples of state leaders from the United States and the United Kingdom who earned large sums from assets and corporate deals after leaving office.

“If politicians know that their future careers may depend on relationships built while in office, the public will inevitably wonder whether today’s decisions are made solely in the national interest, or whether tomorrow’s opportunities are also at the back of the minds of their leaders,” he said.

Several MPs raised concerns regarding negative public sentiment over ministerial pay increases and some asked for more information to be made public on how ministers’ performances were reviewed.

Wong said he understood the concerns, but there was never going to be a “politically convenient time” to adjust salaries.

“If every difficult circumstance becomes a reason not to act, then we will keep putting the issue off, and there will come a point where restraint ceases to be prudence and becomes neglect,” he said, adding that leadership renewal was urgent.

On how ministers’ performance bonuses were distributed, he cautioned against the assessment being made into a public popularity contest and said that the cabinet was a team that Singaporeans should judge based on how the government as a whole performed.

“If we fall short, Singaporeans can choose another team to govern. That is the final accountability in our system,” Wong said.

The pay bump reignited a long-standing debate on the thorny issue, with opposition parties voicing swift criticism. Some pointed out ongoing economic uncertainty and rising unemployment rates, while others criticised the salary framework.

Chong Ja Ian, an associate professor of political science at the National University of Singapore, said the argument of recruiting and retaining talent was justifiable from a human resource perspective.

He said the amounts would, however, appear “eye-watering” to the public and raise questions about “other forms of inspiration” to serve the people.

“Even without the pay rises, political appointees still earn more than enough to afford a very, very comfortable standard of living.”

He suggested that indicators such as total fertility rate and progress on climate issues be included in the assessment for compensation for political officeholders.

Currently, ministers’ salaries include a national bonus, which is linked to socio-economic indicators such as the growth rate of residents’ median income and Singapore’s real gross domestic product.
