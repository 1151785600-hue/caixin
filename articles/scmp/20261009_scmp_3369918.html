After cordial Xi-Trump summit, could China’s US dollar pivot cause tremors?

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1559 words | 2026-10-09

As President Xi Jinping and US President Donald Trump talked tariffs, agricultural quotas and critical minerals at last month’s closely watched summit in Washington, an “elephant in the room” loomed over the discussion: China’s efforts to gradually diversify away from US dollar assets.

Beijing has gradually been cutting its holdings of US Treasuries while pushing to expand the yuan’s global use and developing alternative financial architecture. While these issues may not have featured in the summit’s official communique, they have not gone unnoticed by economists and investors – or by policymakers on both sides of the Pacific.

Charles Chang, a finance professor at Fudan University, said topics such as China’s holdings of US debt were likely to have been raised during the talks, although he added they were unlikely to result in a formal agreement similar to those covering trade.

“Unlike tariffs, which can be set out as part of a policy framework, sovereign debt management involves actual capital allocation and is highly sensitive, making it unlikely to be formally addressed in official statements,” Chang noted.

“However, from a strategic perspective, Beijing’s ongoing reserve diversification does serve as an important piece of leverage in these high-stakes bilateral negotiations.”

These moves, together with Beijing’s push to internationalise its currency and develop alternative payment infrastructure, are viewed by some analysts as largely defensive measures rather than an attempt to displace the US dollar.

But they cautioned that any efforts to protect China against potential US financial pressure could themselves become a long-term source of friction between the two countries, particularly if Washington sees them as weakening its currency’s international role.

Matteo Giovannini, Centre for China and Globalisation

The Treasury retreat

Long regarded as one of the safest assets in global markets, US Treasuries have come under increasing pressure as investors grapple with the more than US$40 trillion federal debt burden and the prospect of interest rates continuing to remain high amid stubborn inflation.

A global sell-off in government bonds in recent months has pushed long-term Treasury yields to multi-decade highs, with both the 10-year and 30-year yields recently rising to their highest levels since 2002. Underscoring this retreat, Norges Bank Investment Management, which manages Norway’s US$2.3 trillion sovereign wealth fund, recently proposed a significant reduction in its exposure to US government bonds.

Short-term market factors have played a role, but the latest unease about American government debt has also fed into a broader debate over “de-dollarisation”.

Barry Eichengreen, a professor of economics and political science at the University of California, Berkeley, described this latest wave as different from previous episodes, as questions mount over the strength of American political institutions and the durability of its geopolitical alliances.

Japan, one of Washington’s long-standing allies, cut its US Treasury holdings for three consecutive months to US$1.1 trillion in July, according to the latest data from the US Treasury Department.

Once Washington’s largest foreign creditor, China continues to diversify its foreign reserves away from US Treasuries as uncertainties in the bilateral relationship persist. The shift has also coincided with growing concern among Chinese officials and academics over the risks of relying too heavily on the American currency.

China dropped to third place among foreign holders of US Treasuries in March last year, and its holdings fell to an 18-year low of US$618 billion this July, according to the US Treasury Department – though questions remain over whether headline figures tell the full story.

Brad Setser, a senior fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations, wrote last month that China’s state-owned banks held substantial dollar assets offshore that were not included in the country’s official reserves.

At the same time, Chang said China’s massive US Treasury holdings might give Beijing some potential leverage because a deliberate sell-off could destabilise markets.

“Beijing can also use modest, short-term increases in its holdings to send a friendly signal, particularly when the Treasury market is under pressure,” Chang said. “It is a reciprocal diplomatic dynamic – if China shows goodwill, it would naturally expect certain concessions from the US in exchange.”

Some analysts argued that China also had an interest in preventing a disorderly crisis in the US Treasury market, even as it continued to gradually reduce its stockpiles.

“Beijing still has significant dollar exposure, so a sharp Treasury sell-off would hurt China as well,” said Matteo Giovannini, a non-resident associate fellow at the Centre for China and Globalisation. “I would therefore distinguish between gradual diversification and actively destabilising the market.”

China’s continued trimming of Treasury holdings was nevertheless part of a broader shift in the market’s investor base, Giovannini noted, making it “more sensitive to supply-demand pressures.”

Wiring a safety net

Alongside China’s diversification away from US dollar-denominated assets, Beijing is building financial infrastructure that could give it more options for cross-border payments and trade. That includes efforts to expand the international use of the yuan and systems such as the Cross-border Interbank Payment System (CIPS) and Project mBridge.

CIPS provides an alternative channel for cross-border payments to Western-dominated networks such as the Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication, commonly known as Swift. Project mBridge, meanwhile, is a multilateral platform designed to ease direct settlements using digital currencies issued by central banks.

Both initiatives have made headway, though the pace of growth has varied. The value of transactions processed through CIPS grew at a double-digit pace for several years before rising by just 2.7 per cent last year, official data showed. Transaction value then rose 11.9 per cent year on year to 100.9 trillion yuan (US$14.9 trillion) in the first half of this year, according to the People’s Bank of China’s annual yuan internationalisation report released last month.

By the end of June, CIPS had 210 direct participants and 1,619 indirect participants, with services reaching 192 countries and regions, the report said.

Analysts said Beijing’s main objective was to make China’s financial system more resilient and reduce its vulnerability to potential sanctions or other restrictions.

“I would see this as building a safety net, consolidating global financial influence and even laying the groundwork for the future – but not as an effort to replace the US dollar, which is not something that could be achieved in the foreseeable future,” said Chang of Fudan University.

He added that meaningfully challenging the US dollar would require China to fully open its capital account – the rules governing how freely money can move in and out of the country. Any such move would require Beijing to surrender greater control over the exchange rate and could undermine its export-driven economic model, he said.

Data also underscores how far the yuan remains behind the US dollar as a reserve currency. The yuan accounted for 2.11 per cent of global central bank reserves in the second quarter of 2026, compared with 56.7 per cent for the US dollar, according to the International Monetary Fund.

The purpose of projects such as mBridge, Chang argued, should be understood primarily in terms of making cross-border trade and payments easier in regions that had often been underserved by the US financial system.

“Once you establish payment infrastructure, you can build trust and facilitate capital flows, which also opens the door to broader financial services,” Chang said.

Project mBridge has brought together the central banks of mainland China, Hong Kong, Thailand and the United Arab Emirates, as well as Macau, which joined in June. Saudi Arabia joined the project in 2024 but ceased taking part in May of last year.

Even if replacing the US dollar is not Beijing’s intention, analysts cautioned that a larger global role for the yuan could still be viewed in Washington as a challenge to the dollar-based financial system.

“Parts of Washington will treat even a regional yuan system as a strategic threat, because payments infrastructure is dual-use: trade convenience plus sanctions resilience,” said Alicia Garcia-Herrero, chief economist for the Asia-Pacific region and the Middle East at French investment bank Natixis.

These concerns could lead to greater scrutiny of CIPS links and correspondent banks, she added.

Washington has also shown sensitivity to potential challenges to the US dollar’s dominant role, including Trump threatening to impose 100 per cent tariffs on Brics countries if they move to create a new currency or replace the dollar.

Brics, a grouping of major emerging economies, currently counts Brazil, Russia, India, China, Egypt, Ethiopia, Indonesia, Iran, South Africa and the United Arab Emirates among its members.

Garcia-Herrero forecast a future of “managed coexistence”, with the US dollar retaining its role as the global reserve and invoicing currency while the yuan expands across Asia, energy markets and China-centric trade. She also expected “occasional political flare-ups when either side talks [about] ‘weaponisation.’”

Giovannini, also an AsiaGlobal Fellow at the University of Hong Kong, expressed a similar view, saying concerns in Washington could become more pronounced if the yuan expands its role in trade and financial infrastructure across the Global South.

“This could also create a feedback loop, with US financial leverage encouraging China to build alternatives, and those alternatives in turn increasing US concerns about dollar primacy,” he said.
