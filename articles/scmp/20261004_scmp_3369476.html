The myth of brotherhood: Hong Kong’s triads evolve amid new criminal landscape

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 2416 words | 2026-10-04

Not long after he entered secondary school, Hong Kong youth Jason* was bullied by an older student with triad links. He was relieved when another senior stepped in to protect him. He helped Jason take revenge on the bully, then recruited him into a syndicate.

Without undergoing any initiation ritual, Jason paid his protector a red packet to become an informal triad member. Later on, he discovered that the two seniors belonged to the same gang but took orders from different bosses.

At first, Jason was just a street-corner kid, pushing people around at a housing estate. But as he drew closer to the triad’s core group, Jason began to realise that brotherhood was a myth and that business was the order of the day. In the underworld, everything was built on money.

‘Once I got closer to the boss, I started realising how it worked. He was using the triad to make money. I felt I could actually make a living off this, so I joined the inner circle to give it a shot,’ he said.

Soon Jason was taking job orders from his boss, which were sometimes conveyed through messaging apps. His tasks ranged from property damage to procuring bank accounts for laundering fraudulent funds. Through these assignments, his illusions about brotherhood evaporated.

He saw peers chicken out during vandalism runs and scramble for excuses when the boss ordered them to engage in street fights.

To protect himself, Jason avoided direct involvement in high-risk operations by outsourcing most of his tasks to his peers, taking a cut of several hundred to several thousand dollars each time they completed a job.

“I stayed away as much as I could to avoid getting arrested,” the 26-year-old said.

Shifting focus in illicit activities

The triad Jason joined 14 years ago was Shui Fong, one of four major syndicates still operating in Hong Kong. The others are Wo Shing Wo, Sun Yee On and 14K.

According to experts in the field – including police, a retired anti-triad investigator and a youth organisation – the four triad groups have evolved over the past decade, with rituals simplified and street violence transforming into white-collar crime and cybercrime. Triad groups also often see one another less as rivals than as partners in making money.

“The violent clashes and bloody scenes are gone. Instead, there are less conspicuous crimes, involving underground banking networks, cross-border money laundering and cybercrime. They do not require a physical presence and are much more low-profile,” said Superintendent Joe Au Yeung Tak of police’s organised crime and triad bureau.

According to police, triad-related crimes rose to 2,554 cases in 2022, up 35 per cent from the previous year. Au Yeung said the spike was driven primarily by a sixfold jump in fraud, with 591 such cases accounting for nearly a quarter of all triad crime that year.

Before the surge, the city’s triad-related crimes between 2016 and 2021 hovered at around 1,700 to 1,900 cases annually.

The number of triad-related cases dipped to 1,353 in 2019 amid a wave of social unrest in the city, with police diverting resources to manage widespread violent protests, according to Au Yeung.

As triads shifted into the digital sphere, law enforcement was adapting to keep pace. “As they transformed, we evolved,” he said.

Historically, police relied on high-profile sweeps and mass arrests to deter gangs. Now their strategy has shifted towards intelligence-led operations targeting deception and financial investigations to cut off the triads’ sources of income.

“Our focus has shifted to tracing the illicit funds, specifically the channels they use to launder the money, and figuring out how we can freeze those assets and confiscate them. Hitting them where it hurts financially is the only way to make them truly feel the pain,” Au Yeung said.

Police also cooperated with other jurisdictions and organisations such as banks and telecommunications service providers to combat triad-related deception.

This multipronged approach has yielded positive results. The number of triad-related deception cases decreased steadily to 323, 162 and 129 in 2023, 2024 and 2025, respectively. The total number of triad-related crimes was below 2,000 last year.

Besides deception, triads also engaged in money laundering, which often involved the use of cryptocurrency, Au Yeung said. In such cases, his team relied on the expertise of colleagues from the cybersecurity and technology crime bureau.

While low-level triad members still relied on traditional crimes such as vice, gambling, drugs, extortion, illicit cigarettes and illicit fuel for survival, other businesses – selling pirated video discs, controlling red minibus routes and managing nightclubs, for instance – had grown obsolete as society evolved.

Au Yeung said different triads and factions had started cooperating in recent years, creating a “win-win situation” for themselves.

“If the pie is big enough and transnational, it’s really not surprising at all for several triad factions to team up and work on it together,” he said.

In the past, each of the 18 districts in Hong Kong was dominated by a single triad faction, a situation Au Yeung described as “strictly demarcated territoriality”.

However, over the past decade or so, territorial boundaries have become increasingly blurred.

“When everyone shares a common interest, focusing on making money rather than fighting, even different factions can collaborate. Nowadays, having multiple factions coexisting peacefully within the same region is actually not uncommon,” he said.

Tougher laws lay bare the myth of brotherhood

Gregory Lau Tat-keung, a former superintendent who retired from the police force in 2013, is another close observer of the gangs and has written a book about them. He has testified more than 40 times against gangsters in court as a triad expert.

“A lot of people have been misled into thinking that the triads are all about loyalty and brotherhood,” he said.

Despite hailing from a supposedly long tradition of ritualistic oaths, Lau recalled that many of the gangsters he had arrested had lined up to be prosecution witnesses.

“Let me be a tainted witness. I’m ratting out my boss. Otherwise, how can I get my sentence reduced?” he said, describing the detainees’ thinking.

“We shouldn’t believe in loyalty. Actual substantial interests are what matter most, including [having] fewer sentences and getting more money for informants.”

The Prisons Ordinance was amended last year to tighten restrictions on inmate visits, including prohibiting them from receiving outside food. The changes were made after the enactment of the national security law in 2020.

Lau said he believed the amendment was effective in deterring minor offenders from taking the fall for syndicate bosses because they were subject to stricter prison rules while on remand.

“Before, it was relatively easy to convince them to break the law for the mastermind, because they mistakenly believed that as long as they could receive outside food [while on remand], their boss still controlled everything,” he said.

“It wasn’t until they were convicted in court that they realised they were in trouble. They suddenly realised they were subject to strict prison regulations, no outside food, no special perks and fewer visitation rights.”

The stricter rules also made it more difficult for visitors to bring inmates cigarettes, which served as a kind of “underground currency” in prisons, Lau said.

History of political resistance

According to Lau, the triads could be traced back to around 1644 with the formation of the Hung League, a Han underground organisation dedicated to overthrowing the Manchu-led Qing dynasty. Sun Yat-sen, leader of China’s 1911 revolution, was a member of the league.

Over time, some exploited the name of the Hung League, with several developing into secret society factions, known as “huidang”, that turned to illegal activities after the fall of the Qing.

Lau said the history of local triad societies could also be traced back to Cheung Po-tsai, a notorious pirate who was granted an amnesty after giving himself up to authorities in 1810. Some of his followers who refused to do the same turned to banditry, establishing what was considered the earliest prototype of triad societies in the city.

The four main triad groups still active in Hong Kong have different origins.

The 14K triad has historical roots tied to the Kuomintang. According to Lau, its original headquarters was located at 14 Baohua Road in Guangzhou, with the “K” in its name allegedly standing for Kuomintang.

Similarly, Sun Yee On was founded by Kuomintang general Heung Chin. Originating from Chaozhou, “Yee On” was the historical name of the region. The organisation was originally registered with the Hong Kong government as the Yee On Commercial and Industrial Guild. After this registration was revoked, the members formed a new group, prefixing the name with “Sun”, meaning new in Chinese.

Many triad groups in Hong Kong traditionally carried the suffix “Wo”, meaning harmony, including Wo Shing Wo. According to Lau, the naming originated from Chung Wo Tong, a secret society formed by Yau Lit, a close ally of Sun Yat-sen, to support the revolution.

Another prominent group with the Wo suffix was Wo On Lok, commonly known as Shui Fong. It was originally established by workers of the Connaught Aerated Water Co Ltd, with “On Lok” being a Chinese transliteration of the company’s name and “Shui Fong”, meaning “water factory”, used as a shorthand for the aerated water plant.

Traditional rituals succumb to modernity

Lau’s book, From Hung League and Huidang to Modern Triads, published earlier this year, offers a rare glimpse into the triads’ traditional initiation ceremonies.

The rituals – gruelling affairs that could last for hours – centred around a sacred altar draped with command banners and ancestral tablets honouring fallen members.

New recruits were required to recite secret verses before lighting joss sticks and being covered with a sandpaper quilt. They shredded paper lotuses and chopped a chicken’s head to seal a blood oath.

They had to pledge allegiance by reciting 36 oaths and swear to abide by 21 regulations, 10 prohibitions and 10 punishments. Finally, new recruits would present their “protectors” with a red packet containing HK$3.60, a symbolic figure invoking the phrase “three times three never ends, six times six is infinite”.

According to Lau, the knowledge of traditional initiation rites has waned over time, with triads either forgoing rituals to avoid arrest or struggling to maintain them in the face of modern limitations.

“Take chopping off a chicken’s head [as an example]. Where do you even get a chicken’s head nowadays? Ever since the avian flu outbreaks, there are no chicken heads any more. So it turned into chopping an egg,” Lau said.

“And taking a blood oath? What about cross-contamination? We might as well just drink red wine.”

He added that he had seen triad members using a red plastic hosepipe or a red mop handle to symbolise the rank of “red pole” – a senior member responsible for organising fights and enforcing gang rules.

To avoid attention from law enforcement, some triads held ceremonies in the mountains, on barges or in mainland China.

More commonly, initiations were reduced to a mere verbal agreement, a practice known as “hanging the blue lantern”, Lau said.

More like rogue ‘employment agencies’

Michael Leung Siu-kei, a supervisor at the Hong Kong Federation of Youth Groups’ youth crime prevention centre, said triads in the past would recruit youths loitering in public spaces such as football fields, but that practice had faded into history.

Triads nowadays were more like “employment agencies”, with syndicates using Telegram, Signal or even in-game chat rooms to advertise various “well-paying part-time jobs” to lure teenagers to work for them, he said.

“They list how much [they pay] for splashing red paint, how much for distributing illicit cigarettes, how much for criminal damage.”

He added that syndicates paid around HK$500 (US$64) for young people to distribute illicit cigarette leaflets at an entire housing estate overnight and HK$6,000 for a group to commit criminal damage against an office. They offered HK$1,000 to anyone willing to lend a physical bank card and HK$2,000 for an online bank account to launder money.

Once a person accepted a job, it effectively meant they had pledged loyalty to a specific triad boss, although these allegiances were often loose, Leung said.

“In fact, when we ask some youths about their gang ties, they’ll say: ‘I’ve never even met my boss.’ Sometimes they claim to belong to one triad faction, but after a while, we find them working on another job under a different faction.”

Leung said he had to constantly warn youths of the risks and serious consequences of accepting these jobs.

Au Yeung agreed that triads’ tactics for recruiting young people were no longer as blatant as in the past, as police deployed undercover officers to catch recruiters.

“The triads use a much softer entry point these days. ‘Want to make some quick cash?’ ‘Come out and meet some new friends.’ ‘There’s free beer’,” he said.

Police also worked closely with schools to prevent triads from infiltrating campuses and conducted cyber patrols to combat online recruitment, Au Yeung added.

For Jason, earning quick money came at a cost as he became wracked with anxiety about being followed and developed a drug habit.

“You get paranoid. You worry people or enemies might have followed you home and know where you live. Whenever I slept, even the slightest noise woke me up,” he said.

Jason was once arrested by an undercover police officer after divulging that he was a triad member, but was acquitted by the court due to lack of evidence.

At the age of 15, he began taking Ice and got involved in drug trafficking. But as his addiction grew more severe over a year, he started consuming his own supply and ended up owing the triad HK$40,000 for the drugs.

Raised primarily by his grandparents, Jason was surprised when his estranged father stepped in to help clear his debt.

“It felt like he had not given up on me,” he said.

Around that time, the death of his beloved grandfather proved a final wake-up call for Jason, prompting him to quit drugs, cut ties with the triad and return to school.

He now runs his own small delivery team.

Looking back, he said he regretted joining the triad and could see more clearly how the organisation had exploited him.

“At the end of the day, the triads are driven purely by self-interest,” he said. “Your family is always your strongest backing. No matter what happens, they will always support you.”

*Name changed at the interviewee’s request.
