China and Oman unite in Mideast peace call on sultan’s ‘historic’ Beijing visit

SCMP | FREE | 1058 words | 2026-11-02

China and Oman have called for a peaceful resolution and full ceasefire between the US and Iran, stressing that safe passage through the Strait of Hormuz must be restored.

The call came during talks in Beijing on Monday between Chinese President Xi Jinping and Oman’s Sultan Haitham bin Tariq.

In their joint statement, Beijing and Muscat encouraged the United States and Iran to resume dialogue and reach a “comprehensive agreement that includes the nuclear issue”.

The two sides also affirmed that “normal and safe navigation” should be restored through the strait, agreeing to maintain communication on issues concerning the strategic waterway.

In the meeting, Xi said the Middle East and Gulf region should not become an “arena for major-power rivalry and geopolitical conflict”.

“China is ready to strengthen communication and coordination with Arab and Gulf countries to jointly strive for the early restoration of regional peace and tranquillity and the realisation of long-term stability,” he said.

He added that Beijing would continue to push for diplomatic resolutions to disputes, adding that the two countries should improve mutual political trust while expanding practical cooperation.

Haitham urged both sides to strengthen cooperation and coordination, describing the state visit as a “new starting point” for bilateral ties, according to Oman’s foreign ministry.

“We call for continued coordination with the People’s Republic of China in a manner that serves our shared interests and contributes to strengthening international security, stability and cooperation,” he said.

“We also express our great appreciation for China’s positions on key issues affecting our region.”

The Omani leader’s two-day trip, which began Sunday, is the first visit to China by the country’s monarch in their 48 years of diplomatic ties.

Xi greeted the Omani monarch with a formal ceremony featuring a 21-gun salute, a military march-past, and a welcoming banquet at the Great Hall of the People.

The trip comes as Oman – strategically located along the Strait of Hormuz – steps up efforts to move away from oil dependence and expand more into global supply chains.

Oman reportedly proposed to Iran in July that the two jointly manage the critical global oil chokepoint, with shipping firms paying voluntary fees, only to turn down a similar suggestion from Iran in September, according to the New York Post.

The sultan’s visit also came amid heightened tensions in the Red Sea, where Yemen’s Iran-backed Houthi rebels have repeatedly traded strikes with a Saudi Arabia-led coalition that supports Yemen’s internationally recognised government.

On Monday, the two sides urged all parties to prioritise the welfare of the Yemeni people.

Beijing also said that its special envoy on the Middle East would visit Iran, Oman, and Saudi Arabia this month to encourage peace talks and ease regional tensions.

Haitham was accompanied on his trip to China by a delegation of business heavyweights, including the Oman Investment Authority president, the minister of transport, communications and information technology, the minister of commerce, industry and investment promotion, and Oman’s central bank governor.

The summit talks also covered bilateral cooperation in technology, artificial intelligence, energy, logistics, digital commerce, food security, and integration between ports and special economic zones, according to Oman’s foreign ministry.

The two sides signed 14 memorandums of understanding spanning central bank digital currencies, the BeiDou navigation system, satellite technology, and supply chain investment.

Writing in state-run People’s Daily on Saturday, Chinese ambassador to Oman Lu Jian described the trip as “a historic milestone”.

He said the two sides would outline a blueprint for future bilateral cooperation, noting their new focus on renewable energy, advanced manufacturing, the digital economy, aerospace and artificial intelligence.

Omani ambassador to China Anwar bin Ahmed Muqaibil called the visit a pivotal moment, telling the Oman Daily Observer that it reflected a shared political will to elevate strategic ties.

He pointed to the convergence between the Oman Vision 2040 development plan and China’s Belt and Road Initiative, which he said provided a long-term strategic dimension for relations, according to the report on Friday.

The two nations in 2018 signed a memorandum of understanding on the belt and road plan, which aligns with Oman’s socioeconomic blueprint from 2021 to 2040 that aims to foster sustainable and private sector-led growth.

According to the report, Haitham’s visit could renew interest from China to invest in Omani infrastructure and industrial projects.

Commercial links between the two countries have expanded steadily. China has ranked as Oman’s largest trading partner since 2018, while Oman is China’s fourth-largest trading partner among Arab nations.

Omani sales to China are led by crude oil, liquefied natural gas and petrochemicals. In return, China exports machinery, electrical appliances, construction materials, batteries and solar panels.

The Oman-China Joint Committee – an intergovernmental body that oversees economic, trade and industrial ties – met in Beijing in late 2025 to discuss further Chinese investment in Oman’s non-oil industrial base.

Beyond commercial deals, Haitham’s trip is drawing close attention for its geopolitical subtext. Positioned on the southern flank of the Strait of Hormuz, across from Iran, Oman holds a critical vantage point over the world’s most sensitive maritime energy bottleneck.

Transit through the waterway has remained disrupted since the US and Israel launched strikes on Iran in February.

Addressing regional concerns, Muqaibil said the potential to strengthen joint coordination – with the aim of settling disputes by peaceful means – could expand into energy security, maritime corridor security and to other global challenges.

Omani Foreign Minister Sayyid Badr Albusaidi voiced a more explicit stance in a recent address at Cambridge University, arguing that regional security must ultimately rely on the Gulf states themselves forging a new framework.

Albusaidi said restoring free navigation through the Strait of Hormuz would be difficult to achieve unless Iran and the United States were both willing to compromise and reach a pragmatic understanding.

He criticised traditional Gulf security arrangements that relied on external military powers while treating Iran as an inherent adversary, saying they created an unstable architecture built on the “entirely false perception that there is a fundamental regional antagonism between Iran and the Arab states of the Gulf”.

“It is us, in the Gulf, who ultimately bear the responsibility for devising, developing and implementing a new regional security structure,” Albusaidi said, calling for an inclusive framework encompassing all eight Gulf littoral states.
