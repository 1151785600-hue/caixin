Wing Shya: photographer to Wong Kar-wai on working with the great and good of Hong Kong cinema

SCMP | FREE DEEP | 1627 words | 2026-09-23

I WAS BORN in Hong Kong in 1964. My parents came from Shandong province, in mainland China, arriving illegally, later legalised – a common story for that generation. I went to the all-boys Salesian School, in Chai Wan, and was not a good student. I spent all my time messing around with friends. Then, at 17 or 18, I watched a film called Boat People (1982) and there was a character holding a camera who looked cool. Something happened in me. “I want to be that,” I thought. Not the actor – the photographer. I saved every dollar I made from part-time jobs until I could buy a camera, then I started to photograph everything: friends, family, the street, skateboarders. I didn’t really have a plan. I just liked looking through that little window.

MY ENGLISH WAS so bad and I didn’t do well in my exams, so university in Hong Kong wasn’t possible. Art school was the only door open to me and, I’ll admit, a good excuse not to get a real job yet. I went to a technical college, the Lee Wai Lee Technical Institute (in Kowloon Tong), before I found the Emily Carr College of Art and Design, in Vancouver, Canada – the only overseas school I could afford, where tuition for international students was the same as for local students.

I WORKED THREE or four jobs at a time, sometimes illegally, as I was on a student visa. I delivered food for a Chinese restaurant, waited tables and even cleaned houses that were a two-hour drive from the city. On weekends, I would sell my drawings in Stanley Park.

I DID A double major: graphic design and fine art. In the summer, I’d drive to San Francisco, or fly to New York on the cheapest ticket I could find. I could earn a few dollars drawing people in Washington Square Park, in Greenwich Village. I had so much energy then.

AFTER GRADUATING IN 1991, six classmates and I opened a graphic-design company in Vancouver. Eight months in, we were doing well. One afternoon at a restaurant in Chinatown, I picked up a Hong Kong gossip magazine and looked at the layout. I said to my friends, “I have to go back – this design is terrible. I want to do something in the city.” They tried to talk me out of it. But within a week, I’d sold my car and bought a ticket home, without telling my mother. I flew back and rang the doorbell. She opened it and said, “Why are you here? You’re not welcome. We have no space.” I slept on the sofa.

WORK WAS HARD to find, but in 1992, I won a biennial photography prize and gave the money to my mother – it’s the right order of things. In 1993, I got a job at J. Walter Thompson (JWT), the advertising agency, but only lasted about a year; I had too much ego and was always fighting clients. I ran into an old school friend, Jan Lamb Hoi-fung – a singer who DJ’d at Commercial Radio Hong Kong – on the street by chance and he got me hired as the creative director there. It was a tiny department, just two of us, responsible for everything – stage design, ads, CD covers, concerts. I worked hard and I loved it. I got to know the biggest Cantopop stars: Jacky Cheung Hok-yau, Andy Lau Tak-wah and Faye Wong – she was there all the time.

THROUGH A FRIEND, Eric Kot Man-fai, my portfolio landed in front of Wong Kar-wai, in 1996. He said I could shoot some promotional pictures. On a Japanese commercial he directed, I hid behind a sofa so the crew wouldn’t throw me out of the tiny set – and when the official photographer could not get enough pictures, my photos were the only ones that worked. Because of that, Wong Kar-wai sent me to Argentina a year later, for Happy Together, starring Tony Leung Chiu-wai and Leslie Cheung Kwok-wing.

I HAD NEVER done film stills professionally. I didn’t even own a sound blimp – the soundproof box for your camera so you can’t hear the shutter going off. Because of this, I mostly shot between takes. I captured the actors sitting, waiting, hanging out: Tony and Leslie just breathing, not performing. One afternoon, on a rooftop, the street noise was so loud that my shutter was no longer an issue and I could finally shoot freely. Most of my images that you know from that film came from that one afternoon.

THEN, THE COLOUR. I was shooting slide film. Wong Kar-wai demanded to see the images immediately, but there were no labs where we were in Argentina that could process slide film. We found a shop that only did negative film. Because slide film is positive film, they ran mine through the wrong chemicals, and that turned everything green. Argentina’s grey walls became luminous. In the end, Wong Kar-wai loved it. It became the visual language of the whole film. I can call it a mistake, or I can call it, “I had no choice. It ended up like this.” After that, I left the radio station and started my company, Shya-la-la Workshop.

ONE DAY, WE were shooting in a train station – it was too noisy for sound recording – so I could shoot freely. Leslie was annoyed at me for distracting him. My shutter was too loud, but I kept going. I explained to him, “I’m only scared of Wong Kar-wai, not you. Because he hired me, only he can fire me.” He looked at me for a long moment, then said, “I like your attitude. When we get back to Hong Kong, you’ll work for me.”

HE MEANT IT. From that day until he died, I shot almost every photograph of Leslie for his work, his concerts, behind the scenes, albums and tours. He was the most considerate person I’ve known in this industry. He remembered everyone’s habits, every name, if you took milk in your coffee. He looked after the whole crew, from driver to delivery boy. We travelled together for concerts, shot in countries I’d never been to. I still think about him.

FOR In the Mood for Love (2000), Wong Kar-wai gave me one instruction: the film is about red. He gave me some music to listen to, and that was it, initially. So, in 1999, I went to Tokyo, bought 40 or 50 different rolls of film, shot test after test and laid every print in front of him. He picked one: an old, obscure film – barely still in production, its colour deeply reddish. I called the manufacturer; they were closing the following month because nobody was buying their film any more. “How many rolls remained,” I asked. “Two hundred,” they said. I bought every single one.

THAT PHOTO FILM was made in the 1960s or 70s, and its dyes were slowly failing – that’s why my shots for In the Mood for Love look the way they do. After we finished the film, there was none left anywhere in the world.

THERE ARE PEOPLE who I learned almost everything from. One, of course, is Wong Kar-wai, who gave me access to everyone, all these famous faces. I learned how to crop a photograph by watching him on the monitor. But another one of my real teachers is William Chang Suk-ping – the famous production designer and art director. Even now, we work together regularly. Just the other day, I was on a shoot, with William beside me. I learned what makes a picture by watching what Wong Kar-wai and William chose to keep and what they threw away. I don’t think of my work as mine alone. It is a long conversation with two people I deeply respect.

IN 2010, FOX came to Hong Kong to make a Chinese film – my feature-film directorial debut, Hot Summer Days. Stars such as Jacky Cheung, Maggie Cheung Man-yuk, Nicholas Tse Ting-fung and Daniel Wu Yin-cho all appeared in it, for very little money, as a favour to me because we had 20 years of trust.

DIRECTING FELT LIKE another universe. You have to think about logistics, 80-people crews, egos, dangerous rigs, scenes and even the crew lunches! I’ve made more films since. I still shoot fashion campaigns, recently for Chanel and Bottega Veneta, and – fingers crossed – I am now writing a television drama.

MY FIRST BOOKS came about because cultural curator Shelly Verthime told me at a dinner many years ago that she wanted to make a book. One day, in 2015, she called from Hong Kong International Airport, so I put a hard drive with every photo I owned into a taxi and sent it to her, at the terminal. She spent a year and a half editing alone. An investor funded the printing and an exhibition in Shanghai, in 2017. My shows in Japan (in Tokyo and Kyoto), the books (Chaos [2020], Solace [2024], Is [2026] and more) – they happened all because someone pushed. I never start these things myself.

MY WORK HAS been in the Mori Art Museum, in Tokyo, and in the V&A, in London. I don’t think about it much. I’ve done it already, and the future is more exciting to me than the past. What I still love is being on set – the mess, the crew eating together, the gossip, the waiting. I think the universe already has a script written. My job is only to follow my heart and see what page it turns to next.
