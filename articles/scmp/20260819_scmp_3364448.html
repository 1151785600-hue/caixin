Philippines’ Marcos slammed over US$3b family estate tax, says ‘very busy’ with government

SCMP | FREE | 1086 words | 2026-08-19

Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos Jnr has put his anti-corruption drive at the centre of his presidency, but questions over his late father’s estate taxes and the family’s past ill-gotten wealth have prompted critics to question his commitment.

Last month, Marcos pledged in his address to Congress to continue fighting alleged irregularities in flood-control projects, stressing that the campaign would be a test of whether his administration could hold even powerful allies to account.

The Presidential Commission on Good Government (PCGG) told This Week in Asia on Tuesday that it had recovered 309.84 billion pesos (US$5.05 billion) as of June 30, describing it as ill-gotten wealth linked to the Marcos family.

The PCGG was created in 1986 as a special agency under the Office of the President and tasked by different administrations over the decades with recovering assets that it said were stolen by the late president Ferdinand Marcos Snr, his wife Imelda Marcos and their associates.

Asked by This Week in Asia on Friday whether he had paid more than 203 billion pesos (US$3.28 billion) in estate taxes on the estate his father left after the latter died in 1989, Marcos pushed back against any suggestion that the family assets should be treated as ill-gotten.

Speaking at a forum with the Foreign Correspondents Association of the Philippines (FOCAP), Marcos said: “To characterise them as ill-gotten wealth immediately, I have to respond and say, the reason why we won [those civil forfeiture cases] we were able to prove that these were not ill-gotten.”

The estate tax issue stems from assessments made after Marcos Snr’s death, which the Supreme Court later held had become final and enforceable, leaving the estate’s executors responsible for settling them.

“I have to admit that I have neglected some of my duties as co-executor because I’ve been very busy with all the things that a president is busy with,” Marcos said. Calling it “a family matter”, he said that “matters of state and governance are a thousand times more important than any family matter”.

He added: “And that is why I leave it, I’ve left it to the lawyers.”

Marcos promised to provide his lawyers’ contact numbers, but This Week in Asia has not received a response as of Tuesday.

The Bureau of Internal Revenue, which was also contacted for comment, sent an automated reply promising to respond “as soon as possible” but has not done so to date.

Jean Franco, a political science professor at the University of the Philippines, said Marcos’ replies “are consistent with his administration’s intention to revise history and change the narrative surrounding his father’s regime”.

“Talking about the [estate tax] issue is not a ‘family’ matter. It is something that is of great public interest. Not to talk about it is to depoliticise an important issue that has haunted the country for decades.”

She said Marcos’ anti-corruption drive “would be difficult to carry out” because “the flood control scandal has not been thoroughly resolved, aside of course from the Marcoses’ historical connection to corruption”.

The August 21 Movement (Atom), a pro-democracy group formed after the 1983 assassination of opposition leader Benigno “Ninoy” Aquino Jnr, has also accused Marcos of trying to distort history.

“Getting to Malacanang does not entitle him to distort Philippine history. Getting to Malacanang should not give anyone, for that matter, the audacity to violate the truth,” it said in a statement.

Atom’s vice-president Bien Gonzales told This Week in Asia that the group decided to speak out because it noticed the president’s “moves to change the narrative … [and] it’s the responsibility of Atom to become the vanguards of the true essence of history”.

Marcos’ long-pending estate tax case

Ruben Carranza, a former PCGG commissioner, said that as co-executor of his father’s estate, Marcos was duty-bound to pay the more than 203 billion pesos because the Supreme Court’s decision on the matter was final and binding in 1997.

Carranza called Marcos’ assertion that none of the wealth his father left behind was ill-gotten “a lie”.

“As you reminded him, the 2003 Philippine Supreme Court decision unequivocally said that any assets of Ferdinand and Imelda Marcos beyond the US$304,000 they earned as public officials is [in the Supreme Court’s words] ‘money stolen by the Marcos spouses from the Republic of the Philippines’.”

Carranza characterised Marcos’ remarks as an admission that he had failed to meet his tax obligations while in office.

Marcos’ explanation that he had not paid because “he has been busy” with matters of state as president was also “a lie” because the tax liability had existed for decades before he took office.

“If Marcos Jnr is aware of other civil cases he claimed they ‘won,’ then he can’t claim he doesn’t know that they lost that case in 2003 and that they then lost a cascade of cases based on that 2003 decision,” Carranza said.

Another unresolved case involves Arelma, an offshore entity tied to a US case to recover the Marcos assets, which the Philippine Supreme Court also awarded to the government.

US court records describe Arelma as a Panamanian entity linked to a New York Merrill Lynch account into which Marcos deposited about US$2 million in 1972.

Carranza said Marcos was wrong to suggest the issue was long dormant, saying “he lied about [Arelma] at the forum when he said he had not heard the word ‘Arelma’ in decades”.

In a separate interview with This Week in Asia, lawyer Robert Swift, who represents victims of human rights abuses during the late dictator’s rule, said that while the Arelma case remained pending, the current president’s lawyers were no longer involved in it.

The battle over the assets is now between about 10,000 human rights victims who are seeking to enforce a US$2 billion damage suit they won in 1995 against the Marcos estate before a federal court in Hawaii, and the Philippine government, which is also seeking to enforce the 2003 Philippine Supreme Court decision forfeiting the assets in favour of the state.

Swift said the victims’ motion against Manila’s claim remained pending before a federal court in New York.

In an August 2025 ruling, John M Walker Jnr, a judge of the US Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, described Marcos Snr as “a dictator and kleptocrat...[who] stole billions of dollars from the republic and its people and used networks of foreign financial accounts and shell corporations to hide stolen funds.”
