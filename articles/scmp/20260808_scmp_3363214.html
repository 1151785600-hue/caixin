Beyond call centres? Philippines reopens Manila for outsourcing edge

SCMP | FREE | 1145 words | 2026-08-08

The Philippines is reopening Metro Manila to new outsourcing hubs as it tries to defend its place in the global services market and win more complex work from multinational companies.

Officials, industry executives and property analysts say the move could help the country compete for higher-value offshore functions – from software engineering and finance to healthcare documentation, data work and AI-related services – by making more modern, incentive-eligible office space available in the capital.

But the decision has also revived a concern raised when new IT parks in the capital were restricted seven years ago: whether allowing more investment back into Manila could weaken the push to build outsourcing hubs in provincial cities such as Cebu, Davao and Clark.

On July 23, President Ferdinand Marcos Jnr issued Administrative Order No. 45 (AO 45) allowing the Philippine Economic Zone Authority (Peza) to accept and evaluate applications for new information technology parks and centres in the National Capital Region.

Peza oversees special economic zones where eligible companies can apply for incentives, making its accreditation an important factor for outsourcing firms and multinational service hubs choosing where to locate.

The order partially lifts a 2019 moratorium which aimed to steer more economic activity into the provinces.

Tereso Panga, Peza’s director general, told This Week in Asia the reopening of Manila would not divert provincial investment.

“Instead, a larger Metro Manila investment pipeline can serve as a gateway for second and succeeding expansions into Central Luzon, Calabarzon [an agglomeration of Cavite, Laguna, Batangas, Rizal and Quezon provinces on Luzon island] and other emerging growth centres”, he said.

According to Panga, Peza has observed that global companies commonly establish their first Philippine operations in locations with deep talent pools, mature infrastructure and established business ecosystems.

Those initial Metro Manila investments could later lead to expansion in other parts of the country, he said, citing the growth of information technology and business process management (IT-BPM) activity in Cebu, Iloilo and Davao.

The Philippines’ IT-BPM industry includes outsourced services for global companies ranging from customer support and back-office work to software engineering, data and AI-related services, healthcare documentation and financial analysis.

“The Peza board is directed to formulate and issue the guidelines necessary to effectively implement the policy and ensure that the thrust to accelerate countryside development continues,” Panga said.

Marcos’ order resolves a long-standing policy constraint, helping Peza respond to the changing requirements of the IT-BPM industry while opening opportunities for property developers, according to Panga.

The IT and Business Process Association of the Philippines, which represents the sector nationally, welcomed the order as a “timely and pragmatic policy” that would strengthen the country’s competitiveness for new investment.

“In today’s highly competitive investment environment, speed and flexibility matter,” said Jack Madrid, the association’s president and chief executive.

“Administrative Order No 45 gives investors greater confidence that the Philippines can respond to evolving business requirements while preserving our long-term commitment to countryside development.”

Belinda Torres, president of ICT-Davao, the regional IT-BPM association, said her association welcomed the changes. Global investors increasingly viewed the Philippines as a “portfolio of locations” to support different stages of growth, rather than limiting their choices between Metro Manila and provincial hubs, Torres said.

“Providing investors with greater flexibility in choosing where to locate their operations makes the Philippines an even more attractive destination for long-term investment,” Torres said.

She cited Davao’s strengths as a hub for digital investment, such as investor confidence in its stable business environment and lower exposure to major natural disasters.

“The future is no longer about asking which city will attract the next investment. It is about ensuring that every investment into the Philippines finds the right environment to grow. Davao is well-positioned to be one of those environments,” she said.

This Week in Asia has also reached out to the IT-BPM association in Cebu for comment.

Panga said Peza saw growing demand from markets including Australia and Japan for outsourced business services, digital operations, engineering and other knowledge-based activities beyond the country’s traditional IT-BPM base.

Five projects in Metro Manila have already received Peza board approval and are awaiting presidential proclamations that will formally designate their sites as special economic zones.

They are Altaire and The Yuchengco Centre in Makati City, One Trium Tower in Muntinlupa City, ARCA South 1 in Taguig City and Parqal in Paranaque City.

Sites designated as special economic zones allow eligible tenant companies to apply for Peza registration and tax incentives, though developers that build or operate the facilities will not receive fiscal benefits.

The authority said the five projects could be proclaimed within the year if they met all requirements, but did not disclose their planned office space, investment values, prospective tenants or projected employment.

This Week in Asia has asked Ayala Land, which is developing ARCA South 1, for comments.

Property consultancy Colliers said Marcos’ order could help resolve a widening supply mismatch in Metro Manila’s office market, noting that companies requiring at least 5,000 square metres faced a more limited range of suitable registered offices.

Peza said last month that 44 per cent of available accredited office space was considered ageing.

Kevin Jara, a director in Colliers Philippines’ Office Services division, said older Peza buildings “may face greater pressure to reprice, upgrade or reposition” as companies increasingly prioritised sustainability and operating efficiency.

He cited Quezon City, Mandaluyong and the Bay Area, which offered more Peza-compliant choices for global capability centres (GCC) and IT-BPM companies, as the most likely beneficiaries of AO 45.

A GCC is an overseas hub that handles functions such as technology, finance and customer support for a multinational company.

Quezon City has about 317,700 square metres of Peza-proclaimed office stock, according to Colliers’ second-quarter data. Mandaluyong has roughly 276,000 square metres of proclaimed and potential supply, while the Bay Area could add about 242,000 square metres of potential space.

Jara said those districts could provide scalable alternatives for companies constrained by limited Peza-accredited options in Fort Bonifacio and Makati’s central business district.

“AO 45’s largest impact will likely be felt in districts where the pipeline can translate more quickly into broader tenant choice and more competitive leasing alternatives,” he said.

Kath Taburada, research manager at Colliers Philippines, said the order could also upgrade the quality of Metro Manila’s office market.

Since the 2019 moratorium, the capital added about 4.3 million square metres of office supply, but only around 40 per cent was Peza-proclaimed, she said.

“AO 45 is not simply adding more space; it is helping unlock newer office stock that was previously unable to fully serve Peza-registered occupiers.”

The supply would serve both third-party outsourcing firms and GCCs, Taburada said, but the stronger strategic opportunity lay with the latter because they typically needed larger offices, stronger infrastructure, skilled talent and room for long-term growth.
