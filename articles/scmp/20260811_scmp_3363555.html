China, India and Pakistan’s dangerous nuclear triangle just became more complicated

SCMP | FREE | 1074 words | 2026-08-11

The China-India-Pakistan triangle has never been short on danger, but Islamabad’s new defensive alliance has added another variable to an already volatile situation.

Last week, Pakistan, Turkey and Saudi Arabia signed a pact in Mecca in which they agreed to treat an armed attack on one as an attack on all. The three governments have described it as defensive and not directed at any particular state.

Analysts said its main significance for India lay less in whether it would be invoked, but in how it could alter Pakistan’s strategic position as well as the balance between the three nuclear powers.

For several decades their relationship has been marked by intermittent deadly flare-ups, most recently the 2020 Galwan clashes between Indian and Chinese troops and last year’s India-Pakistan conflict.

“The agreement institutionalises a trilateral framework combining Saudi capital, Turkish defence technology and Pakistan’s strategic geography,” according to Brahma Chellaney, a New Delhi-based strategic analyst.

“Regardless of whether such provisions are ever invoked [the pact’s] existence could nonetheless complicate India’s strategic calculus by introducing additional uncertainty into crisis planning and deterrence,” he wrote in a social media post.

That uncertainty is important because India already faces two major security challenges from its long-running border disputes with both Pakistan to its west and China to the north.

It has fought four wars with Pakistan since 1947, in addition to last year’s conflict, and one full-scale war with China in 1962.

There have also been a series of deadly border confrontations with the latter along their Himalayan frontier, most recently the Galwan Valley clash in 2020 that killed 20 Indian and four Chinese troops.

Chellaney linked the Mecca agreement to the fallout from India’s military operation against Pakistan last year.

In the conflict, which started after an attack on tourists in Indian-held Kashmir that New Delhi blamed on its neighbour, Pakistan said it had downed a number of Indian warplanes.

New Delhi’s decision to end hostilities after just 3½ days, Chellaney argued, “helped revive Pakistan’s international image, particularly in the Islamic world”.

He said that perception had raised Islamabad’s strategic importance and helped pave the way for a mutual defence pact with Saudi Arabia last year and then its recent expansion to include Turkey.

With the new deal, Pakistan, China’s closest strategic partner, gains further diplomatic weight and possibly access to more diverse military equipment from Turkey, a Nato member.

Pakistan has gained a formal security framework with two states that possess deep financial resources, technology and diplomatic influence

Saira Bano, Thompson Rivers University

A relatively stronger Pakistan would also keep Indian attention divided between its western and northern fronts, an outcome that has long suited Chinese interests.

The agreement “does embolden Pakistan further”, according to Farwa Aamer from the Asia Society Policy Institute, a think tank in New York.

“Pakistan’s further embedded in the wider regional security architecture ... it’s of interest to Beijing to have Pakistan in a more secure and stable position.

“It serves both its economic investment interests as well as geopolitical interests in the region”, she said.

India now has “to deal with a higher political and security profile of Pakistan in the region where we have major interests”, said Kanwal Sibal, India’s former foreign secretary.

“[Pakistan] will feel more emboldened to pursue its combative diplomacy against India and could take risks on the terrorism front,” he added.

“Pakistan has gained a formal security framework with two states that possess deep financial resources, technology and diplomatic influence,” Saira Bano, from Thompson Rivers University in Canada, wrote in an analysis for the Lowy Institute think tank.

“This sits alongside Pakistan’s deeper strategic and defence relationship with China, transforming what India once viewed mainly as a bilateral rivalry into a wider network of alignments.”

The Mecca agreement does not create an automatic anti-India bloc or give China an unconstrained advantage, she added.

But Bano argued its most “consequential feature” was not the resemblance to Nato, whose members also treat an attack on one as an attack on all, but the way it “collapses the strategic distance between the Gulf, the eastern Mediterranean and South Asia”.

She added: “It is an experiment in regional self-reliance that joins three strategic theatres usually analysed separately.”

Saima Afzal, a researcher specialising in South Asian security, wrote in an analysis for US-based platform Counter Punch that Pakistan’s presence meant a defensive network centred on the Gulf “now reaches into an environment shaped by India-Pakistan rivalry, China’s regional role and nuclear deterrence”.

For India the risks are multilayered and its room for manoeuvre is constrained by the added uncertainty caused by the risk Pakistan’s new allies could join any conflict, the need to protect its Gulf interests and the prospect of closer alignment with Israel.

Its large diaspora in the Gulf is vital to both its economy, in the form of remittances, and energy security strategy.

Any future India-Pakistan crisis might risk deeper involvement from the Gulf states or threaten those interests.

Chellaney observed that all three members of the new pact sit along critical sea lanes, giving India “fresh reason to prioritise naval expansion”.

Bano said: “During a future India-Pakistan crisis, uncertainty over what constitutes an ‘attack’ on Pakistan could complicate India’s calculations even if Riyadh and Ankara have no intention of becoming involved.”

Aamer said India had already been widening its defence partnerships, including those with Israel, Indonesia and the United Arab Emirates, and “will also continue to engage Saudi Arabia for commercial opportunities”.

Countries were increasingly pursuing “wider diversification and hedging strategies” by building broader partnership networks as buffers against future economic and security risks, she added.

The key question is whether the Mecca pact changes the balance of power or simply changes the strategic calculation.

Husain Haqqani of the Hudson Institute described it as “more symbolic than substantive”.

He added: “A shared enemy is the first element of a mutual defence agreement and that is absent here.”

Pakistan and Saudi Arabia have also proved reluctant to get involved in each other’s conflicts, such as those involving Afghanistan, Iran or the Houthis in Yemen.

Bano said India’s priority should be to “preserve [its] influence in Riyadh” and strengthen its defensive partnerships, while Pakistan’s role in the new framework “cannot be separated entirely from Chinese weapons, technology and infrastructure”.
